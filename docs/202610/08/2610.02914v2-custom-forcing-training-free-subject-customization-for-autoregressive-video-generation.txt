ABSTRACT
Autoregressive video models can generate minute-long videos in real time, but
they produce generic subjects from text rather than specific subjects from user-
provided images.
Existing customization methods either require costly per-
subject optimization or use pretrained conditioning networks that jointly process
all video frames with bidirectional attention. Neither approach is designed for
causal streaming. We present Custom Forcing, a training-free method that stores
reference-based anchor frames in the persistent KV cache of a frozen autoregres-
sive video model. However, fixed anchors face two limitations: simple condition-
ing allows identity to drift, and the text prompt continues to favor a generic subject.
To address these problems, drift-adaptive value amplification (DVA) scales refer-
ence influence with the degree of identity drift, while anchor contrast guidance
(ACG) steers generation away from the generic class prior. Over two-minute roll-
outs, fixed anchors fall from 0.58 to 0.42 in DINO-I, while Custom Forcing keeps
it between 0.58 and 0.62 without reducing motion. Custom Forcing also achieves
higher subject similarity than bidirectional customization methods and better pre-
serves identity over 30s than causal image-to-video and reference-to-video mod-
els, while generating each frame 9.5–28.5× faster than these long-video baselines.

INTRODUCTION
Video generation models can now turn a text prompt or an input image into a high-quality video, and
they are increasingly used for world models, robotics, and content creation (Brooks et al., 2024; Wan
1
arXiv:2610.02914v2  [cs.CV]  5 Oct 2026


et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026b; Yang et al., 2026b; Pondaven et al., 2026). Bidirectional video genera-
tion models (Brooks et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025) denoise all frames jointly at every sampling step,
so every step processes the entire video and generation is slow. Autoregressive video models instead
generate frames or chunks in temporal order and condition each step on previous outputs (Chen
et al., 2024; Teng et al., 2025). With distillation or adversarial post-training, they need only a few
denoising steps per chunk and can stream video in real time (Yin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026;
Lin et al., 2026), and rolling contexts bound their memory as the video grows (Liu et al., 2026b;
Yang et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). These properties support real-time and long-
horizon applications such as interactive video generation and world models (Yang et al., 2025; Xu
et al., 2026b; Yang et al., 2026b; Pondaven et al., 2026). In such applications, users often want a spe-
cific subject, such as their own pet or toy, to appear throughout the video. Given only a text prompt,
however, a conventional autoregressive video model generates a generic instance of the category.
Image customization methods either optimize the model for each subject (Gal et al., 2022; Ruiz
et al., 2023; Kumari et al., 2023) or use pretrained subject encoders or adapters that generalize
to unseen subjects (Wei et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Video
customization follows the same two approaches (Ma et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025;
Jiang et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026), but optimizing a video model for each subject
is costly, and training with still images makes it difficult to preserve motion (Chefer et al., 2024;
Wu et al., 2025). These methods also denoise the frames of a bounded video jointly, which a causal
generator cannot do: its finalized frames cannot be revised, and the reference must remain available
throughout an open-ended rollout. A simpler alternative gives an image of the subject to an image-
to-video model as its first frame. As Sec. 4.2 shows, however, causal image-to-video models lose
the subject within 30 s, and a bidirectional one cannot generate long videos.

In streaming generation, once a chunk is finalized, appearance
errors propagate through subsequent chunks and cause pro-
gressive divergence from the external subject reference. We
call this identity drift. Existing long-video methods maintain
consistency with generated history using attention sinks (Liu
et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026b; Yi et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026;
Zhao et al., 2026b), persistent memory (Yang et al., 2026a;
Kim et al., 2026; Yesiltepe et al., 2026; Dalva & Yanardag,
2026), or longer contexts (Cui et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025;
Chen et al., 2026b; Mao et al., 2026). However, consistency
with generated history does not ensure fidelity to the user-
provided subject. We store the user’s reference images and a
scene-specific custom anchor as persistent anchor frames in
the KV cache, making them available to every generated chunk without fine-tuning or an additional
conditioning network. These anchors establish the subject initially but cannot prevent identity drift:
as shown by the red curve in Fig. 2, identity similarity steadily declines over a two-minute rollout.
Weak anchor influence loses subject-specific details, and strong influence suppresses motion. The
text prompt also biases generation toward a generic instance of the subject category.
We present Custom Forcing, which addresses these limitations by modifying only the backbone’s
self-attention. Drift-adaptive value amplification (DVA) scales reference influence with the mea-
sured drift of each finalized chunk. Anchor contrast guidance (ACG) contrasts attention outputs
computed with and without the anchor frames and extrapolates along their difference, counteracting
the generic class prior induced by the prompt.
Over two-minute rollouts, Custom Forcing maintains DINO-I between 0.58 and 0.62 (blue curve),
while fixed anchors decline from 0.58 to 0.42 (red curve, Fig. 2). The improvement grows over
time, reaching +0.163 DINO-I in the final window without reducing motion. Custom Forcing also
better preserves subject identity than causal image-to-video baselines over 30 s, with only 11–13%
overhead over fixed anchors.
Our contributions are as follows:
• We present Custom Forcing, a training-free method that customizes a frozen autoregressive
video model by keeping the reference images and a custom anchor in its KV cache as
anchor frames, without fine-tuning or an additional conditioning network.
2


• We introduce DVA and ACG, two self-attention operations that keep the subject over long
rollouts. DVA scales the reference influence with the measured drift of finalized chunks,
and ACG extrapolates away from the attention output computed without the anchors to
counter the generic class prior.
• We show that Custom Forcing keeps DINO-I between 0.58 and 0.62 over two-minute
videos, where fixed anchors fall to 0.42, with comparable motion. It exceeds bidirectional
customization methods and causal image-to-video models, and matches a 14B reference-
to-video model with a 1.3B backbone while generating each frame 9.5–28.5× faster.

2.2
SUBJECT CUSTOMIZATION IN AUTOREGRESSIVE DIFFUSION
We are given a small set of reference images {ci}i∈R of a particular subject and a prompt y that
describes a scene and an action but names the subject only by its category (e.g., “a dog trotting
down a forest trail”). The goal is to generate a video that follows y and in which the subject matches
the reference images throughout the rollout. The generator Gθ stays frozen, and nothing is optimized
for the subject.
Let s(t) denote the similarity between the subject in chunk xt and the reference images, which we
define concretely in Eq. (4). Identity drift (Sec. 1) is the decline of s(t) over the rollout. It differs
from temporal consistency, which compares xt with the preceding chunks x<t, because a video
can remain smooth and self-consistent while its subject slowly turns into a generic member of the
category. Any correction must therefore be made online, before the next chunk is generated.

CUSTOM FORCING: SUBJECT CUSTOMIZATION IN THE KV CACHE
3.1
OVERVIEW
Existing video customization methods introduce a subject through per-subject optimization or an
additional conditioning network (see Appendix A). A autoregressive video model may need neither,
because every chunk already reads the KV cache and part of the cache persists for the entire rollout.
We hypothesize that images of the subject written into this persistent part can serve as its reference
throughout generation if the model reads them with the right strength and in the right direction.
CUSTOM FORCING has three components (Fig. 3). Custom K/V construction (Sec. 3.2) writes the
reference images and one custom anchor of the subject into the cache before generation. Drift-
adaptive value amplification (DVA, Sec. 3.3) sets how strongly the reference images are read, and
anchor contrast guidance (ACG, Sec. 3.4) sets the direction away from the generic subject. DVA
rescales the values before attention is computed, and ACG modifies the attention output.
3.2
CUSTOM K/V CONSTRUCTION
Before the first chunk is generated, we encode N images of the subject with the VAE, one latent
frame per image, and pass them through Gθ as clean frames, which writes their keys and values into
the cache of every block. We call these frames the custom K/V and index them by A = {1, . . . , N}.
The custom K/V occupies the attention sink of Sec. 2.1 in place of the first generated frames. It
precedes all generated frames and is never evicted, so every chunk reads it through the ordinary
attention of Eq. (3), without a retrieval step or an additional conditioning branch.
Reference anchors specify the subject, and the custom anchor specifies the opening scene.
The custom K/V contains two kinds of frames. The reference anchors, at positions R ⊂A, are the
user’s reference images used as provided; they determine what the subject looks like. The remaining
positions A \ R hold the custom anchor, a single image of the subject placed in the scene described
4


by y. We generate it once with FLUX.2 [klein] (Black Forest Labs, 2025) from the reference images
and a short image prompt derived from y (Appendix B). Because the video begins from this image,
we replicate it in all N −|R| remaining positions and place it last, directly before the first chunk.
More copies of the custom anchor keep the opening scene stable but fix the subject to its pose,
whereas more reference anchors restore motion at the cost of scene stability (Appendix F.2).
The custom K/V alone customizes the video but cannot prevent drift.
Writing the custom
K/V into the cache and changing nothing else already produces a customized video, which we call
Custom K/V only. It cannot prevent identity drift, because it reads the anchors with a fixed strength
and does nothing against the bias of the prompt toward a generic subject. Sec. 3.3 and 3.4 address
these two limitations.

3.4
ANCHOR CONTRAST GUIDANCE
The prompt biases the subject toward a generic member of its category.
DVA determines how
strongly the anchor frames are read, but not what the subject is drifting toward. The prompt names
only the category of the subject, so the backbone tends to produce a typical member of that category,
which we call the class prior. The subject of Custom K/V only drifts toward such generic instances
over time (Fig. 6). Counteracting the class prior requires a direction in feature space, and a second
read of the same cache provides one.
Masking the anchors yields a negative branch from the same cache.

tokens and Jgen all other tokens a query can attend to, namely the retained generated frames and the

without the anchors:

CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose CUSTOM FORCING, a training-free method that customizes autoregressive
video generation with images of a user-provided subject through the KV cache. It needs neither per-
subject optimization nor an additional conditioning network, only five reference images and one
generated image of the subject in the scene, and it adds 11–13% to the generation time of fixed
anchors. The custom K/V, written into the cache once, establishes the identity from the first chunk.
DVA reads the reference images more strongly the further the subject drifts, and ACG extrapolates
away from the attention output computed without the anchor frames, steering generation away from
the generic subject favored by the prompt. Without modifying the backbone, CUSTOM FORCING
keeps the subject close to the reference images for two minutes without reducing motion, holding
DINO-I between 0.58 and 0.62 while fixed anchors fall to 0.42. Because it changes only what the
KV cache holds and how it is read, it also applies to Deep Forcing, another autoregressive model
with an attention sink (Appendix D.2). Appendix G discusses its limitations. A direction for future
work is to keep a user’s subject in interactive world models, which also generate video causally over
long horizons.

ETHICS STATEMENT
The user study in Appendix D.7 asked 30 participants to compare pairs of generated videos of
animals and objects. CUSTOM FORCING places a specific subject from a few images into generated
videos without training. Applied to images of real people without their consent, this ability could
produce misleading or harmful videos. Our experiments use only the animals and objects of the
public DreamBooth dataset (Ruiz et al., 2023), and deployments of CUSTOM FORCING should
obtain consent for the depicted subject and label generated videos as synthetic.

A
RELATED WORK
A.1
AUTOREGRESSIVE VIDEO GENERATION
Bidirectional video generation models (Brooks et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025) generate fixed-length
videos by denoising the sequence jointly. Autoregressive alternatives produce frames or chunks
causally, making it possible to stream outputs and extend generation beyond a fixed video length.
Diffusion Forcing introduced per-frame noise levels for causal sequence generation (Chen et al.,
2024); CausVid and Self Forcing distilled or post-trained causal few-step generators from pretrained
video diffusion models (Yin et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026); and MAGI-1 scaled chunk-wise au-
toregressive generation to long contexts (Teng et al., 2025). Later work improves distillation and
rollout training (Lin et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026a; Zhao et al., 2026a; Lu et al., 2026; Zhu et al.,
2026b; Guo et al., 2026), reduces attention and KV-cache cost (Zhang et al., 2026; Lv et al., 2026;
Xu et al., 2026a; Ji et al., 2026), or trains causal world models for interactive control (Xu et al.,
2026b; Yang et al., 2026b; Pondaven et al., 2026).
Long rollouts accumulate errors beyond the training horizon. Recent methods preserve selected
parts of the generated history through rolling attention sinks (Liu et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026b; Yi
et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026b), positional re-indexing, anchor tokens, and evolving
memory states (Yang et al., 2026a; Kim et al., 2026; Yesiltepe et al., 2026; Dalva & Yanardag, 2026),
longer context windows (Cui et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026b; Mao et al., 2026), or
explicit spatial memory (Huang et al., 2025a). Bagger and Pathwise Test-Time Correction address
rollout error through backward aggregation or test-time correction from an initial frame (Po et al.,
2026; Xiang et al., 2026), while IAMFlow tracks identities that have already appeared in an evolving
generated narrative (Liu et al., 2026a). These methods improve consistency with generated context.
Custom Forcing instead maintains fidelity to user-provided reference images that are external to the
rollout.
A.2
SUBJECT-DRIVEN VIDEO CUSTOMIZATION
Subject customization has been extensively studied for images. Textual Inversion, DreamBooth,
and Custom Diffusion optimize embeddings or model parameters for each subject (Gal et al., 2022;
Ruiz et al., 2023; Kumari et al., 2023), while ELITE, IP-Adapter, PhotoMaker, and InstantID learn
reusable encoders or adapters for zero-shot conditioning (Wei et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2023; Li et al.,
2024; Wang et al., 2024). These approaches establish the two main strategies used by later video
methods: subject-specific optimization and pretrained reference conditioning.
Video customization methods based
on subject-specific
optimization include
Magic-Me,
DreamVideo, and CustomCrafter (Ma et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). CustomVideo
instead combines attention control with subject masks for multi-subject customization (Wang et al.,
2026). Still-Moving transfers an image-customized model to video without requiring customized
video data (Chefer et al., 2024). A second group uses pretrained or reusable video-conditioning sys-
tems for zero-shot or feed-forward customization, including VideoBooth, ID-Animator, ConsisID,
16


VideoMaker, SUGAR, MagicMirror, and Stand-In (Jiang et al., 2024; He et al., 2024; Yuan et al.,
2025; Wu et al., 2024b; Zhou et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026). Recent systems
extend this setting to multiple subjects, broader control signals, or unified creation interfaces (Huang
et al., 2025b; Liang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Fei et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025;
Chen et al., 2025b). These methods primarily target bidirectional video generation and typically
denoise a bounded video jointly. They generally do not address open-ended causal rollouts, where
completed frames cannot be revised and reference information must remain available throughout
generation.
V-Warper is closely related because it injects reference appearance through value representations
without additional video training (Lee et al., 2025). Its coarse stage still uses image-domain subject
adaptation, and its fine stage operates during bidirectional video denoising. Custom Forcing instead
stores reference images in the persistent cache of a streaming autoregressive model and adapts their
contribution from the observed rollout.
A.3
INFERENCE-TIME ATTENTION CONTROL
Classifier-free guidance combines conditional and unconditional model predictions at inference
time (Ho & Salimans, 2022). Normalized Attention Guidance (NAG) instead extrapolates between
the attention outputs for a positive and a negative prompt and normalizes the result, which keeps
negative guidance stable in few-step sampling (Chen et al., 2026a). Prompt-to-Prompt and MasaCtrl
manipulate cross-attention or self-attention to control image editing and consistent synthesis (Hertz
et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2023). For consistent subjects without training, ConsiStory and StoryD-
iffusion share self-attention across the images of a batch (Tewel et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024b),
and RefDrop shows that such sharing interpolates self-attention and reference attention, replacing
the interpolation weight with a user-set constant (Fan et al., 2024). Custom Forcing builds on these
ideas for a causal stream. ACG adopts the normalization of NAG but derives its negative branch
by masking reference frames in the cache rather than from a negative prompt, and, unlike RefDrop,
it scales the attention share that each query already places on the references instead of mixing in a
constant amount, so queries that place little attention on the references remain nearly unchanged.
Unlike classifier-free guidance, neither branch of ACG requires an additional forward pass, only a
second attention read in each block. DVA further adjusts the reference values during sampling from
an online measurement of subject similarity.

SMRABooth customizes Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B with a spatial subject LoRA of rank 32 on the query,

size 2, so both see the same number of samples. SMRABooth samples 49 frames at 480 × 832 and
15 fps with 50 steps and the official negative prompt, and raises the LoRA scale from 0.6 to 0.7 at
step 25.
MotionBooth fine-tunes the full UNet of Zeroscope v2 576w with its official configuration: a masked
subject loss, a subject-token cross-attention loss with weight 0.01, and a video loss with weight 1. It
is trained for 300 steps at an effective batch size of 20 with AdamW (learning rate 5 × 10−6, weight
decay 0.01) and gradient clipping at 1 in bf16. The Panda-70M clips that the official recipe uses
for the video loss are no longer available, so we use 500 clips of 24 frames from UCF-101 instead.
MotionBooth samples 24 frames at 320×576 and 8 fps with a guidance scale of 7.5, without subject
motion or camera control. For three subjects, it uses its own class names, “toy car”, “toy robot”, and
“toy monster”.
Training-based autoregressive baseline.
Vanilla AR + LoRA fine-tunes Rolling Forcing itself.
Following DreamBooth (Ruiz et al., 2023), we add a new token <sks> to the text encoder and train
it together with LoRA adapters of rank 16 (α = 16) on the query, key, value, and output projections
of the self- and cross-attention layers. Each reference image is repeated into a static clip of 9 frames
(3 latent frames) and flipped horizontally with probability 0.5. The loss is the mean squared error
of the predicted clean latent at one of the five denoising steps of the distilled generator, sampled
uniformly and computed through the KV cache, plus a prior-preservation loss with weight 1 on
three clips from each class video of the vanilla AR model. We train for 1,000 steps with AdamW,
using a learning rate of 3 × 10−4 and weight decay 0.01 for the LoRA weights and a learning rate
18


of 10−2 without weight decay for the token embedding, with 20 warm-up steps, cosine decay, and
gradient clipping at 1. Training takes about 12 minutes per subject. At inference, the LoRA scale is
0.5.

and FramePack-F1 use their released checkpoints, which are built on HunyuanVideo, with 25 sam-

C
EVALUATION DETAILS
Identity and prompt alignment.
We use every eighth frame of a video. DINO-I averages the
DINOv2-B/14 (Oquab et al., 2024) features of these frames, normalizes the mean, and takes its
cosine similarity with each reference image, averaged over the five reference images. CLIP-I repeats
this with CLIP ViT-B/32 (Radford et al., 2021) image features, and CLIP-T compares the same
normalized mean with the CLIP text feature of the prompt. DINO-I over time applies the same
computation to three 10.5 s windows. Our short videos have 501 frames at 16 fps (31.3 s), which we
round to 30 s in the text, so these windows are their three thirds.
VBench.
Subject Consistency, Background Consistency, and Dynamic Degree come from the offi-
cial VBench implementation (Huang et al., 2024), run on each video at its native frame rate. Subject
and Background Consistency measure temporal stability and Dynamic Degree measures motion, so
together they show whether stability comes at the cost of motion.

D.7
USER STUDY AND VLM JUDGES
User study.
We select 20 of the 100 prompts, two per subject, and pair the 30 s video of CUSTOM
FORCING for each prompt with that of each of five baselines, Custom K/V only and the four image-
and reference-to-video models, which gives 100 pairs. Thirty participants took part. Each participant
rates 20 pairs, assigned by a Latin square so that every prompt appears once and every baseline four
times, which gives 600 comparisons and 120 per baseline. Each screen shows the reference images,
the prompt, and the two videos playing in sync, in random order and without method names, and
participants can pause, seek, and speed up the videos. For each pair, they answer two questions,
“Which video keeps the subject more similar to the reference photos, from start to end?” and
“Which video has better overall quality, considering visual clarity, artifacts, and how natural the
motion looks?” Fig. 10 shows one screen, on which participants compare the two videos and answer
both questions by choosing A or B.
VLM judge.
We ask Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025), Claude Sonnet 5, and
Claude Opus 5.5 the same two questions on the same 100 pairs. Each pair is given as one image
that contains the reference images, the prompt, and ten frames of each video at t = 3, 6, . . . , 30 s in
two rows, with CUSTOM FORCING in the top row for half of the pairs of each baseline. The model
answers Top or Bottom for each question.

Tab. 9 gives the percentage of judgments that prefer CUSTOM FORCING. For the sub-

F.4
CONSTANT AMPLIFICATION AND SUBJECT MASK
Tab. 13 replaces the measured drift b(t) with a constant and removes the subject mask. A constant
b ≡0.05 raises DINO-I by 0.016, but the subject grows until it fills the frame (Fig. 12). CLIP-T,
Subject Consistency, and Dynamic Degree all drop. Larger constants break the video. The adaptive
amplification avoids this because it stays small. Measured b(t) averages 0.022, which gives a target
amplification of about 1.3 against 3 for b ≡0.05, and exceeds 0.05 in only 14% of chunks. Re-
moving the subject mask lowers DINO-I, Subject Consistency, and Dynamic Degree. Background
tokens carry no identity, and amplifying them ties the video to the backgrounds of the reference
images.

G
LIMITATIONS
CUSTOM FORCING controls identity for the subject as a whole, not for its parts. The drift signal
s(t) compares the whole subject with the reference images, and DVA sets the amplification of every
subject token of the reference anchors from this single signal. A change confined to one small part,
such as a wheel of the RC car or a strap of the backpack, may therefore change s(t) little, and
CUSTOM FORCING cannot target that part on its own. The fidelity of CUSTOM FORCING is also
bounded by the frozen backbone. CUSTOM FORCING changes only how the backbone attends to the
anchors, so appearance that the backbone cannot render remains out of reach. Finally, we evaluate
one subject per video for up to two minutes. Multiple subjects and longer horizons remain for future
work.

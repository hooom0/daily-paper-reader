ABSTRACT
In diffusion transformers, a class label or a text prompt is embedded once, and the
same condition is reused at every denoising step. We ask whether predicted em-
beddings can serve as this condition instead. Next-Embedding Predictive Autore-
gression (NEPA) trains a Transformer to predict the next continuous embedding in
a sequence. In generation, the clean image follows the noisy image, so its embed-
dings are the next embeddings after the condition and the noisy image. We train a
NEPA model to predict them all at once with Multi-Embedding Prediction, and
in Embedding Conditioned Generation, a DiT generator is conditioned on these
predictions, recomputed at every denoising step, so the conditioning signal adapts
to the current noisy state. Experiments on class-conditional ImageNet 256 × 256
study the condition of the generator, the design of Multi-Embedding Prediction,
and the scaling of both models. The NEPA model adds a second network to every
sampling step; with it, and combined with REPA, our final model, NEPA-DiT-XL,
reaches an FID of 1.32 using about a third of the training compute of REPA.

Diffusion transformers have become the standard back-
bone for image generation (Peebles & Xie, 2023; Ma
et al., 2024; Esser et al., 2024). Across these models,
the condition enters through a fixed interface: a class la-
bel or a text prompt is embedded once, and the same
condition is reused at every denoising step. It is left
to the generator to work out what the condition implies
for the image at hand as its state moves from noise to a
clean sample. Recent work brings pretrained visual rep-
resentations into this pipeline, as alignment targets for
the generator’s features (Yu et al., 2025), as the latent
space in which diffusion operates (Zheng et al., 2026),
or as intermediate generation targets (Li et al., 2024a).
In all of these, the condition itself stays fixed while the
image evolves. We ask: can the condition itself be pre-
dicted from the current noisy image, at every denoising
step?
NEPA trains a Transformer to predict the next embed-
ding in a sequence, rather than to reconstruct pixels or
discrete tokens (Xu et al., 2025). Unlike a feature ex-
tractor, such a model is predictive by construction: its
outputs are estimates of embeddings it has not yet seen.
In generation, the sequence runs from the condition through the noisy image to the clean image, so
the clean image is what comes next, and its predicted embeddings are a natural candidate for what a
generator should condition on.
We answer this question with Embedding Conditioned Generation (ECG): a DiT generator is con-
ditioned on the embeddings predicted by a frozen NEPA model instead of on the raw condition, and

RELATED WORK
2.1
DIFFUSION MODELS AND REPRESENTATIONS FOR GENERATION
Denoising diffusion models have become a dominant framework for image generation (Song & Er-
mon, 2019; Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2021b; Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021; Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021),
supported by faster samplers (Song et al., 2021a; Lu et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023) and by flow-based
formulations (Lipman et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2026). Latent dif-
fusion models operate in the latent space of a pretrained autoencoder to reduce the computational
cost (Rombach et al., 2022; Esser et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2025b; Yao et al., 2025), while diffusion
transformers replace U-Net denoisers with more scalable architectures (Peebles & Xie, 2023; Bao
et al., 2023). Text-to-image models further improve these systems through stronger architectures,
flow-based objectives, and text conditioning (Nichol et al., 2022; Ramesh et al., 2022; Saharia et al.,
2022; Balaji et al., 2023; Podell et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024b;a; Esser et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024c;
Peng et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; 2026). Beyond synthesis, diffusion models have been adapted to
text-guided image manipulation and editing (Meng et al., 2022; Hertz et al., 2023; Mokady et al.,
2023; Brooks et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023; 2024), to spatially controlled generation (Zhang et al.,
2023), and to temporally consistent video generation (Ho et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2024; Xia et al.,
2024). Pretrained visual representations have been brought into this pipeline as generation targets (Li
et al., 2024a; Wu et al., 2025; Kouzelis et al., 2025b), alignment signals for diffusion features (Yu
et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Jiang et al., 2026; Tian et al., 2026; Leng et al., 2025), and the
generative latent space (Zheng et al., 2026; Tong et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2026). In these models,
the class or text condition is injected by adaLN (Peebles & Xie, 2023) or joint attention (Esser et al.,
2024) and stays fixed across denoising steps. Unified multimodal models condition a diffusion de-
coder on embeddings produced by a language model (Sun et al., 2024b; Ge et al., 2024; Pan et al.,
2025; Chen et al., 2025a), but these embeddings are likewise computed once before sampling. Self-
conditioning (Chen et al., 2023) feeds the estimate of the clean sample from the previous sampling
step back into the denoiser, and RIN (Jabri et al., 2023) carries latent states across steps; both reuse
the estimates of the denoiser itself. We condition the generator on the predictions of a separate NEPA
model, trained with its own embedding prediction objective and recomputed from the current noisy
input at every step.
2.2
PREDICTIVE REPRESENTATION LEARNING
Self-supervised visual learning has progressed from handcrafted pretext tasks (Doersch et al., 2015;
Noroozi & Favaro, 2016; Zhang et al., 2016; Gidaris et al., 2018) to contrastive and self-distillation
methods (van den Oord et al., 2019; He et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020; Grill et al., 2020; Caron et al.,
2020; 2021; Zhou et al., 2022) and to masked reconstruction approaches that predict pixels, tokens,
2

or visual features (Bao et al., 2022; He et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022; Baevski et al.,
2022). Generative image models instead learn by predicting discrete or continuous visual tokens,
either autoregressively or through masked and progressively refined prediction (van den Oord et al.,
2017; Esser et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2022; Chang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Yu
et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2024b). However, pixel and discrete-token
prediction can emphasize low-level details or tokenizer-specific structure. Predictive representation
learning avoids direct reconstruction by predicting targets in a learned embedding space: JEPA and
I-JEPA predict masked target representations from visible context, and V-JEPA extends this prin-
ciple to spatiotemporal prediction (LeCun, 2022; Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024; Assran
et al., 2025). NEPA further combines representation prediction with causal modeling by replacing
the next-token prediction of language models (Radford et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2020) with next-
embedding prediction (Xu et al., 2025). Our Multi-Embedding Prediction extends NEPA to predict
several embeddings at once, analogous to multi-token prediction in language modeling (Gloeckle
et al., 2024).
3
METHOD
Our method has two stages. First, we train a NEPA model with Multi-Embedding Prediction to
predict clean image embeddings from condition tokens and noisy image embeddings. Second, we
train a DiT generator with Embedding Conditioned Generation, where the frozen NEPA model
produces predictive embeddings at each denoising step and the DiT uses them as its condition.
3.1
PRELIMINARIES
Next-embedding predictive autoregression.
NEPA replaces next-token prediction with next-
embedding prediction (Xu et al., 2025). An input x is split into N non-overlapping patches by a
convolutional patch embedding layer f, giving a sequence of continuous embeddings z = f(x). An
autoregressive model hθ reads the sequence and predicts, at each position, the embedding that fol-
lows it, and is trained with a distance D in embedding space between the predictions p and the targets
ztarget:
p = hθ(z),
LNEPA = D(ztarget, p),
(1)
where D is any distance between embeddings. For images, NEPA orders the patches into a sequence
under causal attention, so the target is the next patch, ztarget = z2:N.
Latent diffusion and flow matching. Following latent diffusion models and DiT (Rombach et al.,
2022; Peebles & Xie, 2023), we perform image generation in the latent space of a pretrained autoen-
coder: a frozen encoder E maps each image to a clean latent x0, which serves as the input to both
models, and a frozen decoder maps generated latents back to images. During training, we sample
Gaussian noise ϵ ∼N(0, I) and a noise level t, then construct the noisy input xt = (1 −t)x0 + tϵ.
We train the diffusion transformer to predict the flow target vt = ϵ −x0 (Ma et al., 2024; Liu et al.,
2023). Given a condition c1, the generator Gϕ is optimized with

Generation produces an image through a sequence of states: from
the condition, through a noisy image, to the clean image. We write
this as a sequence of embeddings, the condition, then the N patch
embeddings of the noisy image, then the N patch embeddings
of the clean image. In this sequence, the clean embeddings are
the next N embeddings after the condition and the noisy image,
and we train a NEPA model to predict them with MEP, taking
K = N. The sequence is ordered by generation state rather than
by patch position, so the embedding that follows noisy patch i is
clean patch i. MEP predicts all N embeddings of the clean image
in one forward pass, each from the full context of the condition
and the noisy image. The model is defined by three choices: the
sequence and its readout, an attention mask with the condition as
a causal prefix, and an InfoNCE loss.
Given a clean input x0, we sample a noise level t and construct the noisy input xt = (1 −t)x0 + tϵ.
Both are patchified by the embedding layer into noisy and clean patch embeddings, zt = f(xt) and
z0 = f(x0). As in NEPA, the targets come from the model’s own patch embedding layer f, which is
learned jointly with the model. The targets are compared through InfoNCE rather than regressed: the
model only has to tell each patch apart from the other patches of the image, and the remaining detail is
left to the generator. The noise level is sampled uniformly, t ∼U(0, 1), so that a single model covers
every denoising step at inference, and the condition is replaced by a null token with probability 0.1,
so that the same model also provides the unconditional predictions used for classifier-free guidance
(Section 3.4). As shown in Figure 3, the NEPA model hθ reads the condition c, a begin-of-image
token ⟨boi⟩, and the noisy embeddings, and predicts the next N embeddings, the clean image:
p = hθ([c, ⟨boi⟩], zt),

3.4
EMBEDDING CONDITIONED GENERATION
The second stage trains the generator Gϕ with the NEPA model hθ frozen as its conditioning module.
The NEPA model predicts the clean embeddings, while the generator learns the denoising vector
field.
At each denoising step, the current noisy input xt is first fed into the NEPA model, which outputs
predictive embeddings pt = hθ([c, ⟨boi⟩], f(xt)). The generator follows DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023)
(details in Appendix C), except that it is conditioned on pt through the MM-DiT blocks of SD3 (Esser
et al., 2024): the predicted embeddings and the noisy latent tokens are processed with separate
weights and exchange information through joint attention, followed by single-stream blocks on the
concatenated sequence. The generator receives the condition only through pt and has no separate
class embedding; the timestep enters through adaLN modulation, as in DiT. The training loss is the
weighted flow-matching loss

4
EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate our framework on class-conditional ImageNet-1K generation at 256 × 256 resolution.
At small scale, Section 4.1 ablates the design of Multi-Embedding Prediction and Section 4.2 com-
pares conditions of the generator; Section 4.3 then scales up the NEPA model and the generator, and
Section 4.4 compares our final model with prior work.
Setup. Following ADM (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021), images are center-cropped to 256 × 256 and
encoded by the frozen SD-VAE (Rombach et al., 2022) into 32 × 32 × 4 latents. The NEPA model
follows Xu et al. (2025) and the generator follows DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023), each at B, L, and
XL scale; NEPA-DiT-B/L/XL denotes a NEPA model paired with a DiT-B/L/XL generator. Unless
stated otherwise, the NEPA model is NEPA-XL with patch size 4, and the generator uses patch size
2. The NEPA model is trained with Multi-Embedding Prediction for 240 epochs, then frozen while
the generator is trained with the flow-matching loss for 400K iterations (80 epochs), both at batch
size 256. Appendix C gives further details.
Evaluation. We report the Fréchet Inception Distance on 50K samples (FID-50K), computed with
the ADM evaluation suite and its ImageNet 256 × 256 reference batch (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021).
Unless stated otherwise, ablation and scaling experiments sample with a 64-step ODE solver and no
classifier-free guidance; the sampling setup of our final model is given in Section 4.4.

CONCLUSION
We have shown that predictive embeddings can serve as the condition of an image generator. In
Embedding Conditioned Generation, a DiT generator is conditioned at every denoising step on the
clean-image embeddings that a NEPA model, trained with Multi-Embedding Prediction, predicts
from the condition and the current noisy image. On ImageNet 256 × 256, combined with REPA,
NEPA-DiT-XL reaches an FID of 1.32 with the 250-step SDE sampler and 1.57 with the 96-step
ODE sampler, trained with about a third of REPA’s compute even though it trains and runs two
networks. Code and model weights will be released. Future work is discussed in Appendix B.

A
RELATION TO OTHER METHODS
Relation to RepFusion. RepFusion (Pan et al., 2026a) also conditions a diffusion transformer on
the outputs of a model that reads the noisy input at every denoising step. It builds on two existing
components: the latent space of a representation autoencoder (Zheng et al., 2026), which it adopts
so that noisy latents are compatible with pretrained language-model priors, and a pretrained multi-
modal LLM, which it repurposes as the encoder of noisy representations. Our NEPA model depends
on neither. It is trained from scratch with Multi-Embedding Prediction directly on the latents of a
standard VAE, so the conditioning interface is defined by a training objective rather than by an exist-
ing model. The same recipe can therefore be applied to any Transformer as the NEPA model and to
any generator that accepts conditioning tokens.
Relation to DDT. DDT (Wang et al., 2026b) also splits the generator into a condition encoder that
reads the noisy latent and a velocity decoder conditioned on the encoder output. The two are trained
jointly: the encoder is supervised indirectly through the velocity loss of the decoder, together with
a REPA alignment to DINOv2 features. Our NEPA model is instead trained separately to predict
the clean embeddings of the image and is then frozen, so the condition is a representation learned
with its own objective rather than an intermediate feature of the generator. DDT also shares the
encoder output between adjacent denoising steps to accelerate sampling, and Table 14 studies the
corresponding choice for our NEPA model.
Relation to x0-prediction and self-conditioning. A diffusion model can be parameterized to predict
the clean sample x0 (Ho et al., 2020; Karras et al., 2022), and JiT (Li & He, 2026) predicts it directly
with a Transformer on raw pixels. Self-conditioning (Chen et al., 2023) and RIN (Jabri et al., 2023)
feed such an estimate, or the latent state behind it, back into the same denoiser at the next step. In
both cases the estimate comes from the denoiser itself and is trained by its denoising loss. Our NEPA
model is a separate network, trained with its own embedding prediction objective and then frozen,
and its predictions are recomputed from the current noisy input at every step. Table 7 includes, in
the same setting, a network of the same architecture trained end to end with the generator by flow
matching, and the same network pretrained as a flow-matching model and then frozen.
B
FUTURE WORK
Text conditions. Text-to-image models either encode the prompt once with a separate text en-
coder (Saharia et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024b; Esser et al., 2024), which cannot adapt to the evolving
noisy image and often fixes the length of the condition, or process the prompt together with the image
in a single unified model (Deng et al., 2025), where the condition and the image share one backbone.
A NEPA model could instead read the whole prompt before the noisy image and predict the clean
image embeddings from both, as it does for class labels in this paper.
Unified models. Since the NEPA model is an autoregressive Transformer, a single NEPA model
could in principle serve both as a language model with an output head and as the conditioning model
of a DiT generator. Studying text conditions, other latent spaces, and higher resolutions are directions
for future work.
C
HYPERPARAMETER AND MORE IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
Further implementation details. Throughout the experiments, the generator matches DiT (Peebles
& Xie, 2023) in depth, width, and number of heads, but uses MM-DiT blocks (Esser et al., 2024):
the first blocks are two-stream and process the predicted embeddings and the latent tokens with
separate weights, and the remaining blocks are single-stream blocks on the concatenated sequence.
The NEPA model is kept frozen when training the generator. We use mixed precision (bfloat16)
with gradient clipping at 1.0 for both stages, and keep an EMA of the generator weights with decay
0.9999. The timestep of the generator is sampled from a logit-normal distribution with zero mean
and unit variance (Esser et al., 2024). We use the stabilityai/sd-vae-ft-mse VAE (Rombach
et al., 2022) for encoding images during training and the stabilityai/sd-vae-ft-ema VAE for
decoding samples. For the REPA projector, we use a three-layer MLP with SiLU activations and
hidden dimension 2,048, following Yu et al. (2025). We provide a detailed hyperparameter setup in
Tables 11 and 12.

Sampling. For the system-level comparison, we use the SDE Euler-Maruyama sampler of SiT (Ma

DiT baseline. The DiT-B baseline uses the same generator as NEPA-DiT-B, but conditions it directly
on a learned class embedding instead of the embeddings predicted by the NEPA model. The class
embedding enters the text stream of the MM-DiT blocks as a single token, in place of the predicted
embeddings; in both models, the timestep enters through adaLN modulation. All other settings,
including the training objective, timestep distribution, and optimization, are the same (Table 12).
Computing resources. All models are trained on NVIDIA H200 GPUs. The NEPA model is trained
once for 240 epochs and is then frozen and shared by all generators; each generator is trained for 400K
iterations at batch size 256 (80 epochs). Table 13 lists the parameters and the sampling FLOPs of
both models, counted with the PyTorch FLOP counter for one 256 × 256 image (one multiply-add
counts as two FLOPs); SiT-XL/2 is counted in the same way. Training FLOPs are counted with the

with REPA (400K iterations) together take 3.1 × 1020 FLOPs, and SiT-XL/2 with REPA takes about
1.0 × 1021 FLOPs for its 800 epochs. With classifier-free guidance, the generator and the NEPA
model both run on the conditional and the unconditional branch at each guided step.

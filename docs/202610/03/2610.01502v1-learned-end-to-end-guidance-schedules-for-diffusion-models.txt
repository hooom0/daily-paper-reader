Abstract
Diffusion models are a powerful generative paradigm used across multimedia and
scientific applications. Guided diffusion methods impose requirements on the
generation by adding the gradient of a differentiable loss (the guidance function)
as a drift term during inference. The weight of this drift (the guidance scale) is
critical for the trade-off between data quality and requirement satisfaction. To
achieve both of these goals, guided diffusion must resort to small guidance scales
and lengthy sampling, incurring high computational costs. This work proposes
learned end-to-end guidance schedules (LEEGS) to achieve these objectives with
fewer sampling steps. LEEGS trains a time-dependent schedule by minimizing
the guidance function over a small set of examples using stochastic gradient
descent. Backpropagating through guided sampling is computationally expensive,
so LEEGS uses an approximation of the gradient that cuts training time by a factor
of 4. We evaluate LEEGS on diverse guidance tasks, including (a) image inpainting,
(b) noisy image inverse problems, (c) face-ID-guided generation, and (d) forward
and inverse PDE problems, outperforming baselines at equal budget (50 or 100
NFEs), or matching constant guidance with only 10% of the steps.

Introduction
Diffusion models have been successful in modeling and generating data across many domains, from
multimedia tasks [e.g., image synthesis (Ho et al., 2020; Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021), image restoration
(Kawar et al., 2022; Chung et al., 2023), text-to-image generation (Rombach et al., 2022; Nichol
et al., 2022)] to scientific and engineering applications [e.g., molecular generation (Hoogeboom
et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022), protein generation (Watson et al., 2023), solutions to partial differential
equations (PDEs) (Huang et al., 2024a; Bastek et al., 2025), design optimization (Maze & Ahmed,
2023)]. Guided diffusion [e.g., (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Bansal et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023;
Corso et al., 2023)] allows these models to produce data satisfying ancillary requirements, such
as the physical viability of samples for scientific and engineering applications (Chen et al., 2024;
Christopher et al., 2024a), or solve inverse problems (Chung et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024a). While
several training-free approaches induce a requirement in diffusion models, such as particle-based
(Wu et al., 2023; Corso et al., 2023) or projection-based methods (Christopher et al., 2024b;a), if
its violation can be expressed as a differentiable loss h(x) (the guidance function), then modifying
the diffusion with a drift −∇wh of strength w > 0 (the guidance scale) yields a powerful, general
approach [see, e.g. (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Bansal et al., 2023)].
Yet, guidance exacerbates a well-known limitation of diffusion models: slow inference. All guidance
techniques face a trade-off between data quality (staying close to the model’s learned distribution)
and requirement satisfaction (minimizing the violation h). Small scales (w ≪1) do not sufficiently
reduce the violation h, yielding samples that violate the requirements; while large scales (w ≫1)
cause substantial changes to the original diffusion trajectory, yielding unrealistic samples. To
generate high-quality data satisfying requirements, guided generation algorithms such as (Chung

data quality and the satisfaction of requirements.
Different approaches have been proposed to tackle this problem. For instance, Yang et al. (2024)
rescales the guidance gradient relative to the sampling noise at each step, enabling larger step sizes
and thus shorter trajectories, but still requires choosing a guidance scale. Methods that alternate
between denoising and guidance optimization [e.g. (Zampini et al., 2025; Jayaram et al., 2025; Huang
et al., 2024b)] control the deviation explicitly via a trust region or a proximal penalty. However,
tuning the trust-region radius or penalty weight is as delicate as tuning the guidance scale itself. A
different strategy for mitigating this trade-off has been explored in the classifier-free guidance (CFG)
setting, where the guidance scale is made time-dependent (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2024; Wang et al.,
2024; Sadat et al., 2024; Malarz et al., 2025) following the observation that constant guidance is
suboptimal for CFG (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2024). Learning a time-dependent scale has likewise been
studied for CFG (Yehezkel et al., 2025; Galashov et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025), but not for loss-based
guidance, whose main advantage is that it conditions a pretrained model without retraining.
We bring learned time-dependent guidance scales (guidance schedules) to loss-guided diffusion,
achieving better trade-offs between data quality and requirement satisfaction. LEEGS trains a
schedule for a given model, task, and sampler by minimizing the value of the guidance function along
the diffusion trajectory, averaged over a small set of examples. This requires backpropagating through
the sampling trajectory, a naive implementation of which would incur untenable peak memory
and per-batch compute. We use gradient checkpointing to bound the memory cost, similarly to
when optimizing other diffusion parameters, such as sampling coefficients (Watson et al., 2022),
discretizations (Tong et al., 2025), and noise (Tang et al., 2024). We further propose an approximation
of the objective’s gradient that reduces per-batch compute. Put together, these improvements allow
LEEGS to effectively manage the guidance trade-off in a tractable manner.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We first review guided diffusion sampling in
Section 2, then present our problem formulation and method in Section 3. We introduce our main
empirical results in Section 4, starting with a description of the applications we consider, namely
various guided image generation tasks and PDE problems, followed by further experiments and
ablation studies. Finally, we conclude in Section 6.

The reverse diffusion process can be modified to produce samples that satisfy additional properties or
requirements using the mechanism of guidance. The guided generation process uses a non-negative
guidance function h : X × C →R+ that measures the violation of a desired property or requirement,
often parametrized by a condition c ∈C. The goal is to generate samples x0 that minimize h(x0, c)
[ideally, h(x0, c) = 0]. Consider the example of image inpainting where the goal is to restore an
image missing a block of pixels. Here, the condition c is the degraded image itself and the guidance
function is the normed difference between the generated sample and c at the observed pixel locations
(see Sec. 4.1 for more examples).

Learned end-to-end guidance schedules
Objective.
We seek a guidance schedule W that yields samples that both satisfy the requirement
encoded by h(x0, c) and stay close to the unguided distribution p(x0). We impose the requirements
on average over conditions c drawn from a fixed distribution pC, with the pre-trained model ϵθ held
fixed. The natural objective is to minimize the guidance function at the terminal sample alone, i.e.
Ec, x0∼pW (·|c) [h(x0, c)]; however, this terminal-only objective is hard to optimize directly, since the
gradient of h(x0, c) with respect to ws decays for steps far from the end of the trajectory (large s),
leaving long trajectories with little training signal (Appendix C.6). Including all the terms h(ˆx0|s, c)
(0 ≤s < S) along the trajectory (with the convention that ˆx0|0 = x0) restores a usable gradient at
every step, giving the objective we minimize :

This is roughly 4× cheaper to compute than the true gradient (Appendix C.2). We optimize W by
projected stochastic gradient descent, clipping negative entries to zero after each step; once trained,
W is applied at sampling time with no additional cost. We refer to the resulting method as LEEGS.
Naive backpropagation stores the activations of all S network evaluations along the trajectory, so
peak memory grows linearly in S and quickly exceeds GPU capacity. We avoid this with gradient
checkpointing (rematerialization) (Kumar et al., 2019; Tong et al., 2025): rather than store a step’s
activations, we recompute them during the backward pass, making peak memory independent of S in
exchange for extra computation.
Regularization.
Attempting to drive the guidance function h too low may occasionally push the
guided marginals pW (xs | c) too far from the unguided p(xs), yielding unrealistic samples, which
can be seen as a form of reward hacking (Tang et al., 2024; Zhai et al., 2025). This manifests in
Figure 2 as an increase in the LPIPS metric after a certain point in the optimization. This phenomenon
can be mitigated either by early stopping, when a held-out reward is available to detect the decline in
performance, or by penalizing the magnitude of the guidance schedule W in the training objective as
in, e.g. minimizing L(W) + γ ∥W∥2
2 with γ > 0. Appendix C.3 explores which tasks are prone to
reward hacking and how the penalty alleviates it. Typically, even on tasks which are prone to reward
hacking, it appears only at high S (e.g., S = 100), not at lower S (e.g., S = 25).
Parametrization.
LEEGS optimizes the schedule freely over W ∈RS

a smoother schedule, this requires the functional form to be fixed in advance. Since LEEGS’s cost is
dominated by the guided sampling, we find this trade-off unnecessary in practice.

Experiments
We evaluate LEEGS on guided image generation and PDE problems, comparing it with baseline
methods across budgets and analyzing its schedules, sample efficiency, and sensitivity to sampler
choice and inference steps.
4.1
Benchmark tasks
Image inpainting.
Inpainting seeks to recover a complete image from a partial observation. With
condition c = (y, M), where y is the observed image and M a binary mask marking the missing
pixels, we consider the guidance function h(x, c) = ∥M ⊙x −y∥2, where ⊙denotes elementwise
multiplication. Unlike projection-renoising methods, such as RePaint (Lugmayr et al., 2022), which
inject the observed pixels directly and can leave visible seams at low step counts, guided sampling
enforces the observed pixels softly. We generate synthetic inpainting problems on ImageNet and
CelebA-HQ using the five mask types from Lugmayr et al. (2022): wide and narrow (large or thin
contiguous regions are not observed), super-resolution 2× (one pixel kept per 2×2 block), alternating-
lines (every other row removed), and half (right half removed). We compare against RePaint at a
matched 200-NFE budget and include the original results using 2400 NFEs from Lugmayr et al.
(2022) as a reference. We report LPIPS (Zhang et al., 2018), the perceptual distance to the ground
truth image (lower is better).
Noisy inverse problems.
We use image restoration to illustrate an inverse problem (Kawar et al.,
2022; Chung et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023) with a known degradation operator A. The noisy
measurement of the image xGT under condition c = (y, A) is y = A(xGT) + σn, with an unknown
noise level σ > 0 and n ∼N(0, I). We choose the guidance function as h(x, c) = ∥A(x) −y∥2.
Following Chung et al. (2023), we synthesize problems from held-out FFHQ and ImageNet images
and evaluate five tasks at σ = 0.05: 4× super-resolution, box inpainting (a large centred square
removed), random inpainting (each pixel dropped independently with high probability), and Gaussian
and motion deblurring (details of the operators are in App. B). Unlike the noiseless inpainting
benchmark above, the observations here are corrupted, handicapping methods that inject observed
pixels directly, such as RePaint. We compare against recent methods for inverse problems (CDIM
(Jayaram et al., 2025), DSG (Yang et al., 2024), and DAPS (Zhang et al., 2025)) at 50 and 100 NFEs,
and include the 1000-NFE DPS (Chung et al., 2023) numbers reported in Chung et al. (2023) as a
reference. We also compare against constant guidance and heuristic increasing and decreasing linear
schedules, each with a single parameter tuned by grid search on a held-out set (App. B). Alongside
LPIPS, we report FID (Heusel et al., 2017), the distributional distance between generated samples
and a reference set (lower is better).
Face-ID guidance.
Following Yu et al. (2023); Yang et al. (2024), we generate images of a
target identity given a single reference photo xref (the condition c), using the guidance function
h(x, xref) = ∥F(x) −F(xref)∥2, where F is the feature embedding of a pretrained face-recognition
network [we use the ArcFace model (Deng et al., 2019)]. Such a network embeds faces so that images
of the same person lie close together, so minimizing h steers the generated image toward the reference
identity. We compare against constant guidance and DSG (Yang et al., 2024) on CelebA-HQ at 50
and 100 NFEs. We report FID and the guidance value itself, the FaceID score (identity distance to
the reference, lower is better).

4.2
Main results
For each task and step budget, LEEGS trains a guidance schedule on 30 examples drawn from a
training pool disjoint from the corresponding test set, using Adam with batch size 1 and training
separately for every subtask and NFE budget. This pool has 90 images for every task except FFHQ,
where we instead use a cross-validation-style split. All evaluations (except reference rows taken from
original works) used test sets of size: 950 for FFHQ (Table 1); 1000 for ImageNet noisy inverse
problems (Table 2); 100 for the inpainting and Face-ID tasks (Tables 3 and 4); and 1000 for Helmholtz
and Darcy, 924 for Poisson, for the PDE problems (Table 5). Exact training and dataset details (e.g.,
learning rates, epochs, pretrained models) are given in App. B. All results for LEEGS are the mean of
three independent runs (with randomly drawn training sets).
Noisy inverse problems.
Tables 1 and 2 report LPIPS and FID on FFHQ and ImageNet. On FFHQ,
LEEGS is the best or second-best method in nearly every column at both 50 and 100 NFE, with
the best LPIPS on four of the five subtasks. On ImageNet, LEEGS is best or second-best in every
column at both 50 and 100 NFE, and the best in 4 out of 5 subtasks for ImageNet at 100 NFE with
respect to both metrics. The tuned linear-increasing schedule is itself a strong heuristic here, while
linear-decreasing is consistently poor, illustrating that effective schedule shapes are task-dependent
(Fig. 3) and LEEGS recovers a good one without that prior. LEEGS at 100 NFE also beats the
1000-NFE DPS reference on both LPIPS and FID for most subtasks, a 10× reduction in steps.
Image inpainting.
At a matched 200-NFE budget, LEEGS improves over RePaint on every mask
type and both datasets (Table 3); RePaint with 12× the budget stays ahead on most masks.
Face-ID guidance.
Table 4 reports FaceID similarity and FID for guided face generation. At 50
NFE, all three rows are similar, except DSG’s FID which is significantly worse. However, at 100
NFE, LEEGS performs best on both metrics, improving over DSG and constant guidance.

PDE problems.
LEEGS attains the lowest relative error on 4 of the 6 forward/inverse problems
at 50 sampling steps, and on 5 of 6 at 100 steps (Table 5), in both cases at half the NFE of the
constant-guidance baseline (which runs a second-order sampler). However, DiffusionPDE’s results,
which use 20× more steps, retain an advantage. The linear-decreasing schedule remains a strong
fixed baseline here: it is in fact the best method for the inverse Darcy problem at both step budgets,
and for the Darcy forward problem at 50 steps, though the same heuristic is among the worst on the
noisy-inverse tasks, where linear-increasing dominates instead. Which fixed shape works is task- and
problem-specific and not knowable in advance; LEEGS matches or outperforms both heuristics on
most problems.
4.3
Analysis
Learned schedules.
Figure 3 shows example schedules learned by LEEGS. For a given problem
they have a similar shape across step budgets, but they differ substantially between problems:
increasing schedules help the noisy inverse problems, whereas decreasing schedules help the PDE
tasks, mirroring the performance of the linear-schedule baselines in Tables 1, 2, and 5. Schedules
differ even between closely related tasks: among the five FFHQ inverse problems, App. C.4 shows
that the schedule learned for a task is best on that task in nearly every case, motivating the need for a
learned, task-specific schedule.
Beyond DPS-style sampling.
LEEGS requires only a differentiable sampling trajectory and makes
no assumption on the form of the sampler. Beyond stochastic (DDPM; Tables 1, 2, 3) and deterministic
(Euler; Table 5) samplers, we apply it without modification to a sampler that alternates guided reverse
diffusion with renoising, a common ingredient of inverse-problem solvers such as RePaint (Lugmayr
et al., 2022), DDNM (Wang et al., 2023), and DAPS (Zhang et al., 2025). App. D shows that
this sampling scheme when combined with LEEGS can perform competitively with the vanilla
version of LEEGS on the FFHQ inverse problems (with better results for box and random inpainting),
highlighting the flexibility of LEEGS. Moreover, the learned schedules for this scheme consist of a
sequence of hill-shaped segments (Fig. 17). Such a schedule would be hard to recover even with a
priori knowledge, while the cost of learning them with LEEGS is unchanged.
Effect of inference steps.
LEEGS generally improves with more inference steps, with diminishing
returns (Tables 1–5; App. C.5). As the budget grows, the mean guidance weight decreases mono-
tonically (Fig. 3): more steps need less aggressive per-step guidance. Since the schedule’s shape is
largely shared across budgets and its magnitude follows a predictable trend, a schedule trained at
small S can be interpolated and rescaled to larger S at inference time, offering a way to reduce the
training cost at large S (App. C.5).
Sample efficiency.
LEEGS is trained on only 30 examples per subtask. Both the learned schedule
and the resulting test performance are largely insensitive to the size of the training set, changing little
beyond 30 samples (App. C.1).

Related work
Guidance scheduling (for CFG).
CFG (Ho & Salimans, 2022) steers a conditional diffusion
model toward a condition y (e.g., a class label or text embedding) by combining the conditional and
unconditional noise predictions ϵθ(xs, y) and ϵθ(xs, ∅) (∅the null condition) with a guidance scale w.
As in our setting, w must be chosen, and can be varied over the course of generation. Several works
vary the CFG scale over the trajectory. It was shown by Kynkäänniemi et al. (2024) that a nontrivial
scale (w̸ = 1) is only needed within a limited interval, with the conditional noise prediction ϵθ(xs, y)
used alone elsewhere. Fixed schedules were compared and proposed by Wang et al. (2024); Sadat
et al. (2024); Malarz et al. (2025), and adaptive per-step scales have been derived from the two noise
predictions (Zhu et al., 2026; Pavasovic et al., 2025), from estimated log-likelihoods (Koulischer
et al., 2025), or by a stepwise greedy search (Papalampidi et al., 2025).
Methods that learn the guidance scale [e.g., (Yehezkel et al., 2025; Galashov et al., 2025; Sun
et al., 2025; Azangulov et al., 2026)] are closest to our work. Azangulov et al. (2026) is a primarily
theoretical work in which the guidance scale is learned as a function of the sample xs by optimizing an
optimal-control objective. Yehezkel et al. (2025); Galashov et al. (2025) learn guidance networks that
predict the CFG guidance scale as a function of the intermediate sample xs and time ts. The training
objectives of these methods do not use rewards and instead rely on labeled data. Sun et al. (2025)
uses an evolutionary search to learn a sparse guidance schedule supervised by a constant-guidance
reference process; this is expensive, requiring many 1000-step reference rollouts. To our knowledge,
LEEGS is the first to learn guidance schedules for loss-guided diffusion.
Constrained diffusion.
Constrained diffusion methods interpret the guidance function h as a
constraint function and seek to minimize it, as in our setting. These include projection (Christopher
et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2026; Christopher et al., 2024a), trust-sampling (Huang et al., 2024b),
proximal (Zampini et al., 2025; Jayaram et al., 2025), and consensus (Christopher et al., 2026; Blanke
et al., 2026) methods. Projection-based methods ensure constraint satisfaction, but closed-form
projections are typically only available for convex constraints, so several methods approximate them
with an inner optimization loop (Christopher et al., 2025). Moreover, projections take arbitrarily large
guidance steps, which can deviate substantially from the distribution induced by the diffusion model;
this is typically mitigated by renoising the projected sample, which lengthens the sampling process.
Proximal and trust-region sampling methods explicitly address the guidance trade-off by optimizing
the constraint only up to a threshold, or within a fixed distance of the denoised iterate. However, the
threshold or radius is itself a hyperparameter as delicate to set as the guidance scale.
Consensus-based methods take another approach, splitting inference into two trajectories (one
applying projections, one following the unguided reverse process) and annealing them to coincide
by the end of sampling. A guidance term implicitly pulls the trajectories together, and Blanke et al.
(2026) sets its scale by dual ascent. Consensus methods are closely related to guidance and could be
combined with LEEGS. Unlike the projection- and threshold-based methods above, though, LEEGS
neither projects nor renoises: it learns a single schedule offline and applies it during ordinary guided
sampling at no extra cost.
6
Conclusion
LEEGS is a simple, general method for learning time-dependent guidance schedules for loss-guided
diffusion. It trains a schedule on as few as 30 examples by backpropagating the guidance loss through
the sampler. Across image inpainting, noisy inverse problems, face-ID guidance, and PDE problems
(Section 4), it is competitive with strong task-specific baselines and matches the 1000-NFE DPS
reference using only 100 steps. Two findings stand out: a learned schedule beats constant guidance
at a matched budget, and the useful schedule shape is task-dependent, so it should be learned, not
fixed by heuristic. LEEGS is also robust to sampler choice, working with stochastic (DDPM) and
deterministic (Euler) samplers alike.
Limitations.
Training cost for LEEGS grows with the number of steps S (the objective spans the
full trajectory), making very large S costly. While our gradient approximation (Section 3) is efficient,
it may be less effective when the guidance field is highly sensitive to xs. Finally, while we learn a
task-specific schedule; adapting it to the condition or initial noise remains open.

are used to train LEEGS and the remaining 20 for validation, and the other 950 images form the test
set of Table 1. For ImageNet, we build our own training and test sets. The test set of Table 2 consists
of 1000 images sampled from the ILSVRC 2012 validation set, one per class, and a disjoint pool of
100 images is split into 90 images for training, from which each LEEGS run draws 30, and 10 for
validation.

Background.
We consider static boundary-value problems (BVPs)

(7)

∂Ω, g specifies the boundary condition, a(·) is a spatially varying coefficient, and u(·) is the solution.
The forward problem recovers u from a, and the inverse problem recovers a from u. Following
DiffusionPDE (Huang et al., 2024a), a pretrained diffusion model represents the joint distribution
over pairs (a, u) rendered as 128 × 128 images, and guidance enforces consistency with sparse
observations: for the forward problem, a is observed at 500 randomly chosen locations zobs (≈3%
of pixels), and analogously u for the inverse problem. Following Huang et al. (2024a), we consider

Poisson:

−∇· (a(z)∇u(z)) = 1,
each on a bounded domain Ωwith u(z) = 0 on ∂Ω.

C.1
Ablation on training dataset size
This section analyzes the variance in the learned guidance schedule due to the choice of training set.
We consider two tasks : the noisy motion deblur on FFHQ, and the Helmholtz forward PDE problem,
both at S = 10. We train the guidance schedule on datasets of varying size n, where each dataset
is drawn randomly from a larger pool of 500 samples. For each n we draw 10 datasets and train a
separate guidance schedule on each, holding the total number of training iterations fixed as n varies.
For both tasks, we sweep n ∈{1, 5, 30, 50, 100}.
We evaluate each learned schedule on a held-out test set of 300 samples, reporting the reconstruction
quality [LPIPS for FFHQ, relative L2 error with respect to u (the solution) for Helmholtz forward;
see Figure 6] and the per-draw distance of the learned schedule from the draw-averaged schedule
(see Figure 7). The latter metric directly measures the variability of the schedule, while the former
measures its effect on overall performance. We also plot the mean of the learned schedules across the
10 draws for different n in Figure 8.
We see that the variance with respect to both metrics decreases with increasing n. While the evaluation
metrics plateau at larger n for both tasks, the variability of the learned schedules continues to decrease
for the Helmholtz forward problem. This could be due to the use of a deterministic sampler for the
PDE problem, which reduces the variance of the learned schedule. In contrast, the FFHQ motion
deblur task uses a stochastic sampler, which introduces additional variability in the learned schedule.
Figure 8 shows that the mean schedule is relatively stable across n, especially for the FFHQ motion
deblur task. Note that this is not a measure of variance, but the trend in the average schedule learned
across the 10 draws for each n. This observation seems to imply that training with fewer samples has
more variance and less bias, with respect to the mean schedule learned with larger datasets.

C.3
Effect of regularization
This section studies the effect of weight decay on the end-to-end LEEGS guidance schedule, and its
interaction with the number of diffusion steps S. We consider two FFHQ inverse tasks that behave
differently under prolonged training: superresolution, where we observe an overfitting elbow in
which the terminal constraint keeps improving while held-out reconstruction quality worsens, and
box inpainting, where no such elbow appears. For each task we train the guidance schedule with the
Adam optimizer’s weight decay (wdecay) applied to the guidance-schedule parameters, sweeping
wdecay ∈{0, 10−5, 10−4, 5 × 10−4 10−3} across S ∈{10, 25, 50, 100} diffusion steps, for a total
of 20 configurations per task. We train using a fixed set of 30 samples for all runs. The learning rate
and number of training epochs are fixed per task and S. For superresolution, we use a learning rate of
0.3 and train for 30 epochs at S ∈{10, 25}, 25 epochs at S = 50, and 15 epochs at S = 100. For
the box inpainting task we train for 20 epochs with a learning rate of 0.3 for S = 10, 25 epochs with
a learning rate of 0.3 for S = 25, and 25 epochs with a learning rate of 0.2 for S ∈{50, 100}.
We evaluate each learned schedule on the held-out test set of 300 samples, reporting LPIPS between
the reconstruction and the ground-truth image. Figure 2 in the main text shows the corresponding
training LPIPS at S = 100: without weight decay, training exhibits a sharp reward-hacking elbow,
past which the terminal constraint keeps improving while the reconstruction degrades. Increasing
weight decay progressively flattens this elbow, and at γ = 5 × 10−4 it disappears entirely. The
resulting training LPIPS, however, never reaches the value attained just before the unregularized
elbow, so early stopping at that point is another fix for this failure mode.

C.4
Generalization across tasks
This section asks whether a guidance schedule learned for one task generalizes to other tasks, or
equivalently, whether task-specific training is beneficial. We study this on the five FFHQ noisy
inverse tasks in three parts: we compare the learned schedules, evaluate each schedule on every other
task, and repeat this evaluation after matching the schedule magnitudes to isolate the effect of shape.
All schedules are the LEEGS runs (first draw) used for Table 1 at S ∈{50, 100}; training details
are given in Appendix B (Table 7). Learned schedules for ImageNet and the PDE tasks are given in
Appendix G.
Learned schedules.
Figure 12 (left) shows that the learned schedules differ in magnitude: box
inpainting and the two deblur tasks cluster together, superresolution is larger, and random inpainting
has by far the largest guidance. To compare shapes independently of magnitude, Figure 12 (right)
divides each schedule by its mean. The shapes also differ, consistently across both S: gaussian
deblur rises roughly linearly over the second half of the trajectory, while superresolution and random
inpainting rise earliest and level off or drop towards the end, with a sharp drop in the final steps for
random inpainting. Superresolution appears to differ the most with respect to shape from the other
tasks, with a much more gradual slope as well as a hill shape.
Cross-task transfer.
To test how much these differences matter in practice, we sample with the
schedule learned for each task (row) under the measurement operator of every task (column), giving
a 5 × 5 matrix for each S. Each cell is evaluated on the same fixed subset of 300 images of the
test set of Table 1; the diagonal is re-evaluated on this subset and therefore differs slightly from the
Table 1. Figure 13 (left) shows that the matched schedule is best in 4 of the 5 columns at both S. The
exception is motion deblur, where the gaussian deblur schedule wins, but the two are nearly identical
(0.209 vs. 0.212 at S = 50, 0.177 vs. 0.179 at S = 100). This is expected, since both tasks blur the
image with a convolution kernel under the same noise model, and their learned schedules are close in
magnitude.

Extension to sampling with renoising
This section applies LEEGS to a sampler that alternates guided reverse diffusion with renoising, i.e.
forward diffusion back to a noisier state. Renoising is used by, e.g., RePaint (Lugmayr et al., 2022),
DDNM (Wang et al., 2023), which calls it time travel, and DAPS (Zhang et al., 2025). We denote
this variant LEEGS-renoise, and the standard DPS-style sampling used in the rest of the paper simply
LEEGS. Using normalized diffusion time τ ∈[0, 1] (0: noisiest, 1: clean), the sampler splits the
S guided steps into K outer steps of S/K inner steps each. Outer step j = 0, . . . , K −1 starts at
τ = j/K and reverse-diffuses with guidance to the clean end τ = 1 over S/K uniformly spaced
steps. The resulting sample is then renoised in without guidance, to the start τ = (j + 1)/K of the
next outer step, using the forward process. The sampler therefore moves back and forth between
the clean and noisy ends, with progressively later starting points after each outer step. Thus, each
outer step also covers a shorter interval with finer steps than the previous one. The final sample
is the output of the last outer step. The learned schedule has one weight per guided step, i.e. S
weights in total, and the NFE budget is the same as for LEEGS with S steps, since renoising requires
no network evaluation. For example, in Figure 17 (box inpainting), the five outer steps start at
τ = 0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and each has its own 10 weights. Training only changes how the trajectory
is unrolled: only COMPUTEGRADIENT of Algorithm 1 is replaced, by the version in Algorithm 2;
GUIDEDSTEP itself is unchanged.

Learned schedules.
Figure 17 shows the learned schedules. The magnitude varies somewhat
between outer steps. Every outer step except the last has a hill shape, rising and then turning down
towards its end. The last outer step instead increases roughly linearly, except for random inpainting,
where it drops sharply at the final step. Similar to the observation for LEEGS (Appendix C.4), the
learned schedules for superresolution and random inpainting are also similar for LEEGS-renoise.
For both, the peak guidance grows with each outer step, whereas for the other tasks it stays roughly
constant after the first outer step. Gaussian deblur is an outlier, with an anomalously large magnitude
in the first outer step (peak ≈85, vs. ≈37 in later outer steps).
Conclusion.
This experiment shows that the main idea of this paper (learning time dependent
guidance scales to minimize guidance functions by backpropagating through the sampling trajectory)
is applicable to diverse scenarios beyond DPS-style sampling. Moreover, while DPS-style sampling
may admit competitive simple schedules, such as the increasing linear schedule in Table 1, it is much
harder to tune or approximate a schedule by hand for a sampler with renoising, whose schedule is a
sequence of per-outer-step segments. For LEEGS, the cost is the same in both cases, since it depends
only on the number of NFEs in the sampling trajectory.

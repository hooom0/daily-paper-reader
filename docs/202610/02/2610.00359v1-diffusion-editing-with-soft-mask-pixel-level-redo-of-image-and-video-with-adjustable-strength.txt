Diffusion Editing with Soft Mask: Pixel Level Redo of
Image and Video with Adjustable Strength
Candi Zheng1,*,†
Yuan Lan2,*
Abstract
Diffusion models with prompt and reference image-guided editing have seen rapid
progress, yet they remain too coarse for pixel-level control. One promising direc-
tion is to incorporate a soft mask that specifies spatially varying edit strengths—but
training such fine-grained control demands expensive pixel-wise annotations, while
existing zero-shot methods often yield unsatisfactory results. We introduce Soft-
Paint, a new zero-shot sampling method that leverages soft masks to enable a
continuous spectrum of edits, from fully preserving the original content to com-
pletely re-synthesizing the masked region. Going beyond zero-shot inpainting
methods, we design a Langevin-iteration-based sampler that respects per-pixel soft
mask strengths, which applies universally to image and video diffusion models, en-
abling tasks such as video editing. The method is gradient-free, memory-efficient,
and achieves smooth, pixel-level edits across multiple image and video backbones.

have dramatically improved the efficiency of DDPMs, reducing the sampling steps from hundreds
to dozens. Recent unified generation-and-editing models, such as FLUX.1 Kontext [13] and Qwen-

Consider a digital artist or a casual user who wishes to edit an image or video. They often have
a reference x0 and want to modify specific parts while preserving others. While text prompts can
describe the desired change, they are often too coarse to precisely specify the exact region to be
modified. Binary masks, as used in standard inpainting, improve spatial control, but they still enforce
a hard decision between “preserve exactly” and “change completely.” In practice, however, user
intent is rarely binary; they often desire a continuous control of redo strength—for example, slightly
broadening an original smile, or turning the original sky into a dramatic sunset while preserving its
rough layout. A natural way to express such intent is through a soft mask, whose values specify how
strongly each spatial location should adhere to the reference image. Figure 1 shows how a single
generation with such a continuous-opacity mask yields a spatially graded edit. Furthermore, with
the rapid release of diverse open-source models (e.g., Stable Diffusion, FLUX, Qwen-Image) from
various organizations, users need a universal editing tool that works out-of-the-box for any model,
without waiting for model-specific fine-tuning or training specialized adapters.
This practical need for universal, fine-grained control leads to a fundamental technical challenge:
Since diffusion models are trained primarily for whole-image generation, their global denoising
mechanism does not naturally support such spatially varying, soft redo. The key question is:
Given a diffusion prior p(x), how to sample from a reference-guided distribution p(x | x0, r)?
Here x0 is a given reference image and r ∈[0, 1]d is a continuous soft mask: r = 0 means no
redo (reconstruct the reference), while r = 1 means full redo (ignore the reference and recover free
generation). Figure 2 illustrates how a single scalar r shifts this balance between preservation and
redo. This formulation represents a generalized inpainting problem, where binary inpainting is just a
special case with r ∈{0, 1}.
Current approaches to conditional sampling often face significant trade-offs. Training-based methods
[15–17] are effective for binary inpainting, but extending them to soft redo is much more difficult:
preparing supervision for continuous per-pixel redo strengths is expensive, typically requires enor-
mous teams of expert human labelers. On the other hand, training-free methods often either rely
on heavy gradient computations (backpropagating through the U-Net), leading to prohibitive GPU
memory usage for large production models, or suffer from inferior performance.
In this work, we propose SoftPaint, a pixel-level redo sampler that is model-agnostic and training-
free, based on Langevin Dynamics Monte Carlo. SoftPaint supports flow models, as well as ODE-
and SDE-based diffusion models. It introduces two core innovations: (1) the Unified Redo (UR) Score,
a unified guided score for the entire image that combines the diffusion prior score with an analytic
Gaussian tether to the reference; and (2) Diffusion-Damped Langevin (DDL), a diffusion-damped
Langevin solver that improves stability under large step sizes while keeping the sampling algorithm
simple. Experiments confirm that SoftPaint produces high-quality image and video inpainting, while
enabling fine-grained redo control for general editing. By avoiding gradient computations, SoftPaint
is memory-efficient and deployment-friendly, empowering users to perform soft redo on consumer
hardware across diverse diffusion architectures.

From Binary Mask to Soft Mask
Inpainting in diffusion models traditionally relies on binary masks to distinguish between known and
unknown regions. However, practical editing scenarios often require finer control over the degree of
preservation for each pixel. This motivates the transition from binary inpainting to soft inpainting,
where a continuous soft mask controls the redo strength. In this section, we formalize both settings
and highlight the non-trivial challenges that arise when extending binary masks to continuous ones.

The UR score g(x, t) and its underlying distribution π(xt) fulfill the four properties of Section 2.
First, π(xt) is a proper product of probability densities, ensuring a well-defined target. Second, the
boundary conditions are met: r = 0 gives Σ = 0, βt = 1, and γt = 1 + λ, which for λ = 0 enforces
exact reconstruction; r = 1 yields Σ →∞, βt →0, and γt →0, reducing g to the unconditional
model score. Third, intermediate r smoothly interpolates between these extremes because Σ(r)
continuously modulates βt and hence γt. Finally, g(x, t) requires only the pretrained score smodel
and an analytical reference term, making it readily samplable without retraining, as detailed in the
next section.

Experiments
We evaluate SoftPaint on two soft-mask editing benchmarks: soft image editing on FlowEdit [22]
(Qwen-Image [14]), and soft video editing on DAVIS-Edit [23] (CogVideoX-5B [24], Wan2.2 [25]).
SoftPaint natively supports per-pixel soft masks r∈[0, 1]H×W . We use uniform r within the dataset-
provided region for controlled edit-strength sweeps, then evaluate a spatially varying four-zone
mask separately in Table 1. A binary-mask comparison isolating the inner-loop operator appears in
Appendix C.4. Hyperparameters are ablated in Appendix C.
Baselines.
We compare against two zero-shot methods with continuous soft-mask support: (i)
Differential Diffusion (DD) [26], the most relevant training-free per-pixel method; and (ii) Naive
mixing (n=0), which disables SoftPaint’s inner Langevin loop, reducing it to per-step RePaint-style
blending [19]. Where applicable we also report Qwen-Image’s instruction-tuned editor (Qwen-Image-
Edit) as a single reference point.
Metric anchoring.
Soft-mask methods trade edit strength against reference preservation along
a continuous curve; the same r corresponds to different effective edit strengths for DD and Soft-
Paint (Fig.4 left panel). We therefore anchor all comparisons at matched CLIP-Text, a backbone-
independent proxy for edit strength, and report other metrics at those anchors.

Soft-Mask Image Editing
This experiment asks two questions: (i) Does SoftPaint provide finer controllability as well as smooth
edit strength? (ii) At matched controllability, does SoftPaint produce higher-quality edits? The
FlowEdit benchmark [22] is well-suited here because its diverse object-centric masks and paired
source/target prompts let us decouple “how much the edit was realized” (CLIP-Text) from “how clean
the result looks” (LPIPS, PSNR, CLIP-IQA, FID).
Setup.
All three soft-mask methods (SoftPaint, DD, and Naive Mixing) use the same Qwen-Image
backbone with 20 denoising steps on the FlowEdit benchmark [22]. SoftPaint uses n=2, s=0.2,
guidance scale λ=5, where n is the number of inner iterations per diffusion step and s is the inner-
loop step size. The soft-mask strength r ∈{0, 0.1, . . . , 1.0} is swept jointly across DD, Naive
Mixing, and SoftPaint. We also report Qwen-Image-Edit once using its default inference setting.
Metrics follow the standard FlowEdit protocol: LPIPSedit and PSNRedit for edit quality, PSNRkeep for
background preservation, CLIP-Text for edit alignment, CLIP-IQA for no-reference quality, and FID
for distributional realism.
Edit controllability.
Figure 4 (left panel) maps r to CLIP-Text for all three methods on Qwen-
Image. SoftPaint shows the smoothest behavior, with CLIP-Text growing nearly linearly as r
increases. DD is less sensitive at low mask strength, with CLIP-Text changing more slowly in the
weak-edit regime. Naive Mixing remains pinned near the source level (CLIP-T ≈29.0) until r>0.8,

The right three panels trace the quality trade-off: at matched CLIP-Text, SoftPaint achieves lower

finer controllability. Beyond training-free baselines, we also compare against Qwen-Image-Edit,
a modern unified instruction-based editing model. This Qwen-Image-Edit baseline (CLIP-T 30.2,
LPIPS 0.306) appears far from both soft-mask curves, indicating worse LPIPS and PSNR; that is,
although it achieves the edit goal, it deviates substantially from the given reference image. More
apples-to-apples comparison is in Appendix Table 4.
Equal-NFE comparison.
SoftPaint uses 20 outer steps and n=2 additional inner model evaluations
per step, for 60 network function evaluations (NFE) in total. To test whether extra compute explains
the Qwen-Image results, we also run DD for 60 outer steps. At the weak, medium, and strong
matched-CLIP-T anchors, SoftPaint has lower LPIPSedit and higher PSNRedit than DD at the same
60-NFE budget. Appendix Table 3 reports all five metrics alongside the original 20-NFE DD results.

To test nonuniform soft masks directly, we divide each FlowEdit

Related Works
Diffusion Models and Image/Video Editing. Diffusion models [4] have become a dominant
paradigm for high-quality image and video generation. Early work accelerated the stochastic DDPM
sampling process via deterministic ODE flows, notably DDIM [27] and advanced ODE solvers,
enabling fewer-step generation. Rectified flow [10, 11] further reparameterized the ODE-based
diffusion with improved numerical properties, later scaling to large text-to-image and text-to-video
models such as Stable Diffusion 3 [28] and FLUX [29].
Beyond generation, diffusion models have been widely adopted for image and video editing, where
an input image or video together with a text prompt specifies the desired edit. A large body of
work has tackled this problem from different angles: instruction-based editing via fine-tuning on
paired data [30], spatial conditioning through plug-in modules [15], image-prompt adaptation with
decoupled cross-attention [31], and multi-task frameworks unifying diverse editing operations [32].
More recently, large-scale proprietary models such as FLUX.2 Kontext [29] and Qwen-Image [14]
have integrated reference-image conditioning directly into their training pipelines, supporting multi-
reference and text-image-to-image editing with strong semantic preservation.
Despite these advances, existing image and video editing models operate at the semantic level and

Training-Free Conditional Editing with Diffusion Models. A line of training-free methods tackles
conditional editing by enforcing spatial constraints during the reverse diffusion process. Langevin-
based approaches [33] such as RePaint [19] and LanPaint [34] perform iterative denoise–renoise under
binary inpainting masks [35], while Sequential Monte Carlo methods [36, 37] and linear-inverse-

key limitations: (i) they are designed for binary masks and do not support continuous-opacity soft
masks, and (ii) many rely on gradient-based optimization or line search, incurring 2–4× higher GPU
memory than standard inference.
Differential Diffusion [26] is a notable exception that enables per-pixel edit-strength control, effec-
tively supporting soft masks in a training-free manner. However, its underlying editing capability
is limited: on binary masks it reduces to the naive replace method [19], which in turn constrains its
soft-mask performance. Our proposed method, SoftPaint, addresses both gaps: it natively supports
continuous soft masks with a memory-efficient, gradient-free sampler that is compatible with modern
ODE-based diffusion backbones.

6
Conclusion and Limitations
We introduced SoftPaint, a training-free and model-agnostic sampler for continuous soft-mask image
and video editing. By combining the Unified Redo score with Diffusion-Damped Langevin, SoftPaint
enables pixel-level redo control while remaining gradient-free and memory-efficient. Experiments
show improved edit controllability and a better quality-preservation trade-off.
A practical consideration for SoftPaint is its inference speed. As a training-free method that requires
no additional optimization or fine-tuning, SoftPaint can be directly applied to any pretrained diffusion
backbone with the same memory footprint as standard generation. The trade-off is that each inner
Langevin iteration introduces one extra network evaluation, making the overall generation roughly
2×–5× slower than the backbone’s standard sampling. This latency can be a practical limitation
for interactive editing and large-scale deployment. However,we believe the zero-training cost and
universal compatibility offer a reasonable trade-off; further speed improvements are left to future
work.
Broader Impacts
Our work can benefit creative workflows, content restoration, and research
on controllable generative modeling by enabling finer image and video edits. However, the same
capability could be misused to produce deceptive edits of real scenes or people.

Runtime
SoftPaint’s runtime is dominated by the inner-loop budget n, since each inner DDL update reuses
the backbone denoiser. On Qwen-Image (roughly 20B MMDiT) at 1024 × 1024 with 20 diffusion
steps on a single accelerator with approximately 65 TFLOPS of FP16 compute, measuring only
the CUDA-synchronized denoising loop (excluding model loading, encoder passes, VAE decoding,
and post-processing), DD averages 10.2 s/image while SoftPaint with n=2 averages 29.4 s/image.
SoftPaint uses the same peak GPU memory as standard forward-only backbone inference, i.e., it does
not introduce extra memory beyond ordinary inference.

C.4
Binary-mask inpainting: isolating the inner-loop operator
We also test whether the DDL update offers a benefit beyond repeated denoise–replace–renoise
updates under binary masks. On the ImageNet binary-inpainting benchmark, we use 1,000 validation
images at 256 × 256 resolution, an ADM diffusion model [45], 20 outer Euler steps, and box or
checkerboard masks. The comparison holds the outer schedule and binary mask fixed. Replace [18]

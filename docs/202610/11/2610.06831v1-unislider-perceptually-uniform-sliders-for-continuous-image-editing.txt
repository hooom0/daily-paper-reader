ABSTRACT
Sliders provide an intuitive interface for continuous image editing. In current
generative approaches, however, the slider is simply a rescaling of the method’s
strength parameter, such as an adapter coefficient, a prompt weight, or an inter-
polation factor. This strength relates poorly to perceptual change. The image
can partially revert as the slider moves, long stretches of the range produce no
visible difference, and short intervals transform the image abruptly. Remapping
the strength could fix this uneven pace, but only if the trajectory is monotone,
which current methods do not enforce. We therefore distinguish the slider from
the strength, and require perceptual distance from the input to grow linearly with
the slider value. We introduce UniSlider, a lightweight LoRA trained on a few-
step editing backbone so that its strength approximates this ideal slider. Few-step
sampling lets us impose this objective in pixel space without intermediate ground
truth, and the backbone’s output is preserved at full strength. However, a low-rank
adapter cannot make the strength fully uniform. Our slider is thus an inference-
time remapping of the strength, obtained by adaptive sampling. Since training
optmizes to make the trajectory monotone, this remapping closes the remaining
gap without extra training or parameters. On a new benchmark of 300 continuous
edits evaluating uniformity, monotonicity, edit fidelity, and identity preservation,
UniSlider outperforms all prior methods and is preferred in a user study.

INTRODUCTION
Sliders are the near-universal interface for continuous image editing. In traditional editing software,
a slider behaves like a physical dial, changing the image proportionally to how far the handle moves,
so the same displacement produces a comparable change anywhere in the slider range. Generative
sliders inherit this interface, but most fail to match its behavior. Because the mapping from slider
value to perceptual change is highly non-uniform, users may find them confusing to control.
Existing generative sliders depart from the expected behavior of a physical dial in two ways. The first
is non-uniformity, which has two complementary symptoms: dead zones, intervals across which the
image barely changes, and jumps, intervals across which the image changes far more than a small
drag should imply. The second is non-monotonicity, which appears as reversals: moving the slider
forward partially undoes the edit. Much of the range thus becomes unusable, leaving the user with
only a few effective settings that depend on the edit.
Perceptual uniformity has not, to our knowledge, been posed as an objective for generative image-
editing sliders. Prior work has focused on what the edit does, not on how changes are distributed
along the range. The slider is simply a linear rescaling of the method’s internal strength parameter,
inherited from whichever quantity happens to be continuous: an adapter coefficient (Gandikota
et al., 2024; Zarei et al., 2026), a prompt weight (Parihar et al., 2026; Guerrero-Viu et al., 2024), or
an interpolation factor (Li et al., 2026; Endo et al., 2026). Remapping this strength could fix non-
uniformity, but only if the editing trajectory is monotone. Since the strength is typically supervised
only at its endpoints, if at all, nothing enforces this. We therefore distinguish between the internal
strength, which drives the edit, and the user-facing slider, whose value should grow linearly with
perceptual distance from the input. Figure 2 (top row) illustrates this for SliderEdit (Zarei et al.,
2026), the current state of the art. We sample images at equal steps of its strength and plot the
relative perceptual change of each. An ideal slider traces the diagonal (equal steps, equal change),
but SliderEdit’s curve shows a dead zone and a jump.
We introduce UniSlider, a text-based editing method with a perceptually uniform slider. We re-
purpose a few-step editing backbone by training a lightweight LoRA to edit an image such that
the perceptual distance from the input is proportional to the given strength. Using a few-step edit-
ing backbone allows decoding to pixel space during training and imposing perceptual uniformity
directly, with no ground-truth intermediate images. This alone already removes most of the dead
zones and jumps, making our vanilla LoRA track the ideal perceptual diagonal far more closely
(Fig. 2, middle row). However, the low-rank space cannot reach full uniformity on every image
and editing concept. We close the remaining gap with an inference-time adaptive sampling step
that remaps the strength values onto the slider shown to the user, so that equal slider steps produce
near-equal perceptual change without any additional training (Fig. 2, bottom row). Figure 1 shows
three UniSlider examples.
Evaluating a slider quantitatively is itself challenging. Infinitely many trajectories are valid for the
same edit instruction, so metrics that compare against a reference image do not apply. There is also
no shared benchmark. Existing slider methods report results on small, method-specific test sets that
are not publicly available, while general editing benchmarks such as PIE-Bench (Ju et al., 2024)
include edits with no meaningful intermediate strength, such as adding or removing an object. We
address this evaluation gap by introducing a benchmark specifically designed to assess continuous
image editing.
We summarize our contributions as threefold:
• We identify and formalize perceptual uniformity as a failure mode of text-based image sliders.
• We propose UniSlider, a perceptually uniform slider for continuous image editing. A lightweight
LoRA is trained on top of a few-step editing backbone, optimizing a pixel-space perceptual metric
so that perceptual change grows linearly with the strength value. Inference-time adaptive sampling
then maps the strength to a perceptually uniform slider.
• We introduce a benchmark for continuous text-based image editing, covering 300 cases in three
editing categories, which evaluates uniformity jointly with edit fidelity and identity preservation.
UniSlider performs consistently best across the metrics.

RELATED WORK
Sliders are attractive for editing as they provide means to dial edits up or down rather than accepting
or rejecting them outright. Unfortunately, building generative sliders is challenging, due to the
lack of ground-truth supervision. Existing generative sliders fall into two categories. Training-free
methods expose a continuous coefficient in the inference process: classifier-free-guidance scale,
semantic space interpolation, or velocity-field decomposition without any additional supervision
(Endo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Ekin & Gandelsman, 2026). This approach requires no training
data, but the resulting axis is a byproduct of the diffusion sampler rather than a quantity shaped to
behave predictably, and we find it the less reliable of the two categories (Section 6).
Training-based methods instead learn the strength axis, typically as a LoRA adapter scaled by a
coefficient. Concept Sliders (Gandikota et al., 2024) trains one adapter per concept from a small set
of contrastive image pairs, targeting generation rather than editing a given input. Alchemist (Sharma
et al., 2024) fine-tunes a diffusion model on a synthetic dataset of rendered material variations to
control object-intrinsic attributes. Kontinuous Kontext (Parihar et al., 2026) takes a different route to
supervision as it builds pseudo-ground-truth intermediate edits by morphing between an input and
its edit with a pre-trained morphing method. Consequently, its output quality is tied to the morphing
procedure, and it inherits any artifacts that procedure introduces. SliderEdit (Zarei et al., 2026)
trains a LoRA to cancel the edit of the base editing model, using the frozen model under a neutral
instruction as a self-supervised target. In all these methods, the slider exposed to the user is simply
the learned strength, linearly rescaled to [0, 1], so it inherits any non-uniformity of the strength axis.
Taken together, this line of work establishes that a LoRA’s scaling coefficient is a good place to
expose edit strength as it is simple, requires no architecture changes, and composes with any editing
backbone. However, with the partial exception of Kontinuous Kontext’s morphed trajectories, none
of these methods place any requirement on what happens between the endpoints. The strength
coefficient is supervised, if at all, only at its extremes, and the middle of the interpolation is left
to whatever the LoRA does on its own. This is precisely the gap UniSlider targets. A broader
discussion of related works is provided in Appendix B.

Definition.
Let xsrc be the input image and c be an editing text instruction. A few-step pretrained
image editing model f produces a single output xedit = f(xsrc, c), which we refer to as the de-
fault edit. We extend this model into a continuous one-parameter family xs = f(xsrc, c, s) where
s ∈[0, 1] denotes the editing strength. The strength should provide a smooth transition from the
input image to the default edit while preserving the behavior of the underlying editor at both end-
points.

In particular, we require xs=0 = xsrc and xs=1 = xedit, so that introducing continuous control does
not alter the input or the default edit. Moreover, intermediate outputs xs∈(0,1) should remain on the
perceptual path between the two endpoints and not introduce changes unrelated to the instruction.
Given a perceptual distance D(·, ·), these requirements correspond to
D(xsrc, xs) + D(xs, xedit) = D(xsrc, xedit),
∀s ∈[0, 1].

Most importantly, we want the perceptual change to be distributed uniformly across the slider.
Specifically, the perceptual distance from the input should increase linearly with the slider strength,
D(xsrc, xs) = sD(xsrc, xedit),
∀s ∈[0, 1].
(2)
Under this formulation, equal increments of s correspond to equal perceptual changes, eliminat-
ing the dead zones and jumps commonly observed in existing editing sliders. For the rest of the
paper we use DreamSim (Fu et al., 2023) as perceptual distance D(·, ·), as our empirical evalua-
tion demonstrates it correlates best with human judgments of edit magnitude and training stability
(Section 6).
Method.
We build on few-step text-based diffusion transformer (DiT) editing backbones which
use T = 4 inference steps or less. Strength is exposed through a LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) adapter
applied to all DiT projection layers and scaled by the slider value:
W(s) = W0 + (1 −s)∆W.
(3)
The adapter ∆W vanishes at full strength s = 1, so the model reduces exactly to the frozen backbone
and returns the default edit xs=1 = xedit by construction. The other slider endpoint, the input
xs=0 = xsrc, has to be learned. We share the parameterization in Eq. (3) with Zarei et al. (2026) but
not the training scheme. Rather than supervising the adapter towards a predefined notion of strength,
we train it to produce a trajectory whose perceptual distance from the input follows Eq. (3) while
remaining on the path between the input and the default edit. We use Flux.2-Klein-4B as our image
editing backbone.
Training.
At each training step, we draw s ∼U(0, 1), set the adapter scale according to Eq. (3),
and run the full T-step sampling process to obtain xs. Because T is small, this is affordable inside
the training loop and, following NP-Edit (Kumari et al., 2026), allows us to define the objective
directly on decoded pixels rather than on velocity estimates.
We measure the perceptual distances between the strength-s edit and the two endpoints, penalizing
deviations from their desired values:
L = (D(xsrc, xs) −sD(xsrc, xedit))2
distance to input
+ (D(xs, xedit) −(1 −s)D(xsrc, xedit))2.
distance to default edit
(4)
The two symmetric terms enforce uniform progress along the perceptual path between the input
and the default edit. Because the target distance from the input grows strictly with s and the target
distance to the default edit shrinks, the loss also penalizes reversals. A trajectory whose distance
from the input decreases anywhere, or whose distance to the default edit increases, cannot reach
zero loss. Our loss thus promotes perceptual uniformity, monotonicity and path consistency without
requiring ground-truth intermediate images. xedit is produced by the frozen backbone from its
corresponding xsrc and cached. Figure 3 shows an overview of the UniSlider training scheme.

Training data.
To train the LoRA layers, we curate a small training set of 300 images from the
MIT5K (Bychkovsky et al., 2011) dataset. We then use Qwen3-VL-30B (Bai et al., 2025) to gen-
erate image-specific text instructions describing meaningful and continuous edits for each image.
Examples from the training set are provided in the supplementary material.
Spatial masking.
When the backbone operates in a (latent) space aligned with the image (e.g.
Flux.2-Klein), we can spatially localize the editing process at inference time. Inspired by inpainting
methods (Lugmayr et al., 2022; Avrahami et al., 2022), we preserve the latent features of the input
image z0 in regions that should remain unchanged. Specifically, we modify the latent representation
at each flow-matching step as
zt = zt ⊙M + zσ ⊙(1 −M).
(5)
M denotes a spatial mask and
zσ = (1 −σ)z0 + σϵ,
(6)
where σ is the noise sampler. Unlike conventional inpainting methods, which typically use a binary
mask, we allow M ∈[0, 1] to enable smooth spatial transitions between edited regions (mask values
1) and preserved regions (mask values 0).

IMPROVING PERCEPTUAL UNIFORMITY WITH ADAPTIVE SAMPLING
The training objective in Eq. (4) produces slider trajectories that satisfy endpoint consistency, remain
on the editing path, and vary smoothly and monotonically with the slider strength. While these
properties provide a strong basis for perceptually uniform editing, the limited capacity of a low-rank
model adapter prevents achieving perfect uniformity across arbitrary image–instruction pairs.
To characterize the capacity limitation, we overfit the adapter on N ∈1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 examples and
evaluate the achieved uniformity on them, sweeping the adapter rank r ∈4, 8, 16, 32. In Fig. 4,
each panel plots the perceptual (DreamSim) distance D(·, ·) between the input to the slider output
at strength s, normalized by the total distance between input and default edit. An ideal, perceptually
uniform slider traces the diagonal, whereas flat and steep regions represent dead zones and jumps.
Figure 4 reveals a consistent trend. For small N, the adapter achieves near-perfect uniformity, but
increasing the number of examples eventually leads to less uniform trajectories. This transition
occurs at larger N when the adapter rank is higher, indicating that the adapter capacity determines
how many image–instruction pairs can be represented with highly uniform trajectories. Increasing
the rank can extend this capacity, but at the cost of increased parameter count and computation,
contrary to our goal of lightweight adaptation. Instead of increasing model capacity, we therefore
ask whether the existing trajectory can be traversed more effectively at inference time.
Adaptive sampling.
We introduce adaptive sampling as a lightweight extension to our training
scheme that improves perceptual uniformity without modifying or retraining the learned adapter.
Rather than exposing the strength directly as the slider (i.e. sampling the learned trajectory at eq-
uispaced values of s), we place the samples adaptively by recursively splitting the interval with the
largest perceptual distance under D (Appendix C). We then use these samples to remap the strength
axis. For each edit, we compute a mapping u = g(s) from strength to the user-facing slider value
u ∈[0, 1], such that the perceptual distance from the input grows approximately linearly with u,
as in Eq. (2). This remapping relies on the monotonicity obtained through training. Prior methods,
whose strength is supervised at most at its endpoints, offer no such guarantee, and remapping cannot
repair their reversals (Table 3).
The last column of Fig. 4 demonstrates the benefit of this inference-time strategy. We train a single
adapter on our full 300-example training set at different ranks r and apply adaptive sampling at in-
ference time, evaluating on the N = 32 (now unseen) examples corresponding to the final column.
Despite using the same adapter and sampling budget, adaptive sampling substantially improves the
uniformity of the resulting slider trajectories by being an average of ×3.7 times closer to the diagonal
in terms of RMSE againts the N = 32 experiment. Crucially, this improvement requires no addi-
tional training or model parameters with a negligible overhead. Thus, adaptive sampling provides a
simple way to exploit the smooth trajectories learned by the LoRA adapter while compensating for
variations in how perceptual change is distributed along the strength axis.

BENCHMARK
Existing image editing benchmarks, such as Pie-Bench (Ju et al., 2024), primarily evaluate whether
a model can perform a given edit, but are not designed to assess continuous editing across slider
strengths. Other works therefore rely on small, concept-specific evaluation sets, limiting the diver-
sity of edits and scenes considered. To address this gap, we introduce a benchmark specifically
designed to evaluate continuous image editing, comprising 300 carefully curated image–instruction
pairs spanning diverse scenes and editing concepts. We categorize the edits into three types: Global
or Stylization, which apply a consistent change across the image; Attribute or Local, which modify
a specific object or region; and Structural, which alter the image content with changes that depend
on its semantics. Detailed examples of the image and instruction generation, full metric definitions
and evaluation protocols are provided in the Supplementary Material.
Images and text instructions.
We select diverse images from the publicly available Adobe-MIT-
FiveK (Bychkovsky et al., 2011) dataset and curate text instructions describing edits that admit a
meaningful continuous progression from the original image to the desired result. Instructions were
designed to avoid inherently binary operations, such as object removal, and instead cover edits for
which intermediate strengths are well-defined. We further manually curate the resulting image–
instruction pairs to ensure that the requested edits are meaningful and sufficiently diverse across the
three categories.
Metrics.
We evaluate six properties that characterize a high-quality image editing slider: edit
fidelity, identity preservation, endpoint consistency, image quality, strength monotonicity, and per-
ceptual uniformity. Edit fidelity measures whether the requested edit is successfully performed at
full strength, while identity preservation evaluates whether the original scene and unrelated content
are maintained. At its lower endpoint, the slider should return the input image unchanged. We assess
image quality on all generated images using a no-reference quality metric. Strength monotonicity
measures violations in the progression of edit fidelity, while perceptual uniformity is measured from
the variation of perceptual distances between consecutive slider outputs. We use complementary
image- and VLM-based metrics for these properties. Full metric definitions, validations, examples
of the metrics, and evaluation protocols are provided in Appendix E.

EXPERIMENTS
Benchmark analysis.
Table 1 reports all the slider methods of Section 2 on our benchmark. These
methods use different editing backbones, so we normalize the comparison when possible. The
training-free methods (FlowSlider, VeloEdit, DiffusionSteering) carry no dataset dependency, and
we reimplement them on Flux.2-Klein-4B, the same backbone UniSlider uses. Kontinuous Kontext
and SliderEdit are tied to their own training data, which is not publicly available, so we report them
on Flux.1-Kontext as published. For SliderEdit, we additionally retrain it on our training set with the
Flux.2-Klein-4B backbone (see Appendix E). We also include the closed-source Firefly Tune from
Adobe Firefly. UniSlider achieves the best overall performance, consistently ranking either first or
second across all metrics, with the largest gains observed in monotonicity and uniformity, the two
properties we identified as critical for effective sliders. Note that SliderEdit and UniSlider preserve
the editing capability of the base model at full strength, while the other methods degrade it because
of their formulation.
User study.
We also conducted a two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) user study with 25 ob-
servers, with a total of 881 pairs of sliders compared from the 7 methods shown in Table 1 where
we ask: ”Which method is better overall?” and ”Which one spreads the change more evenly?”,
⋆and ≡, respectively in Table 1. UniSlider wins 82% of the comparisons for both questions as
computed using the Bradley-Terry method (Bradley & Terry, 1952), outperforming the second-best
method by a large margin in both overall quality and uniformity. We provide further metrics in the
supplementary section Appendix E.

Figure 5 shows UniSlider on three edits: a global one (turning the scene into
modelling clay), a local one (making the man more excited), and a structural one (turning on the
burner). In all three cases, the intermediate steps are perceptually evenly spaced, with no dead zones

of the crocodile. The facial example alters only the expression, keeping identity, make-up and hair
stable at every slider value.The burner example increases the intensity of the flame in perceptually
equal steps.
Simultaneous and localized edits.
Figure 6 combines a masked edit with a global one in a 2D
grid. The horizontal axis applies a masked edit restricted to the blue car (the masked region is shown
in red in the first cell), while the vertical axis applies a global stylization to the whole scene. We
obtain the second axis by composition. We run a forward pass with the first instruction, then feed
each output back as the input for the second. UniSlider is trained on a single instruction at a time
and never sees composed edits or spatial masks during training, yet the grid remains approximately
uniform along both axes and the two edits remain largely independent, moving along one axis neither
undoes nor amplifies the other. See Appendix F for additional qualitative results, including masked
and 2D edits.
Training analysis.
UniSlider relies on a perceptual distance D(·, ·) to define perceptual uniformity
and to train for it. We therefore examine three questions: (i) whether perceptual distances track how
humans judge the magnitude of an edit, (ii) how much the choice of D matters for training, and
(iii) whether supervising intermediate strengths is necessary or constraining the endpoints alone is
enough.
(i) Learned perceptual metrics are trained to judge which of two concepts is closer to a reference (Fu
et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2026), but our method needs D to measure the magnitude (e.g. be cali-
brated) of an edit, comparably across edit types. To validate our intended use, we designed a 2AFC
study. 15 annotators saw an input image and two edits of it, made with different instructions that
represented different strengths, and chose the one that changed more. We correlate the aggregated
choices by Bradley–Terry (Bradley & Terry, 1952) with each metric’s distance to the input (Table 2,
first two columns). PLCC is the key measure, since uniformity requires D to scale linearly with
perceived change. Perceptual metrics correlate best (PLCC 0.81–0.86, highest for DreamSim and
TPIPS), while pixel RMSE (0.74) and a color histogram (0.51) lag. This proves that perceptual
metrics are a good proxy for measuring strength, specially when compared to pixel-wise metrics.

(iii) We train a variant of our model supervised only at the lower endpoint. Mathematically, this cor-
responds to minimizing L = D(xsrc, xs=0), ensuring that the lower endpoint of the slider represents
the unedited image. Consequently, the intermediate edit strengths remain unconstrained. This vari-
ant does not produce a uniform slider, even when combined with the adaptive sampling at inference.
Quantitatively, the produced slider is worse by a factor of 3 in monotonicity and uniformity. This
drop is caused because while the endpoints pin the ends of the trajectory, they say nothing about
how it is traversed in between, which isolates the contribution of the uniformity term.
Adaptive sampling analysis.
Table 3 applies our adaptive sampling to other sliders to isolate the
contributions of training and remapping. Under DreamSim, the metric used for sampling, remap-
ping improves the uniformity of every method, bringing SliderEdit to the level of UniSlider with-
out remapping. Under TPIPS, which is used in neither training nor sampling, the gain shrinks for
the baselines, whereas UniSlider still improves by 9%. Remapping can only equalize a trajectory
that is already smooth and monotone, and SliderEdit’s is not. Its VVQA increases by 34%, because
remapping concentrates samples where the change is fastest, which is precisely where the trajectory
doubles back. For UniSlider, whose trajectory is optimized to be monotone and uniform, the same
procedure instead reduces VVQA by 13%. Training and adaptive sampling are therefore complemen-
tary. UniSlider without remapping already matches or outperforms every baseline with remapping,
and combining both is best overall. Further analysis and additional ablations are reported in Appen-
dices G and H, respectively.
7
CONCLUSIONS
We have shown that generative editing sliders are not perceptually uniform, and that this is largely
a matter of how the strength is parameterized rather than of what the base model can generate.
UniSlider separates the slider exposed to the user from the strength that drives the edit. It trains a
lightweight LoRA with a pixel-space perceptual objective so that its strength approximates a percep-
tually uniform slider, and remaps the strength at inference time to recover the uniformity a low-rank
adapter cannot represent on its own. The result is a slider whose value has the same meaning across
images and edits, as confirmed by our benchmark, which measures uniformity jointly with edit
fidelity and identity preservation.
Limitations.
UniSlider is bounded by its editing backbone as the slider interpolates towards the
backbone’s default output, so any misinterpreted instruction or artifact is carried along the whole
trajectory. Likewise, UniSlider aims to be uniform under D rather than under human perception.
Our user study shows that current metrics are a good proxy, but not a perfect one. Adaptive sampling,
limited by a fixed rendering budget, approaches uniformity under D without reaching it exactly. See
Appendix C for further details. Training an editing model whose strength is perceptually uniform
by design, removing the need for remapping, is a natural next step.

FURTHER RELATED WORK DISCUSSION
Section 2 covers slider methods for continuous image editing. Due to space constraints, we extend
that discussion here with the broader trends in text-based image editing that our backbone builds on,
and with interactive editing paradigms that give users control along axes other than strength.
Text-based image editing.
Early text-driven editors operated by inverting a real image into a pre-
trained diffusion model’s noise space and then manipulating the generation process directly through
cross-attention injection (Hertz et al., 2023), partial re-noising and re-denoising (Meng et al., 2022),
or latent optimization (Kawar et al., 2023). These methods require no editing-specific training data
and can be strikingly precise, but each edit costs a fresh inversion and often per-image optimization,
and the target has to be specified as a new caption or embedding rather than a direct instruction.
Instruction-tuned editors removed this overhead by training a single feed-forward model on (image,
instruction, edited image) triples: InstructPix2Pix (Brooks et al., 2023) bootstrapped such a dataset
from GPT-3 and Prompt-to-Prompt, MagicBrush (Zhang et al., 2023a) replaced synthetic triples
with human-annotated ones and added multi-turn edits, and Emu Edit (Sheynin et al., 2024) scaled
this recipe across a wide range of edit types with task embeddings. These editors generalize well and
require only a single instruction at inference, but still rely on a many-step diffusion sampler, and ex-
pose no notion of edit strength: the model produces one edit, not a family of them. The most recent
instruction-based editors move this recipe onto rectified-flow diffusion transformers with few-step
distillation (Labs et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025), which is what makes decoding to pixel space inside
the training loop (central to our method, Section 3) affordable in the first place.

Interactive image editing.
A separate line of work gives users control over where or how an
edit happens, rather than only what it is. Spatial conditioning methods such as ControlNet (Zhang

Implementation details for reproduction.
We train a single LoRA adapter on the attention pro-
jections of every transformer block of FLUX.2-Klein1, rank r = 32 at 4B and r = 16 at 9B. The
strength s is not a network input but the adapter scale, applied as (1 −s). At s = 1 the adapter van-
ishes and the frozen backbone produces its own edit, which serves as the training target. Supervision
therefore requires only (image, instruction) pairs, never ground-truth intermediates.
We train for 2000 steps on our training dataset with AdamW, learning rate 10−4, gradient clipping at
1.0, batch size 1, and a 768-pixel long side; each step draws one example and one s ∼U(0, 1]. One
choice matters for reproduction. The noise vector is drawn once per example and reused at every s
at inference time, so a sweep is one trajectory. Inference uses four steps with σend = 1/28. Training
a 4B adapter takes about two hours on one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 and yields an 8 MB adapter.
Frames shown for UniSlider are placed by the adaptive sampling algorithm. Other baselines use a
uniform grid over their own documented control range or parameter. All methods are compared at
11 frames.

We start from the two endpoints, xsrc and the default edit xedit, and render the midpoint xs=0.5. We
then measure D between adjacent frames and repeatedly split the largest gap in ϕ until the budget is
exhausted. Rather than splitting at the midpoint in s, we fit a monotone PCHIP spline through the
measurements of ϕ, invert it to locate the perceptual midpoint of that gap, and render there. This
targets the strength at which the trajectory is expected to be halfway through the gap in perceptual
terms, which is where a uniform slider would place its next stop.
Measuring ϕ during sampling has a second benefit We can detect when a newly rendered frame is
perceptually too close to one we already have and leave it out. Users are better served by fewer
frames than by a slider with visually repeated stops. We therefore discard a new point for display
if it falls within ε of a neighbor in ϕ, while keeping it as a measurement so that it still sharpens the
spline. We set ε as a fraction of D(xsrc, xedit) (0.03) rather than as an absolute distance, since D
varies by over an order of magnitude across edits. Algorithm 1 summarizes the procedure.
Adaptive sampling adds no parameters and no training. It requires only the ability to render at an
arbitrary strength and a distance with which to measure, so it applies to any continuous editor, and
we show in Section 6 that it also improves the baselines. Its one assumption is that ϕ is monotone.
If a trajectory doubles back, the largest gap has no well-defined perceptual midpoint and placement
degrades towards uniform sampling. The overhead is negligible in practice. Rendering 11 frames
with Flux-Klein-4B takes 7.02 s on average and 7.16 s with adaptive sampling, an increase of 2%,
measured on a NVIDIA RTX Pro 6000.
Limitations.
We expand on the three limitations discussed in Section 7.
UniSlider interpolates between the input and the default edit of the frozen backbone, so it cannot
correct the backbone’s failures. Figure 7 shows an example with Flux.2-Klein-4B. Besides inflat-
ing the ball, the default edit changes its colors, and since every intermediate frame moves towards
this output, the unrequested color change is carried along the whole slider. Conversely, UniSlider
benefits directly from stronger backbones, since adapting it to a new few-step editor only requires
retraining the adapter (Appendix H).
Our slider is uniform under D, which is only a proxy for human perception. DreamSim is among
the metrics that best correlate with human judgments of edit magnitude (Table 2), but the correlation
is not perfect, and any disagreement translates directly into non-uniformity as perceived by the user.
In Fig. 7, DreamSim cannot separate the requested change (inflating the ball) from unrelated ones. It
places the second frame on the diagonal because a white patch on the right part of the ball has turned
red, although the ball is still deflated. The slider is thus uniform under DreamSim, but a human
observer sees a dead zone. Text-conditioned metrics such as TPIPS could, in principle, measure
only the change related to the instruction, but with 14 billion parameters, TPIPS is too expensive
for interactive use. Since UniSlider is agnostic to the choice of D in both training and sampling, it
would directly benefit from more efficient text-conditioned or better-calibrated perceptual metrics.
Adaptive sampling estimates the remapping g from a fixed budget of renders (typically the number
of frames shown by the slider), so it approaches uniformity under D without reaching it exactly.
To measure the remaining gap, we compare it with a dense reference. We render the trajectory at
51 equispaced strengths, fit a monotone PCHIP spline to ϕ(s), invert it at the target slider values
uk = k/10, and render the resulting frames, for a total of 60 renders. Because it samples the
trajectory densely, this reference closely approximates the exact remapping. Figure 8 compares both
on one edit. The dashed red curve is the fitted ϕ(s), which corresponds to our Vanilla slider without
remapping (s = u). The blue curve shows adaptive sampling with only 11 renders, and the solid
red curve shows the frames rendered by the dense reference at each uk. Note that we only show
five images due to space constraints. The Vanilla slider already stays relatively close to the diagonal
but follows an S-shape. Adaptive sampling brings the curve much closer to the diagonal, and the
dense reference matches it almost exactly. For instance, its frame at u = 0.5 lies exactly halfway, in
perceptual distance, between the input and the default edit. The remaining gap of adaptive sampling
is small compared with the non-uniformity of the Vanilla slider. The frames produced by adaptive
sampling are also visually very similar to those of the dense reference, while requiring less than
one-fifth as many renders. Adaptive sampling therefore offers a favourable trade-off for interactive
use, and its budget can be increased when exact uniformity matters more than latency.

FURTHER TRAINING DATASET DETAILS
Training needs only (image, instruction) pairs: the edited image at s=1 is the frozen backbone’s
default output, so no edited image, no intermediate, and no human annotation is required. We
assemble 300 such pairs from MIT-Adobe-FiveK (Bychkovsky et al., 2011). Source photographs
are drawn from the FiveK pool after removing every image used by our benchmark, so the training
set and the evaluation set share no image.
Instructions are generated through a three-stage VLM pipeline. Each sampled photograph is first
described neutrally and screened for content sufficiency: blank walls, plain sky, water surfaces and
abstract textures are discarded, since they offer nothing an edit can act on. Surviving images are
paired with a concept seed sampled from a fixed pool of 500 seeds spanning art medium, artist style,
cinematic and videogame looks, color tone and art movement (global); person and object attributes;
and count increase, addition or resizing, atmosphere, vegetation and urban density (structural). A
second stage writes an instruction conditioned on the image and its seed, and a judge stage accepts
or rejects it.
One constraint on instruction wording is specific to this setting and worth stating. The instruction
must not encode any degree, range, percentage or mechanism. No “from 1 to 5”, no “by 30%”, no
“slightly”. Strength is carried entirely by s, and an instruction that names its own intensity would
make the text and the slider compete to specify the same quantity. The resulting instructions are
correspondingly short: a median of eight words (range 4–17), e.g. “Add more cars to the street”.
We over-generate and filter. 540 candidates against a target of 300, leaving room for rejections.

FURTHER BENCHMARK DETAILS
Motivation.
Current slider methods are evaluated on small, method-specific test sets that make
cross-paper comparison unreliable. Kontinuous Kontext (Parihar et al., 2026) evaluates on a held-
out split of its own morphing dataset, which is unfair to methods that were not trained on that kind of
data. SliderEdit (Zarei et al., 2026) builds a facial-editing benchmark, which is too domain-specific
to say anything about performance elsewhere. Neither benchmark is public. The alternative some
methods take is to reuse an existing image-editing benchmark: FlowSlider (Endo et al., 2026) and
VeloEdit (Li et al., 2026) both draw images from PIE-Bench (Ju et al., 2024), but PIE-Bench targets
one-shot edits and includes operations such as object removal or addition that have no meaningful
intermediate strength as an object is either there or it is not. Reusing it for a slider benchmark inherits
that mismatch. A further, more practical problem cuts across all of these: training-free methods are
steered by a pair of prompts (positive and negative), while trained sliders take a single instruction,
so any shared benchmark has to supply both prompt formats for every example or silently favor one
family of methods over the other. No existing benchmark does this. Together these gaps (small and
non-public test sets, evaluation domains too narrow to generalize from, and a benchmark format
built for a different kind of edit) are why we build a dedicated benchmark for continuous image
editing.
Images and instructions.
The benchmark holds 300 examples, 100 each of global, attribute and
structural edits. Source photographs come from MIT-Adobe FiveK (Bychkovsky et al., 2011) at
16


Preprint
up to 1600 pixels on the long side, in both orientations, and are disjoint from the training set. The
training data was sampled only after every benchmark image had been excluded, so no photograph
appears in both. We only use the MIT-Adobe-FiveK as a good pool of high-quality and diverse
images.
Each example carries an instruction, and neutral descriptions of the input and of the intended result
for training-free methods. Benchmark instructions are markedly longer and more specific than the
training ones, a median of 18 words against 8, because they must pin down a single intended outcome
precisely enough to be scored in the edit fidelity section. We show examples from our benchmark
when comparing UniSlider with other slider methods.
Metrics.
Every method is rendered at K = 11 slider positions s0 < s1 < · · · < s10, normalized
so that s0 is the method’s minimum strength and s10 its full edit. We write xsrc for the input image
and xsk for a method’s own output at position sk.
Because methods render at different native resolutions (768 × 512 to 1600 × 1067), every image is
resampled to a common 768-pixel long side before any metric is computed. Without this, pixel-space
and no-reference quality scores would partly measure render size rather than edit quality.
A benchmark for continuous editing needs to characterize six properties, and we group the metrics
accordingly:
• Edit fidelity. Does the intended edit happen?
• Identity preservation. Is everything else preserved?
• Endpoint consistency. Does the slider return the input unchanged at its minimum setting?
• Image quality. Are the output images free of degradation?
• Strength monotonicity. Does the slider move consistently in one direction?
• Perceptual uniformity. Are its increments perceptually even?
None of these six questions has a single correct answer. A continuous edit has no ground truth as
infinitely many intermediate trajectories satisfy the same instruction at the same strength, and no
reference image exists to compare against. This is why every metric used is either no-reference or
judges a property directly from the pair of images or the trajectory itself, rather than measuring dis-
tance to a fixed target the way conventional image editing benchmarks do. With these six questions
in hand, we now define the metrics used for each.

Image quality.
Continuous editors can satisfy every other metric while degrading the image, so
we add no-reference quality measures evaluated at all strength levels and averaged. In Table 1 we

responds to semantic degradation that MUSIQ can miss. We do not use NIQE (Mittal et al., 2012)
as it is tuned for compression and blur statistics and scores diffusion artifacts poorly.

The original work adapts FLUX.1-Kontext and Qwen-Image-Edit. To separate the contribution of
the slider formulation from that of the backbone, we retrain it on FLUX.2-Klein-4B, the same base
model, training set and schedule used by our method. We keep the published hyper-parameters:
rank-16 LoRA (3.9M parameters) on the eight attention projections (Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo and their
context counterparts), learning rate 5 × 10−5, gradient clipping at 1.0, and 1000 optimisation steps
at an effective batch size of 6, trained in about one hour on a single NVIDIA A40 80GB GPU.
Three changes follow from the backbone and the evaluation protocol. First, FLUX.2-Klein-4B is not
guidance-distilled, so the guidance scale of 3.5 used for FLUX.1-Kontext has no corresponding input
and is omitted. Second, Klein encodes prompts without pooled projections and assembles latents
through a different packing path, so the conditioning tensors are rebuilt accordingly while playing
identical roles in the loss. Third, the reference implementation center-crops inputs to 10242 so that a
batch can be stacked; because our benchmark requires the input aspect ratio to be preserved and our
training images are of mixed orientation, we instead train at batch size 1 with gradient accumulation
6 on aspect-preserving resizes, which keeps the effective batch unchanged while avoiding a train/test
mismatch in field of view.

FURTHER ANALYSIS
Perceptual metrics correlation with human judgment.
Learned perceptual metrics such as
DreamSim (Fu et al., 2023) and TPIPS (Wang et al., 2026) are trained on relative human judg-
ments. Annotators are shown two candidates and a reference and asked which candidate is closer,
never by how much. Their values are therefore calibrated for ranking, and nothing in their training
guarantees that a distance twice as large corresponds to a change that looks twice as big. Perceptual
uniformity requires precisely that, so we validate it directly with a user study.
Every uniformity measure in this paper rests on a perceptual distance D, so we check that D agrees
with people when measuring strength before using it to score methods. We built 15 scenes, each with
a concept (e.g. “make it winter”) rendered at four prompt-specified magnitudes from subtle to very
strong, and collected 538 two-alternative forced-choice judgements. We show the original image
and two edits and ask: "which edit changed the image more?". For each observer, nine pairs were
repeated to measure self-consistency, and discard the observers that voted differently in duplicated
pairs. Figure 11 (right) shows two of the 15 examples.
We recover a Bradley–Terry scale per scene from these judgments and correlate it with each metric’s
distance to the input, reporting both Pearson (PLCC) and Spearman (SRCC) correlations in Table 2.
SRCC measures whether a metric orders edits as humans do, while PLCC measures whether its val-
ues scale linearly with perceived change, which is what perceptual uniformity requires. DreamSim
and TPIPS agree best with human judgments and are effectively tied, followed by LPIPS and CLIP
. Pixel-space RMSE is clearly behind and a color-histogram distance further still. For every metric
PLCC and SRCC are within 0.01 of each other, so the metrics that rank edits correctly also scale
close to linearly with perceived magnitude, which is what justifies using D as a measure of strength
rather than only as a ranking signal. See Figure 11 (left) for a plot of the results.

twice as slow. At 8B parameters and 7.2 s for 11 frames, Flux-Klein-4B is also the most efficient of
the four, generating a full slider four times faster than Qwen-Image-Edit and nine times faster than
FireRed-1.0.
LoRA rank.
The bottom block of Table 6 sweeps the adapter rank r ∈{4, 8, 16, 32} on Flux-
Klein-4B. Edit fidelity and identity preservation are unchanged across the sweep, and uniformity
improves only marginally with rank, with no measurable cost in inference time. This is consistent
with the capacity analysis of Section 4. Rank governs how many image–instruction pairs the adapter
can hold a uniform trajectory for, but on the full dataset the residual non-uniformity is recovered by
adaptive sampling rather than by additional capacity. We use r = 32 throughout, as it is best on
every metric at a cost of 7.9M trainable parameters, under 0.1% of the backbone.

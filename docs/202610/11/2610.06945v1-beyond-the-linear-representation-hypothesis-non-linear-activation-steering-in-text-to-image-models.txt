ABSTRACT
Mechanistic interpretability often relies on the Linear Representation Hypothesis
(LRH), which assumes that high-level concepts are encoded as linear directions
in activation space. Yet a natural visual concept does not necessarily require a
linear visual transition: between sunny and stormy lies an intermediate weather
state such as a sky with a few white clouds, not simply a weaker storm; between
a caterpillar and a butterfly, the progression is not a caterpillar with continuously
growing wings. This raises the question of whether such true intermediate states
are also represented nonlinearly by the model. Indeed, when we prompt text-to-
image models directly for intermediate attributes, their activations rarely fall along
the straight direction connecting the endpoints. Therefore, we propose KANSteer,
which models concept traversal as a curve passing through its intermediate states.
Seeking a representation that is both simple and interpretable, we propose to use
Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs), which provide a one-dimensional coor-
dinate whose learned functions define the trajectory. This allows the steering di-
rection to vary along the concept while preserving an interpretable representa-
tion. Across several concepts and text-to-image diffusion transformers, we find
that their activation trajectories substantially deviate from straight lines, and that
KANSteer provide a closer fit and smoother traversal of intermediate attributes
than linear steering.

INTRODUCTION
In deep learning, many semantic concepts admit an ordered progression, yet their representations
need not change in a uniform, linear fashion. For example, the transition between seasons, the
metamorphosis of an insect, or the progression of human aging is not simply a matter of scaling a
single visual feature. Instead, these concepts evolve through meaningful intermediate states, where
different attributes emerge, change, and disappear at different stages of the progression. Despite
the complexity of these transitions, mechanistic interpretability has predominantly relied on the
Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH) to understand how models encode them. LRH posits that
high-level concepts are represented by straight directions within a model’s activation space. In
text-to-image diffusion models, this perspective has motivated methods for precise control over
generated content (Surkov et al., 2024), model debiasing (Modell, 2026) or unlearning (Gaintseva
et al., 2025; Cywi´nski & Deja, 2025). However, recent research in Large Language Models (LLMs)
has highlighted a critical limitation of the LRH: while concepts may be linearly identifiable, they
are not always linearly traversable. When navigating between related states–such as moving from
Thursday to Friday or adjusting a character count-the underlying geometric path forms a curved
manifold rather than a straight line (Raval et al., 2026; Wurgaft et al., 2026).
This distinction becomes particularly important in visual domain. For a discrete structure such as
days of the week, the goal is ultimately to reach one of a finite number of states; an imperfect
trajectory can still place the representation close to the intended state. Visual concepts that are
inherently continuous, offer no such simplification. Between two endpoints lie a sequence of mean-

ingful intermediate states, and these states are themselves part of the concept. If they do not lie on
a straight path, linear steering can therefore misrepresent the concept throughout the traversal, even
when its endpoints are correctly identified. We directly test this by examining the activation tra-
jectories induced by ordered visual attributes in text-to-image diffusion models. For each concept,
we prompt the model with a sequence of progressively varying attribute levels and measure how
their corresponding representations evolve in activation space. Across a range of visual concepts,
these intermediate representations consistently deviate from straight line connecting the endpoints,
revealing that visual concepts are not always linearly traversable.
This suggests that a concept can still have a simple 1D representation without following a fixed
linear path for its intermediate states. We therefore model concept traversal as a one-dimensional
curve through activation space. Using a Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN) (Liu et al., 2024), we
parameterize this curve directly from the observed intermediate states, allowing steering direction
to vary along trajectory while retaining a single interpretable coordinate. In this way, the representa-
tion preserves the simplicity of linear steering without imposing a straight path on a concept whose
geometry may be inherently curved. We evaluate this approach across a range of ordered visual con-
cepts and diffusion-model configurations. For each concept, we compare nonlinear steering against
a linear trajectory under the same set of observed attribute states. While both approaches recover the
endpoints, their behavior between them differs substantially: the nonlinear trajectory more closely
follows the intermediate states in activation space and produces images that better match both the
corresponding text and their prompt-generated visual references. These improvements are reflected
consistently across directional and image-based similarity measures, indicating that the learned tra-
jectory captures the progression between attributes rather than merely connecting its endpoints. We
further test whether the learned trajectories can extend beyond the range of attributes used to fit
them. By withholding later stages of each ordered concept during fitting, we evaluate whether the
resulting trajectories can recover them. The nonlinear trajectories provide substantially more faith-
ful extrapolations, both qualitatively and quantitatively, whereas linear steering remains constrained
to a fixed direction and increasingly departs from the underlying progression.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We demonstrate that visual concepts in T2I diffusion models are not linearly traversable:
intermediate attributes deviate from straight directions defined by their endpoints.
• We introduce KANSteer, a 1D-nonlinear steering framework that replaces the fixed steer-
ing direction with a learned activation-space trajectory, preserving an interpretable scalar
concept coordinate while allowing the steering direction to vary along the trajectory.
• Given the same supervision, KANSteer improves over the linear direction on every metric
and all three model configurations, raising directional CLIP from 0.108 to 0.170 and low-
ering FID from 47.9 to 28.6 on FLUX.1-schnell (1-step). Notably, it transfers unchanged
from the FLUX.1-schnell 1-step activations it is fitted on to FLUX.1-schnell 4-step and
FLUX.1-dev generation. Furthermore, we show that KANSteer can extrapolate beyond its
fitted range while generating realistic samples.

RELATED WORK
Linear representation hypothesis (LRH).
The LRH proposes that a high-level concept is repre-
sented by a direction in activation space. This idea has been formalized for language models by Park
et al. (2023; 2024); Jiang et al. (2024), and used to control properties such as truthfulness (Li et al.,
2023), sentiment (Tigges et al., 2023), and refusal (Arditi et al., 2024) by adding a fixed steering
vector. An analogous picture has emerged in vision, where diffusion models exhibit surprisingly
linear internal representations. Simple linear probes can extract scene properties–such as depth and
figure-ground structure–from intermediate activations well before denoising is complete (Chen et al.,
2023). Similarly, linear projections of attention layers in diffusion transformers yield sharp concept
saliency maps (Helbling et al., 2025). Recent work highlights that specific knowledge localizes to
just a few blocks (Basu et al., 2024a;b; Zarei et al., 2025), and sparse autoencoders can decompose
these activations into interpretable, causal features (Surkov et al., 2024; Cywi´nski & Deja, 2025).
These structural discoveries naturally motivated direct interventions. Early methods identified the
U-Net bottleneck as a semantic latent space that is largely homogeneous and linear, where scal-

2024; 2025). Yet, despite the variety of these activation-space methods, underlying intervention
mechanism remains identical: they all steer by updating x′ = x + αv for a fixed vector v.
Extending linear representation hypothesis
In LLMs, several works argue that the linear mani-
fold does not hold for all concepts. The clearest example is the representation of weekdays, where
each day is identifiable as a separate direction but the seven form a circle rather than a line, so
moving from Monday to Tuesday requires a different displacement than moving from Thursday
to Friday (Engels et al., 2024). Character counts likewise lie on curved low-dimensional mani-
folds (Gurnee et al., 2026). A concept can therefore be linearly identifiable without being linearly
traversable. This has motivated steering methods that replace a fixed direction with a trajectory,
learning a concept manifold with a supervised probe (Modell, 2026), obtaining curved trajectories
with polynomial kernel PCA (Raval et al., 2026), or fitting cubic splines through concept centroids,
which keeps steering on trajectories already present in the model where a linear direction passes
through off-manifold regions and produces unnatural outputs (Wurgaft et al., 2026). Engels et al.
(2024) note that such representations are ”mostly discontinuous”, as the model does not naturally
represent states between adjacent weekdays or months. Visual attributes differ, since meaningful
intermediates are expected even when only a few are specified, and in T2I diffusion models they can
be generated and observed directly. Unlike prior approaches that recover trajectories from activation
geometry or concept centroids, we fit a parametric trajectory with a KAN to the intermediate states
themselves, which gives an explicit scalar coordinate and a channel-wise decomposition, and makes
the trajectory define the steering rule rather than being fitted separately from the intervention.

We test this directly by constructing intermediate states independent of steering direction. For each
concept, we specify five ordered attributes a1 < a2 < · · · < aK, with first and last serving as
the steering endpoints and the remaining three defining intermediate targets. For example, for hair
curliness we use straight, slightly wavy, wavy, curly, and coily. For aging, we use preteen, teenage,
young adult, middle-aged, and elderly. We then generate images for all five attributes using same
prompt template, and same seed, changing only attribute phrase (See Section 5.1 for more details).
These prompt-generated representations give us an explicit trajectory of intermediate states against
which to evaluate linear steering. For each concept, we first learn the linear steering direction using
only the two endpoint attributes. We then sweep the steering coefficient between these endpoints
and compare the resulting representations to those obtained from the three intermediate prompts.
To assess whether the ordered attributes of a concept lie on a straight path, here we analyze the
geometry of the attributes in the activation space. For each concept we take the delta activation of
every {attribute, seed} pair, and since all K attributes of a seed are generated from the same initial
noise, we subtract the per-seed mean across attributes. This removes the shared initial noise and
leaves the variation which the attributes induce. We then project the centered activations onto their
leading principal components. If the attributes are linearly encoded, the K attribute clusters would
fall on a single line in this projection, and their ordering would run straight along it. However, the
results in Figure 1 reveal that across the concepts, the intermediate attributes sit off the straight path
between the two endpoint attributes. A quantitative analysis confirms that intermediate attributes
deviate substantially from the straight path between the endpoints (Appendix A.1).

METHOD
In Section 3, we establish that the attributes of many concepts do not lie on a line in activation space,
so a single direction cannot carry a concept through its intermediate states. This motivates replacing
fixed steering direction used by linear activation steering with a 1D trajectory. To do so, we fit a

model of how activations change across all K attributes of a concept and derive the steering rule
from that model. Steering then amounts to moving the activation along this learned trajectory.
4.1
A ONE-DIMENSIONAL TRAJECTORY MODEL
We model the activations induced by the ordered attributes as points along a shared concept tra-
jectory in activation space. For each seed, we generate one image for each attribute a1, . . . , aK
from the same initial noise, changing only the attribute phrase. Because the resulting images share
their initial noise, their activations retain much of the same image-specific content, such as layout,
identity, and background. We therefore view the activation under attribute ak as
xk ≈b + γ(tk),
(1)
where b ∈RD is the content shared across the attributes of a given seed and patch, and γ : R →RD
is the concept trajectory. The trajectory describes how the representation changes as the attribute
progresses, while the scalar tk specifies the position of attribute ak along that trajectory.
A displacement from xi to xj can be expressed as γ(tj)−γ(ti). Thus, steering requires two compo-
nents: a model of the trajectory γ, and a way to assign an activation its coordinate on that trajectory.

4.3
STEERING ALONG THE LEARNED TRAJECTORY
Once the encoder and trajectory have been learned, steering requires no separate vector estimation.
Given an activation x, its current position on the trajectory is z = eθ(x). A steering strength α
specifies a displacement in this one-dimensional coordinate. We therefore move from z to z +α and
apply the corresponding displacement in activation space:
να(x) = γθ (eθ(x) + α) −γθ (eθ(x)) ,
x′ = x + να(x).
(5)
The steering vector να(x) depends on current position eθ(x). Thus, same coordinate shift can pro-
duce different activation-space displacements at different points along the concept. This allows the
intervention to follow a curved trajectory while retaining a single scalar notion of concept strength.
Linear steering is recovered as a special case. If the trajectory is affine, γθ(z) = c + zw, then
να(x) = [c + (z + α)w] −[c + zw] = αw,
(6)
which is independent of x. Thus, the proposed formulation generalizes fixed-vector steering: a
straight trajectory recovers the standard linear intervention, while a curved trajectory produces
position-dependent steering vectors.

4.4
KAN PARAMETERIZATION
The trajectory and encoder can be parameterized by many nonlinear one-dimensional models. We
use Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) (Liu et al., 2024) because they preserve an explicit
scalar coordinate while allowing the trajectory to be nonlinear.
The encoder eθ : RD →R is implemented as a one-dimensional KAN that maps the activation to
the scalar coordinate z. The decoder γθ : R →RD is implemented as a KAN layer of width [1, D].
In this form, each output coordinate is a learnable univariate function of the same scalar input:
γθ(z) = [ϕ1(z), . . . , ϕD(z)] ,
(7)
with each function parameterized as
ϕd(z) = wb,d, SiLU(z) + ws,d, Splined(z).
(8)
The spline term provides flexibility, while SiLU provides a smooth base function. This allows to
deviate from linearity and represent curved activation-space trajectories observed in Section 3.

Implementation Details.
We generate at 10242, giving P = 4096 image patches of width D =
3072. All fitting is done at block 18, the last dual-stream block, which we identify as the most
influential for these attributes by activation norm and causal patching (Appendix A.2), where we
extract the image-stream delta activation x = Hout −Hin ∈RP ×D. Following (Surkov et al.,
2024), we fit on a random 10% of the patches per seed, using the same patch indices across the K
attributes of a seed so that the pairing in equation 3 is preserved, with 800 training and 100 validation
seeds. Since KAN layers place their spline grid over [−1, 1], we divide every activation by the mean
patch norm before fitting, and set the grid to 5 intervals with order-3 splines. A few patches carry
massive norm but no concept information and would dominate a squared-error fit, so we drop any
patch whose norm exceeds the 0.99 quantile of the per-seed patch norms, taken as the maximum
over the K attributes (Appendix A.2). At inference we add the steering vector at every block from
18 onward (Surkov et al., 2024), rescaled by the mean patch norm to each block’s activation scale.

5.2
RESULTS
We demonstrate results in figure 3. The fixed direction holds source attribute the most and then
switches abruptly to the target. For instance, hair stays straight and then curls, and a young face
becomes elderly. KANSteer advances through the attribute continuously and recovers intermediate
states, giving wavy hair before curly, and a face that passes through middle age. The same pattern
holds across the concepts (Appendix A.5). Where the attributes lie close to a line i.e., human age, the
linear direction still reaches the target and the both methods differ mainly in continuous traversal.
Where the trajectory bends, as for facial expression, hair curliness and butterfly metamorphosis, it
skips intermediate attributes and forces the endpoint at the cost of leaving reference distribution.

5.4
DOES NONLINEAR STEERING BETTER PREDICT THE CONCEPT TRAJECTORY?
The previous sections show that KANSteer outperforms linear steering on reconstruction and per-
ceptual quality. Here we ask a further question that does the nonlinearity in the KAN decoder
actually capture the geometry of the concept trajectory? To realize this, we first measure the non-
linearity of the learned deocder curve γ(z). The decoder function of ϕc(z) is evaluated over the
empirical z-range and decomposed against orthonormal polynomials; the nonlinear share 1 −R2 is
the fraction of centered energy above degree one. Figure 5(Right) demonstrates 1 −R2 as a func-
tion of sub-interval width which shows that over the full attribute range, the nonlinear share grows
monotonically, reaching 25% on hair curliness and 14% on facial expression. This is consistent with
a trajectory that bends gradually over the course. Secondly, we test whether this curvature predicts
where concepts actually land in activation space. For each held-out seed we encode all five attribute
activations through the trained model, compute the predicted displacement γθ (eθ(xj))−γθ (eθ(xi)),
and average over seeds and patches to obtain centroid-level predictions. We then measure the co-
sine similarity between each model’s predicted centroid displacement and the actual displacement.
Across the concepts, KANSteer achieves a mean cosine alignment of 0.55 against 0.37 for the linear
model. The gap is largest on hair curliness (0.88 vs. 0.20), where the trajectory curves the most.
Figure 5(Left) shows per-seed patch-mean activations projected onto first two principal components.
For each held-out seed, KANSteer and the linear model predict per-patch displacements across all
five attributes; averaging these predictions over seeds and patches yields predicted centroids which
shows that KANSteer follow the curved path traced by the actual prompt-generated distribution (cir-
cles), while the linear-direction collapse onto a single direction that diverges from the true trajectory.

CONCLUSION
In this paper, we analyze whether ordered visual concepts in T2I diffusion models can be traversed
along a single direction, and find that in general they cannot. We therefore describe the traversal
as a 1D curve and propose KANSteer, which fits it across all pairwise attribute transitions, keeping
a single scalar coordinate while letting the steering vector depend on where the image already sits
along the concept. Linear steering is the special case of a straight curve, so the difference between

the two measures how far a concept is from a line. That difference is consistent across three FLUX.1
configurations, holds for attributes withheld from fitting, and is largest on the concepts whose tra-
jectories bend the most. Linearity is therefore a local approximation rather than a description of a
concept over its full range, and we hope that treating the traversal as a curve offers a useful starting
point for interpreting and controlling non-linear concepts in generative models.

MOST OF THE CONCEPTS ARE NOT LINEAR IN ACTIVATION SPACE
Figure 1 shows PCA projections for three concepts; Figure 7 extends this to all remaining concepts
in our evaluation set. In every case the intermediate attribute centroids deviate from the straight line
between the two endpoints, confirming that the nonlinearity is not concept-specific but a general
property of how diffusion models encodes ordered attributes.
Figure 6: Residual norm ρk per concept. ρk = 0 means the displacement to attribute k lies exactly
on the endpoint direction; ρk = 1 means it is entirely orthogonal. The dashed line marks the held-out
endpoint average. Intermediate attributes (indices 2-4) are far from the line for every concept.
To quantify how far each intermediate attribute sits from the best possible linear trajectory, we
compute the noise-corrected residual norm ρk (Eq. in Sec. 3). The steering direction v = µK −µ1
is fitted on a held-out split of 700 seeds, and ρk is evaluated on the remaining seeds, so even the
endpoint level is a genuine test rather than an identity. Centroid noise is corrected via a half-split
estimator where the energy of the noise residual is subtracted from both numerator and denominator
so that a perfectly collinear concept returns ρk = 0 at any sample size.
Figure 6 reports ρk for all concepts. At every intermediate attribute (indices 2-4) the residual norm
exceeds 0.4 and typically exceeds 0.7, meaning that at best 30% of the displacement to an inter-
mediate level lies along the endpoint direction. This is the best case for linear steering where no
choice of scaling α can reduce ρk further. The consistently high values across concepts confirm that
the nonlinearity observed in the PCA projections is quantitatively large, which ultimately indicates
towards the need of a non-linear trajectory fitting method.

ABSTRACT 

Unified multimodal diffusion large language models (dLLMs) offer a single archi-tecture for both image generation and multimodal understanding, but their iterative decoding requires tens to hundreds of forward passes. Existing few-step distilla-tion methods largely focus on either image generation or text generation, making it unclear how to compress a fully discrete multimodal dLLM into a single effi-cient student while preserving both generation and understanding. We introduce 

Omni-Diffusion-Distill , a unified two-stage distillation framework that retains strong generation and understanding capabilities while substantially reducing the inference cost of a unified multimodal dLLM. Omni-Diffusion-Distill aligns the distillation of both generation and understanding, for both images and text, in the discrete token space. In the first stage, the student is trained to skip decoding steps by replaying cached teacher trajectories, and in the second stage the student is refined on intermediate states along its own rollouts. We further remedy two sources of degradation in unified distillation with a pairwise collision penalty that reduces repetition under parallel text decoding, and entropy-matched guidance

1 INTRODUCTION 

Building a single model that can both understand and generate across text and images has become an important direction in multimodal learning. Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) provide a natural foundation for this goal: they generate by iteratively decoding masked tokens with a bidi-rectional model (Austin et al., 2021; Sahoo et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2025), while vector-quantized image tokenizers allow images to be represented in the same discrete token space as text (van den Oord et al., 2017; Esser et al., 2021). Recent unified multimodal dLLMs exploit this formulation to support both image generation and multimodal understanding within a single network (Yang et al., 2026; Xin et al., 2025). Their flexibility, however, comes with a substantial inference cost: produc-ing one output can require tens to hundreds of sequential decoding steps. Distillation is the standard way to shorten an iterative sampler, and for a unified model it has to shorten the decoding of images and of text at once. However, existing few-step distillation methods largely treat image generation (Yin et al., 2024b; Song et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025a) and text dLLMs (Kim et al., 2025; Deschenaux & Gulcehre, 2025; Zhang et al., 2026) separately, and directly applying them to a unified model raises two modality-specific challenges, illustrated in Figure 2. First , for text, reducing the number of de-coding steps requires committing more tokens in parallel. Tokens committed in the same decod-ing step are sampled from independent per-position predictions and therefore cannot condition on one another, leading to the curse of parallel decoding (Wu et al., 2025d; Liu et al., 2025a; Kang et al., 2026). This problem becomes increasingly severe under aggressive step reduction and of-ten manifests as repetition in long-form generation. Second , for image generation, removing the unconditional forward pass at inference requires the student to internalize classifier-free guidance (CFG) (Ho & Salimans, 2022) during distillation. CFG sharpens the teacher distribution throughout decoding (Sanchez et al., 2023), and repeatedly matching these sharpened targets progressively low-ers the entropy of the student’s own predictions. This loss of entropy reduces sample diversity (Ho & Salimans, 2022; Yin et al., 2024b). In image editing, it makes the student tend to leave the source im-age unchanged (Brooks et al., 2023). This raises a core question: 

Can we unify the distillation of image and text generation in a multimodal dLLM? 

We answer this question with Omni-Diffusion-Distill , a unified few-step distillation framework for fully discrete multimodal dLLMs. In these models, a single denoiser predicts a categorical distribution over tokens for both text and images. Image and text decoding can therefore share one distillation framework. We train Omni-Diffusion-Distill in two stages. First , an off-policy stage replays cached teacher trajectories from which the student learns to skip decoding steps. Second ,an on-policy stage refines the student on intermediate states along its own rollouts. Importantly, we address the degradation of unified distillation with two remedies: a pairwise collision penalty 

that penalizes the probability that two text positions decoded in parallel commit the same token, and entropy-matched guidance that rescales the CFG target so that its entropy matches that of the conditional prediction. Figure 1 shows the overall achievements of Omni-Diffusion-Distill, and Figure 3 gives an overview of the framework. We summarize our contributions as follows: • Image generation task. At 8 decoding steps, Omni-Diffusion-Distill reaches overall scores of 

0.828 on GenEval and 83 .0 on DPG-Bench, ahead of every distilled baseline on all five text-to-image benchmarks and within 0.8 points of the full-step teacher on DPG-Bench. On Graph-200K it reaches a DINOv2 image similarity of 0.789 against the full-step teacher’s 0.799 .• Multimodal understanding task. At 64 decoding steps, Omni-Diffusion-Distill reaches GPT judge scores of 20 .0 on MM-Vet and 57 .2 on COCO captioning, twice the same-step teacher’s 

28 .4. It leads every distilled baseline on all five open-ended benchmarks. • Efficiency. The student generates an image in 8 steps and answers a question in 64 . On one A100 this is 18 .2× faster than the teacher for text-to-image generation and 21 .2× for understanding. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Unified models differ in how images are represented: a shared discrete vocabulary (Team, 2024; Wang et al., 2024), a separate tokenizer for generation (Wu et al., 2025b), or continuous latents generated with diffusion (Zhou et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025). Closest to us are those that gener-ate with an iterative discrete denoiser over a shared text and image vocabulary (Yang et al., 2026; Xin et al., 2025; Swerdlow et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025). Few-step distillation methods are largely modality-specific: trajectory-based distillation and distribution matching for image diffusion (Sal-imans & Ho, 2022; Song et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b; Yin et al., 2024b;a; Zhou et al., 2024), output re-masking for discrete image generators (Zhu et al., 2025a), objectives that train on student-generated sequences (Agarwal et al., 2024; Ko et al., 2024) and preference objectives (Rafailov et al., 2023) for autoregressive LLMs, and, for text dLLMs, trajectory-based distillation (Kim et al., 2025; Deschenaux & Gulcehre, 2025; Zhang et al., 2026) and distribution matching (Zheng et al., 2026). Unified models have been accelerated by consistency distillation (Xu et al., 2026) and by speculative decoding with a distilled continuous image branch (Lu et al., 2025). Omni-Diffusion-Distill instead performs on-policy refinement on states the student itself produces, and treats both modalities as masked tokens of one model, so one framework serves both. Appendix A expands this discussion. 

3 METHOD 

3.1 BACKGROUND AND PRELIMINARIES 

Why a fully discrete unified model. Omni-Diffusion-Distill leverages a single denoiser that pre-dicts a categorical distribution at every position, for both text and image tokens. At every step, the teacher therefore gives an explicit prediction, and one distillation framework can learn from it di-rectly. Most unified models do not have such a denoiser. Some generate images with a continuous diffusion branch or decoder (You et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2025d; AI et al., 2026). Some use separate parameters for the two modalities (Li et al., 2026d). Others replace masked diffusion with discrete flow matching (Wang et al., 2026a; Luo et al., 2026). Only a few unified models are fully discrete (Yang et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2025; Swerdlow et al., 2025; Xin et al., 2025). Our method is not de-signed around a specific architecture. Figure 3: Overview of Omni-Diffusion-Distill. Stage 1 replays cached teacher trajectories off-policy, and Stage 2 refines the student on its own rollouts. Entropy-matched guidance rescales the image teacher’s entropy, and a pairwise collision penalty reduces text repetition. 

Notation. Text tokens, vector-quantized image tokens and a mask token share one vocabulary. Masked diffusion learns to recover masked output tokens. Decoding starts from a fully masked output. At each step, the model commits some masked positions, replacing their mask tokens with sampled tokens that stay fixed. We call the partially decoded output at decoding step t the state xt,and write M(xt) for its masked positions. At each masked position i, the frozen teacher pϕ and the student qθ predict p iϕ(· | xt) and q iθ (· | xt).

Block-causal attention. The teacher attends bidirectionally over the whole sequence. The student uses a block-causal mask for text outputs and bidirectional attention for images. Every position can attend to the prompt. Text outputs are split into blocks, and each text token attends to its own block and to all earlier blocks. At inference, the student decodes all image tokens in parallel and commits the most confident positions at each step. Text is decoded one block at a time, so the KV cache of finished blocks can be reused exactly. 3.2 MOTIVATION FOR TWO -S TAGE TRAINING 

Stage 1 teaches the student to decode in fewer steps. The student is initialized from the teacher, but it was never trained to decode in so few steps. With fewer steps, it must commit more tokens in each forward pass. Stage 1 teaches this offline from teacher trajectories cached in advance. After Stage 1, the student can decode in fewer steps, but its quality is still below the teacher’s (Table 19). 

Stage 2 reduces the remaining quality gap. Stage 1 trains only on states from the teacher’s trajec-tories. At inference, however, the student continues from states produced by its own earlier steps, so its errors can accumulate. Stage 2 addresses this by training the student on intermediate states of its own rollouts. This stage also addresses the two challenges of repeated text and entropy collapse. 3.3 STAGE 1: O FF -P OLICY TRAJECTORY REPLAY FOR FEW -S TEP DECODING 

Cached teacher trajectory. For each training example, we run the teacher once offline and cache its trajectory. The cache records the step at which each token is committed and a few keyframes along the trajectory. Each keyframe stores the state at that step and the teacher’s predicted distributions at the positions committed since the previous keyframe. On image tasks, we merge guidance into the teacher logits before storing them, ℓcfg = ℓuncond + wtask (ℓcond − ℓuncond ). Here ℓcond and ℓuncond 

are the teacher’s logits at a masked position with and without the prompt, and wtask is the guidance strength of the task. The student therefore learns the guided distribution directly. 4Preprint 

Objective. Stage 1 optimizes the same four terms for every task, 

L1 = λtraj Ltraj + λsc Lsc + λpath Lpath + λgt Lgt , (1) 

where the λ are scalar weights. Each training step samples two keyframes of a cached trajectory. The trajectory term Ltraj is the forward KL divergence between the cached teacher predictions and the student’s predictions on the earlier keyframe, at the positions the teacher commits between the two keyframes. Since the teacher made these predictions at later steps, the student learns to commit in one step what the teacher commits over several. The self-consistency term Lsc uses the student’s prediction at the later keyframe as a fixed target for its prediction at the earlier one, at the positions still masked at the later keyframe. This keeps the student consistent when it skips ahead along the trajectory. The path term Lpath rebuilds an intermediate state at a random step from the cached commit step of each token and trains the student to predict the tokens the teacher finally commits at its masked positions. The ground-truth anchor Lgt is the masked cross-entropy on the training data. This branch trains the student to match the teacher’s image distribution (lines 6–11). The student first predicts on a rollout state xt

(line 6). We sample a clean image ˆx0 from this prediction and re-mask it to obtain the scored state s (line 7). The visible tokens of s are thus generated by the student. The student’s pre-diction on s does not capture the distribution of images it generates, so following distribu-tion matching for diffusion models (Yin et al., 2024b;a; Zhu et al., 2025a), we estimate this distribution with an auxiliary network rψ . The student is updated only at the scored positions 

M, which are masked in both s and xt (line 8). Sampling discrete tokens is not differentiable, so we cannot backpropagate through ˆx0. In-stead, we directly set the gradient on the student’s logits zθ to the difference between the predic-tions of rψ and the teacher on s, at each position in M (line 10). Meanwhile, rψ is trained with cross-entropy on the student’s samples, CE( rψ (· | s), ˆx0), so that it keeps up with the student. 

Entropy-matched guidance. The teacher prediction here is the CFG target softmax( ℓcfg ). It is much sharper than the conditional prediction softmax( ℓcond ) at every step, and its entropy changes little along the trajectory (Figure 10). A student trained to fit it keeps losing entropy over the de-coding steps and ends even sharper than the target (Figure 2 and Table 18). Such a sharp student tends to leave the source image unchanged in editing. We therefore rescale the CFG logits with a temperature ν (line 9). The text branch differs from the image branch in two ways (lines 13–17). First, it matches the teacher at each position instead of over the whole output. The student predicts these per-position distributions directly, so no auxiliary network is needed. Second, its main challenge is repetition under parallel decoding. We take s as the rollout state at a random step (line 13), and M as the positions not yet committed in s (line 14). 

Sequentially conditioned teacher predictions. Running the teacher once on s gives its one-step marginal p iϕ(· | s) at each position. This marginal does not know which tokens the other positions will commit. We instead let the teacher decode the scored positions one at a time (line 15). At each position i, the teacher commits a token, and we record the distribution it used, denoted pseq ,i ϕ . This distribution is conditioned on the tokens the teacher has already committed. The student therefore learns from sequential context, while its own decoding stays parallel. The matching term is the Jensen-Shannon divergence between pseq ,i ϕ and q iθ , averaged over the positions in M (line 16). 

Penalty on repetitions in parallel decoding. L text match is averaged over positions, so it is already minimal when each position matches its own teacher prediction. However, positions committed in the same decoding step are sampled independently, and the matching term does not penalize repeated tokens among them. When a token is plausible at two positions of a block, the student may commit it at both. We call this chance the collision probability. For positions i and j, it equals the inner product ⟨q iθ , q jθ ⟩. All scored positions of a block are predicted from the same state s, and any two of them may be committed in the same decoding step. 5 CONCLUSION 

We present Omni-Diffusion-Distill, a few-step distillation framework for fully discrete unified mul-timodal dLLMs. The student learns to skip decoding steps from cached teacher trajectories and is then refined on its own rollouts, with one on-policy loop for images and text. To address the challenge each modality faces, entropy-matched guidance keeps the image student from becoming overconfident, and a pairwise collision penalty reduces repetition in parallel text decoding. Distilla-tion also makes the text attention block-causal, so understanding reuses its KV cache exactly. The student generates an image in 8 steps and answers a question in 64 , 18 .2× and 21 .2× faster than the teacher, and outperforms all distilled baselines on most benchmarks, even approaching the full-step teacher on several. Our approach is limited to unified dLLMs that represent text and images as discrete tokens, and we validate it only on Lumina-DiMOO. The student still trails the full-step teacher, most on text rendering and detailed captioning. On image editing, it also trails the few-step teacher at 8 steps, although it uses a third of the NFE.

A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

A.1 UNIFIED MULTIMODAL UNDERSTANDING AND GENERATION 

Unified models differ mainly in how they represent and generate images alongside text. Autoregres-sive models generate image tokens with the same transformer that generates text (Team, 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). Janus and Janus-Pro keep this design but use separate visual encoders for understanding and generation (Wu et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2025c). Hybrid designs keep text autoregressive but generate images with continuous diffusion, either inside the same transformer or through a separate decoder or branch (Zhou et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a). A third family generates with an iterative discrete denoiser. One group uses masked diffusion over a shared text and image vocabulary for both understanding and generation. It includes MMaDA and its parallel variant (Yang et al., 2026; Tian et al., 2026), Lumina-DiMOO (Xin et al., 2025), UniDisc (Swerdlow et al., 2025), Muddit (Shi et al., 2025) and Omni-Diffusion (Li et al., 2026a). Lavida-O also denoises VQ image tokens with masked diffusion, but processes them with a separate and smaller generation branch (Li et al., 2026d). Unified LLaDA variants keep a discrete text de-noiser but attach a continuous image decoder (AI et al., 2026; You et al., 2026b). A closely related group replaces masked diffusion with discrete flow matching and keeps the same iterative denoising formulation (Wang et al., 2026a; Luo et al., 2026; Susladkar et al., 2026). D-DiT combines discrete text diffusion with continuous image diffusion in one bidirectional model (Li et al., 2025d). A sep-arate line uses dLLM decoding only for understanding. These models pair a masked diffusion text decoder with a vision encoder and do not generate images (You et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2025b; Yu et al., 2025). A.2 DIFFUSION LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS AND DISCRETE GENERATIVE MODELING 

Discrete diffusion extends denoising to categorical data. Early work defines the forward corruption as a discrete Markov process with uniform, absorbing or structured kernels (Austin et al., 2021; Hoogeboom et al., 2021), and continuous-time formulations give tractable objectives and samplers (Campbell et al., 2022). The absorbing kernel, also called the mask kernel, works best for language. Score entropy (Lou et al., 2024), simplified masked diffusion ELBOs (Sahoo et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024) and reparameterizations that view masked diffusion as modeling clean-data conditionals (Ou et al., 2025) steadily closed the gap to autoregressive models. This line led to dLLMs at LLM scale (Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025a). Another thread interpolates between autoregression and diffusion, with block-wise (Arriola et al., 2025), semi-autoregressive (Han et al., 2023) or position-dependent (Wu et al., 2023) decoding, or with continuous diffusion over embeddings (Li et al., 2022; Gong et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2023). A key property of dLLMs is that they predict an explicit distribution at each position. In continuous diffusion, the distribution of the generator is implicit. A.3 DISTILLATION FOR FEW -S TEP GENERATION 

Few-step distillation has developed along three largely separate lines, for image generators, autore-gressive LLMs and dLLMs. We review them in turn, and then discuss unified models and on-policy distillation. Distilling image generators. Few-step distillation of continuous image diffusion models is well studied. Trajectory-based methods distill several teacher steps into one. Examples include progres-sive distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022), guidance distillation (Meng et al., 2023), consistency and trajectory models (Song et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023), and rectified flow (Liu et al., 2023b; 2024a). Distillation for discrete image generators, which are masked or au-toregressive models over VQ codes, is less studied but growing. Di[M]O distills a masked image generator into a one-step sampler with token-level distribution matching on re-masked outputs. It keeps entropy in the student by randomizing the token initialization (Zhu et al., 2025a), and its soft-embedding variant further improves one-step quality (Zhu et al., 2025b). Other work distills discrete diffusion image generators through dimensional correlations (Hayakawa et al., 2025) or fixed-point iteration (Wang & Tong, 2026). For autoregressive image models, one-step samplers have been obtained with flow matching (Liu et al., 2025b), and knowledge distillation has been applied to vi-sual autoregressive models (Peruzzo et al., 2026). Apart from distillation, other methods speed up masked and autoregressive image sampling with better unmasking schedules (Besnier et al., 2025; You et al., 2025), speculative Jacobi decoding (Teng et al., 2025) or reward-tuned step reduction (Subbaraman et al., 2025). 

Distilling language models. For autoregressive LLMs, classical knowledge distillation matches teacher logits (Hinton et al., 2015). Off-policy matching suffers from exposure bias, because the student’s own generations drift away from the teacher’s. On-policy methods address this by training on sequences generated by the student, as in generalized KD, which supports divergences such as the generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence (Agarwal et al., 2024). DistiLLM combines a skew KL ob-jective with an adaptive off-policy scheme that reuses student-generated sequences (Ko et al., 2024). Another line uses preference objectives such as direct preference optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023), which pushes the model toward preferred responses and away from dispreferred ones. Direct dis-criminative optimization carries this parameterization to visual generative models and contrasts real data with samples from a frozen reference model (Zheng et al., 2025). 

Distilling diffusion large language models. Distillation of dLLMs is recent. For text, consistency and trajectory-based methods distill dLLMs to a few steps (Kim et al., 2025; Deschenaux & Gul-cehre, 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026), and a discrete distribution matching objective adapts Diff-Instruct to dLLMs (Zheng et al., 2026). Few-step dLLMs also make errors because they commit positions independently. Infinite-state masking reduces this error (Yoo et al., 2026), confi-dence thresholds address it at inference (Wu et al., 2025d), and dimensional-correlation distillation targets it during training (Hayakawa et al., 2025). Many other works accelerate dLLM inference 21 Preprint without full distillation. Training-free methods use KV caching and parallel decoding (Wu et al., 2025d), or adaptive sampling and cache eviction (Wei et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2026; Song et al., 2025). Trained methods shorten the schedule through certainty forcing, block diffusion fine-tuning, or distillation into a block-autoregressive hybrid (Chen et al., 2025d; Wu et al., 2025c; Wang et al., 2026b). Each of these methods targets a single modality. 

Distilling unified multimodal models. Closest to us are the few works that distill or accelerate a unified model for understanding and generation. UniCMs (Xu et al., 2026) applies consistency distillation to Show-o on both modalities, but its text side follows an autoregressive parallel decod-ing trace instead of a diffusion process. Hyper-Bagel (Lu et al., 2025) accelerates BAGEL with speculative decoding and a multi-stage image pipeline. The first stage distills classifier-free guid-ance, and the image generator is a continuous diffusion model. Some unified dLLMs build few-step distillation into their generation decoder (AI et al., 2026). Others distill a unified teacher into an understanding-only student (Li et al., 2026b). 

On-policy distillation. On-policy distillation (OPD) trains the student on its own generations in-stead of teacher-forced data (Agarwal et al., 2024; Song & Zheng, 2026; Zhang, 2026). It has developed rapidly for language models. Recent work improves stability by mixing forward and reverse KL according to teacher entropy (Jin et al., 2026), analyzes when and why OPD helps (Li et al., 2026e), and extends OPD to dLLMs (Su et al., 2026). In the last work, a block-causal student is trained on its own rollouts and supervised by the frozen autoregressive model it was con-verted from. Trajectory-based distillation for dLLMs instead supervises the student on states from the teacher’s own rollouts (Zhang et al., 2026). Another thread makes the student its own teacher through a self-reference (Zhao et al., 2026). Li et al. (2026c) distill a student along its own rollouts with a closed-form KL at each step of the sampler trajectory. They cover several continuous image diffusion tasks, each with its own teacher. Existing OPD methods each handle one modality. Omni-Diffusion-Distill differs from these works in two ways. First, both modalities are masked tokens of one model. A single on-policy framework therefore covers both, with a modality-specific state, teacher prediction and matching term. Second, a pairwise collision penalty for parallel text de-coding and entropy-matched guidance for images address the degradation that step reduction causes in each modality.

The collision penalty and entropy-matched guidance in Section 3.4 address the two challenges in Section 1. These are repetition in parallel text decoding and entropy collapse caused by fitting the sharpened CFG target in image distillation. Table 18 measures both directly. 

Text degeneration metrics. We compute degeneration metrics on the outputs of the five open-ended understanding benchmarks and average them across benchmarks. For a generated token se-quence y1, . . . , y n, we report four metrics. • Rep-1 is the fraction of adjacent token pairs with yi = yi−1. It measures repeated phrases instead of repeated tokens. • Loop@5 is the fraction of outputs that contain at least five identical tokens in a row. It captures severe repetition loops. • Distinct-1 is the ratio of unique tokens to all tokens in an output. Higher values mean more diverse outputs.

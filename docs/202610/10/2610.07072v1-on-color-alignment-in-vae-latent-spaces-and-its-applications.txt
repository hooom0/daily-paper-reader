ABSTRACT
Variational autoencoders (VAEs) are a key part of modern text-to-image models,
which generate images within their latent space. VAEs are known to disentangle
the main factors of variation in the data, and color is known to be one of the most
structured of these in natural images: decorrelating it yields one luminance axis
and two opponent-color axes. Color should therefore be expected to emerge as
a distinct factor in the VAE latent space. Yet how these latent spaces represent
color remains largely unexplored. In this work, we show that the VAEs of text-
to-image models share a color subspace aligned with brightness and opponent-
colors. Through a linear approximation of the encoder and targeted latent steering,
we find this subspace consistently across a broad range of VAEs, from SD1.5 to
FLUX.2 and Z-Image. Building on this characterization, we propose three appli-
cations: ColorTuning , which achieves state-of-the-art in precise numerical color
generation on the fine-grained CSS3/X11 system of GenColorBench, saturation
control, to adjust the global chromatic intensity, and color transfer, to change
the palette to match a reference. The code and models are publicly available at
https://julian075.github.io/Color_Subspace/

INTRODUCTION
Natural images are far from random: they occupy a small, highly structured subset of the space of all
possible pixel arrays, with strong spatial and spectral correlations (Simoncelli & Olshausen, 2001).
Efficient coding theory holds that a visual system should exploit this redundancy by mapping its in-
puts to a compact, decorrelated representation (Barlow, 1961; Malo, 2020). For color, this principle
gives a remarkably consistent answer: decorrelating the cone responses to natural scenes yields one
achromatic luminance axis and two chromatic opponent axes, namely red–green and blue–yellow
(Buchsbaum & Gottschalk, 1983; Ruderman et al., 1998). These axes closely match the cardinal
directions of early human color vision (Derrington et al., 1984) and are mirrored in perceptual color
spaces such as CIELAB. Luminance and opponent colors are therefore not an arbitrary convention
but a property of natural images, one that any efficient encoder should be expected to recover.
In fact, the evidence suggests that artificial systems trained on natural images develop this color
representation too. Unsupervised methods related to independent component analysis, applied to
color image patches, learn separate achromatic and color-opponent features (Hoyer & Hyv¨arinen,
2000; Gutmann et al., 2014). Deep networks behave similarly: the first-layer of CNNs trained
for classification display achromatic and color-opponent units (Krizhevsky et al., 2012; Flachot &
Gegenfurtner, 2018), and CNNs trained for low-level vision tasks show human-like color axes and
behavior, including susceptibility to color illusions (Gomez-Villa et al., 2020), and distinctive spatial
bandwidth for color channels (Li et al., 2022). Finally, vision foundation models, trained on natural
images at a far larger scale, may therefore retain this color structure, and recent evidence supports
this expectation (Akbarinia, 2025; Wickramanayaka & Oizumi, 2025; Gomez-Villa et al., 2025).
Most modern text-to-image models are latent generators: rather than operating on pixels, they gener-
ate within the representation learned by a VAE and decode the result back to image space (Rombach
et al., 2022). VAEs are known to organize this representation around the factors of variation in the
data: they tend to disentangle generative attributes (Higgins et al., 2017; Burgess et al., 2018) and to
align their latent axes with the principal directions of the data (Rolinek et al., 2019; Zietlow et al.,
2021). Yet, although color is one of the most thoroughly studied and highly structured properties of
natural images, how it is represented in these latent spaces has only recently begun to be explored
(Arias et al., 2025; Pach et al., 2026). Meanwhile, text-to-image models still fail to bind colors to
objects, render named colors faithfully, or follow numerical specifications such as HEX or RGB
codes (Butt et al., 2026a). The color structure may already be present in the latent space; but the
generators simply do not use it.
Recent studies have begun to probe color in these latent spaces with uniform images, recovering
opponent hue axes in the Stable Diffusion VAE (Arias et al., 2025) and an HSL-like subspace in
the FLUX VAE (Pach et al., 2026). Such inputs reveal where colors are mapped, but not how
color is encoded alongside the spatial content of real images. We instead analyze the response to
natural images and show that the VAEs of modern text-to-image models share a color subspace
aligned with brightness and opponent-color directions (Fig. 1). On the encoder side, the singular
vectors of a linear approximation of the encoder are achromatic and opponent-color patterns; on
the decoder side, traversing individual latent channels shifts the color of the decoded image. We
find this structure consistently in the VAEs of SD1.5, SDXL, SD3, FLUX, and FLUX.2, with 4 to
32 latent channels, and show that it is only partially disentangled from image structure. Building
on it, we propose ColorTuning , which steers generation to follow numerical color specifications
and achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on the CSS3/X11 system of GenColorBench (81.20% vs.
51.96%), and show that the same color basis also enables saturation control and color transfer.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We empirically show that the VAEs of modern text-to-image models share a color subspace
aligned with brightness and nonlinear opponent-color axes, consistent across model families
and latent sizes from 4 to 32 channels, and grounded on natural image statistics.
• We characterize this subspace through a linear approximation of the encoder and targeted
latent steering, and quantify its partial entanglement with image structure.
• We propose a number of applications based on our characterization: ColorTuning achieves
state-of-the-art in precise numerical color generation on the fine-grained CSS3/X11 system of

RELATED WORK
What VAEs disentangle. Unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without inductive biases
on both model and data (Locatello et al., 2019), yet VAEs often disentangle in practice (Higgins
et al., 2017; Burgess et al., 2018). Rolinek et al. (2019) traced this to the diagonal posterior, which
aligns the latent axes with the local principal directions of the data, and Zietlow et al. (2021) showed
that this yields disentanglement only when the variance structure of the data matches its generative
factors. This line of work has been evaluated almost exclusively on synthetic benchmarks. We test
it on color, whose natural-image statistics decorrelate into one luminance and two opponent axes
(Buchsbaum & Gottschalk, 1983; Ruderman et al., 1998).
Color in vision foundation models. The color representations of deep networks have been com-
pared with human perception, with limited alignment (Wickramanayaka & Oizumi, 2025; Ehab
et al., 2026). For text-to-image VAEs, Arias et al. (2025) recovered opponent hue axes in the Stable
Diffusion VAE and Pach et al. (2026) an HSL-like subspace in the FLUX VAE, both by probing
with uniformly colored patches. We instead analyze the response to natural images, across five
VAEs with 4 to 32 latent channels.
Precise color control. Existing methods either act on the text conditioning, by learning color to-
kens (Butt et al., 2024) or grounding HEX codes and RGB values in a CIELAB-aligned ColorBook
(NumColor; Butt et al., 2026b), or intervene at inference time, either by rewiring cross-attention
(ColorWave; Laria et al., 2026) or by optimizing the latents (Shomer-Chai et al., 2026). Closest
to ours, Pach et al. (2026) recolor objects through an HSL mapping interpolated from eight anchor
colors, but this approch is only specifical for FLUX. ColorTuning instead recovers the color ba-
sis of any pretrained VAE and learns the displacement toward the target from measured CIELAB
outcomes. An extended discussion is given in Appendix A.2.
2.1
BACKGROUND
2.1.1
VARIATIONAL AUTOENCODERS
Variational autoencoders (VAEs) (Kingma & Welling, 2014; Rezende et al., 2014) learn a proba-
bilistic mapping between images x ∈Rdx and a lower-dimensional latent representation z ∈Rdz.
An encoder E parameterizes an approximate posterior qϕ(z|x), typically a Gaussian with diagonal
covariance, and a decoder D parameterizes the likelihood pθ(x|z). Both are trained jointly by max-
imizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), which in its β-weighted form (Higgins et al., 2017) is
defined by :
Lβ = Eqϕ(z|x) [log pθ(x|z)] −β DKL (qϕ(z|x) ∥p(z)) ,
(1)
where the first term encourages faithful reconstruction and the second regularizes the posterior to-
ward an isotropic Gaussian prior p(z) = N(0, I) via the Kullback-Leibler divergence (DKL). Set-
ting β > 1 strengthens this regularization and is known to promote disentangled latent factors
(Higgins et al., 2017; Burgess et al., 2018).
The VAEs of modern latent generative models operate in the opposite regime. Following Rombach
et al. (2022), they are trained with β ≪1 (on the order of 10−6), together with perceptual and
adversarial losses (Esser et al., 2021), which prioritizes reconstruction quality and keeps the latent
space only mildly regularized. These VAEs are trained once and then kept frozen. The encoder
compresses an image of size H × W × 3 into a spatial latent of size H
f × W
f × C, where f is
the spatial downsampling factor and C is the number of latent channels, and the generator operates
entirely in this latent space.

COLOR IN AUTOENCODER LATENT SPACE
We study how the latent spaces of autoencoders represent color in two steps. We first show em-
pirically that color is directly readable from the raw latent channels: traversing a single channel
moves the decoded image along a consistent color direction, within a limited range before its spatial
content also changes (Sec. 3.1). We then explain this behavior from the input side through a linear
approximation of the encoder, whose singular vectors are opponent-colored patterns (Sec. 3.2).
3.1
LATENT CHANNELS ENCODE COLOR
In classical VAEs, individual latent coordinates often capture a single factor of variation of the data:
traversing one coordinate while keeping the others fixed changes, for example, only the azimuth of
a face (Higgins et al., 2017; Rolinek et al., 2019). We apply this same probe, a latent traversal, to
the raw latent space of autoencoders, focusing on color.
Since these latents are spatial, we refer to each latent dimension as a channel, and traverse channel
c by adding a constant offset δ at every spatial position, zc →zc + δ, before decoding. As shown in
Fig. 2, modifying δ progressively shifts the color of the decoded image along a consistent direction.
We observe this across deterministic autoencoders (AEs), VAEs, and VQ-VAEs with 3, 4, 8, and 32
channels trained from scratch, as well as in the pretrained VAEs of SD1.5, SDXL, SD3, FLUX, and
FLUX.2 (See the Fig. 2 and Appendix A.3). Color is therefore directly readable from the raw latent
channels, without any learned probe.
Color is, however, spatio-chromatically entangled: the same channels that carry color also carry
spatial information. For small offsets, traversing the channel modifies only the color of the decoded
image; but beyond a certain magnitude, it also begins to alter its spatial content. This behavior is
visible in the edges and textures in the outermost columns of Fig. 2 (see Appendix A.3 for more
examples). We quantify this in Fig. 3 as a function of δ. We measure color change with ∆E00
(CIEDE2000) relative to the unperturbed reconstruction, and spatial change with MS-SSIM com-
puted on grayscale images using only its contrast and structure terms, so that the intended color and
brightness shifts are not penalized. As |δ| increases, ∆E00 grows steadily, while MS-SSIM stays
close to 1 for small offsets and then drops. Color can therefore be edited by moving along the latent
channels, but only within a limited range before the edit starts to change the spatial content of the
image.
In general, we find that individual channels do not uniquely map one-to-one onto specific colors: a
single channel typically moves the image along a mixture of them, and several channels can move it
in similar directions (e.g., two different channels may both turn the image green). A notable excep-
tion arises when there are only 3 latent channels. In this case, both the VAE and the AE align closely
with the opponent color space: one channel controls brightness, and the other two control the oppo-
nent axes (see Appendix A.3). For the VAE, this is the behavior predicted by Rolinek et al. (2019).
For the AE, it is not: its objective has no mechanism that favors a particular orientation (Rolinek
et al., 2019). However, opponent color directions do appear in plain autoencoders (Gomez-Villa
et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022), and, as shown below, they consistently emerge in modern autoencoders.

3.2
LINEAR APPROXIMATION OF THE VAE
The traversals in section 3.1 show that color is readable from the latent channels, but not why. To
answer this we turn to the input side and ask which image patterns the encoder is most sensitive
to. Because a VAE is trained to reconstruct its input, its response stays close to the identity and is
well described to first order, which lets us replace the encoder by a matrix whose singular vectors
are precisely those patterns, ordered by how strongly they drive the latent. If the color structure
observed above originates in the encoder, it should appear here as achromatic and opponent-color
patterns among the leading singular vectors.
Let X ∈Rdx×N represent a matrix containing N input images. Let Z ∈Rdz×N represent their
corresponding low-dimensional latent representations, obtained via the VAE’s encoder, Z = E(X).
Subsequently, let ˆX = D(Z) represent the reconstructed output images generated by the VAE’s
decoder. The application of the model (encoder + decoder) is denoted here as: ˆx = m(x).
Following Gomez-Villa et al. (2020) and Li et al. (2022), we analyze the behavior of the network
through the linear approximation of its response. Considering any image x as a deviation from the
average image (flat gray image, 0), the first-order approximation of the autoencoder is:
ˆx = m(x) ≈m(0) + ∇x m(0) · x = Mvae · x
(3)
where the response to the average image is re-calibrated as zero, and Mvae ∈Rdx×dx is thus
the Jacobian of the network response w.r.t. the input at 0. For VAE architectures, this assump-
tion is justified by the ELBO objective, where the expected log-likelihood reconstruction term,
Eqϕ(z|x)[log pθ(x|z)], forces the latent space to preserve sufficient information for data recovery.

APPLICATIONS
Sec. 3 shows that VAE latents carry a color structure inherited from natural image statistics, but
spread across correlated channels and only partially disentangled from spatial content. We therefore
recover an explicit color basis for each VAE, computed once (Sec. 4.1), and use it for three appli-
cations in T2I generation, handling the residual entanglement at inference time: numerical color
precision (Sec. 4.2), saturation control, and color transfer (Sec. 4.3).

4.2
NUMERICAL COLOR PRECISION
Numerical colour precision refers to the ability to generate an object with the exact RGB value or
HEX code specified in the text prompt. State-of-the-art T2I models still struggle with this task,
exhibiting a range of failure cases (see Fig.15 and Butt et al. (2026a;b)). In this subsection, we
present ColorTuning , which exploits the discovered directions to steer the generation towards the
exact colour specified in the user prompt.
4.2.1
STEERING THE GENERATOR
Our approach is based on two key properties of the discovered color directions. First, diffusion
and flow-matching models resolve low spatial frequencies first Biroli et al. (2024): the coarse color
of a region is settled well before sampling ends, so decoding the predicted clean latent ˆz0 (Eq. 2 in
Appendix 2.1) at an intermediate step already reveals the color the object will have in the final image.
Second, the characterization of Sec. 4.1 gives the directions along which that color can be moved.
Measuring the current color and applying the corresponding displacement over the remaining steps
is therefore enough to reach the target (see Fig. 16 in Appendix A.7).
Training. The basis of Sec. 4.1 fixes the directions, but not the magnitude: how far a given dis-
placement moves the object in CIELAB depends on the color it starts from, since the decoder is
nonlinear. We therefore learn this relation empirically. For a set of prompts containing a color
name, we generate one image without intervention and, for each displacement m ∈M along the
basis, a second image with the perturbation applied to the target object. Measuring the color of the
object in both images gives a triplet: the color before the perturbation, the color it reached, and the
displacement that produced it. On this data we train a small MLP that takes an initial and a target
color, Ci = (Li, ai, bi) and Ct = (Lt, at, bt), and predicts the displacement (m1, m2, m3) along
the basis that carries Ci to Ct (Alg. 1).

measure its mean color in CIELAB. The MLP maps this color and the target to a displacement along
the color basis, which we add to the latent inside the object mask at each remaining step, with a
weight that decays linearly to zero so that the final steps re-integrate the perturbation into the image
structure (Alg. 2; hyperparameters in Appendix A.7).
4.2.2
RESULTS OF COLORTUNING
Setup. We evaluate ColorTuning on GenColorBench (Butt et al., 2026a), a benchmark for precise
color generation in which prompts specify object colors as HEX codes and RGB values. We report
accuracy on its two color systems, ISCC-L3 and CSS3/X11, under both formats. We apply Col-
orTuning to six generators covering the three latent sizes analyzed in Sec. 3: SDXL (4 channels),
SD3, SD3.5M, FLUX and Z-Image (16), and FLUX.2 (32). We compare against the vanilla models
and against two color-control methods, each on the backbones for which it is available: ColorWave
on SDXL and NumColor on SD3, SD3.5M and FLUX.

4.3
FURTHER COLOR APPLICATIONS DURING GENERATION
The same characterization naturally extends to other forms of color control at generation time, with-
out requiring any modification to the procedure introduced in Sec. 4.1. Fig. 6 (top) illustrates satu-
ration control: by traversing the corresponding latent directions, our method can continuously mod-
ulate the saturation of the generated image (in the figure from 100% to 50% at 10% intervals). Such
saturation-aware generation could facilitate the synthesis of images within narrower color gamuts,
potentially reducing the need for post-processing gamut mapping techniques (Moroviˇc, 2008).
Fig. 6 (bottom) shows a second application: color transfer during generation. In this setting, the input
consists of both a text prompt and a reference image whose color distribution should be reproduced.
Our method leverages the discovered latent directions to match the color distribution of the reference
image while simultaneously generating the content specified by the prompt. Full details of both
applications are given in Appendix A.8.

CONCLUSIONS
We have shown that color structure is already present in the raw latents of autoencoders trained on
natural images: in AEs, VAEs, and VQ-VAEs alike, traversing a single latent channel shifts the
color of the decoded image. This observation can be explained by a linear approximation of the
encoder, whose leading singular vectors correspond to the achromatic and opponent-color patterns
predicted by natural image statistics. Recovering these axes as an explicit basis turns the observation
into a tool for color control during generation. Our main use of it is ColorTuning , which achieves
9


Preprint
state-of-the-art accuracy in numerical color generation on the fine-grained CSS3/X11 system of
GenColorBench; saturation control and color transfer further show, qualitatively, that the same basis
supports other forms of color control.
Limitations.
Existing methods for encouraging precise color synthesis in generative models typ-
ically rely on learning new concepts that enforce specific color relationships. In contrast, our ap-
proach leverages the inherent color alignment already present in the VAE latent space to dynamically
guide the generation process. However, this strategy remains sensitive to large steering magnitudes.
As shown in our analysis, the extracted latent color channels are not fully disentangled from spatial
and structural features. Consequently, when the MLP predicts a large displacement from the source
color toward the target one, the resulting latent perturbation affects not only color but also image
content, leading to structural distortions in the final output.

A.2
RELATED WORK
A.2.1
REPRESENTATION LEARNING AND DISENTANGLEMENT IN VAES
Whether a VAE recovers the true factors of variation in its data is a question of identifiability. Lo-
catello et al. (2019) showed that unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without inductive bi-
ases on both model and data, and identifiability has since been proven only under additional assump-
tions such as auxiliary variables (Khemakhem et al., 2020). Yet VAEs often disentangle in practice
(Higgins et al., 2017; Burgess et al., 2018). Rolinek et al. (2019) traced this to the diagonal posterior,
which makes the decoder Jacobian locally orthogonal and aligns the latent axes with the local prin-
cipal directions of the data. Relatedly, Reizinger et al. (2022) connected the ELBO to independent
mechanism analysis in the near-deterministic regime. Zietlow et al. (2021) further showed that this
bias yields disentanglement only when the variance structure of the dataset matches its generative
factors. What a VAE disentangles is therefore dictated by the principal directions of its training data.
Color is a natural test of this view: it is one of the most structured properties of natural images, whose
statistics decorrelate into one luminance and two opponent-color axes (Buchsbaum & Gottschalk,
1983; Ruderman et al., 1998). Yet this line of work has been evaluated almost exclusively on syn-
thetic benchmarks, where color appears at most as a discrete hue label, and has not examined how
VAEs trained on natural images represent it. Only recently have studies begun to probe color in
the latent spaces of text-to-image VAEs (Arias et al., 2025; Pach et al., 2026), and none relates the
structure they find to the statistics of the training data. We instead analyze the encoder’s response
to natural images and show that, across model families and latent sizes, these latents share a color
subspace aligned with the luminance and opponent axes that natural image statistics would predict.
A.2.2
COLOR REPRESENTATIONS IN VISION FOUNDATION MODELS
Recent work has begun to characterize how color is encoded in the representations of vision founda-
tion models. Wickramanayaka & Oizumi (2025) compared the internal color representations of 16
deep networks with human similarity judgments over 93 colors, and found that only CLIP models
reached fine-grained structural agreement with human perception. Ehab et al. (2026) probed more
than 50 vision encoders, including the SD3 VAE, and compared their chromatic discrimination
thresholds with human discrimination ellipses, finding weak alignment across all model families.
For generative models, Arias et al. (2025) encoded uniformly colored patches with the Stable Diffu-
sion VAE and applied PCA to their spatially averaged latents, recovering an intensity axis and two
opponent hue axes. When probing grayscale synthetic patterns separately, they found that one of
these chromatic channels also carries shape information. More recently, Pach et al. (2026) identified
an HSL-like color subspace in the VAEs of FLUX, also by probing them with uniformly colored
images, and used it to steer the color of generated objects. These studies characterize color through
flat stimuli, which reveal where colors are mapped but not how color is encoded alongside the spa-
tial content of real images. Moreover, each examines only one or two VAEs, leaving open whether
the structure they find is specific to a particular model or shared across text-to-image VAEs with
different architectures and latent sizes.
A.2.3
PRECISE COLOR CONTROL IN TEXT-BASED IMAGE MODELS
Methods for precise color control in text-to-image models intervene at two points of the generative
pipeline. A first family acts on the text conditioning. ColorPeel (Butt et al., 2024) learns color
prompt tokens from synthetic stimuli that disentangle color from shape, but covers only a few colors
per training session and is only able to interpolate between nearby colors. Targeting numerical
specifications, NumColor (Butt et al., 2026b) addresses the fragmentation of hex and RGB codes
into meaningless subword tokens by aggregating them into a single unit and grounding it with a
ColorBook of learnable embeddings aligned with CIELAB.
14


Preprint
A second family intervenes at inference without retraining the backbone, either by rewiring the
cross-attention of IP-Adapter to bind a color descriptor to a patch of the target color (Laria et al.,
2026), or by optimizing the latents with attention and color losses (Shomer-Chai et al., 2026). Clos-
est to our work, Pach et al. (2026) recolor objects by shifting latents within the color subspace
discussed above, through a mapping to HSL interpolated from eight anchor colors. Their interpreta-
tion is derived for FLUX alone, from flat color patches. We instead build on a general property: the
latents of VAEs trained on natural images align with luminance and opponent-color axes, a structure
inherited from natural image statistics (Sec. 3). This lets us design a characterization procedure that
recovers these directions in any pretrained VAE, along which ColorTuning learns the displacement
toward the target from measured CIELAB outcomes rather than from a fixed geometric mapping.

(Li(x, y), ai(x, y), bi(x, y)) at each spatial location (x, y) within the target region (or across the

pulls the generation into the restricted gamut within the latent space (see Figure 17).
Color Transfer. For color transfer, the target color distribution is specified either by a discrete color
palette (e.g., a set of hex values) or by an exemplar reference image. When using an image, we
first extract its principal colors via k-means clustering in CIELAB, enforcing a minimum perceptual
distance in CIEDE2000 to obtain differentiated dominant, focal, and secondary accent tones. To
initialize the trajectory in the appropriate chromatic basin, we take the most chromatic color, locate
its nearest entry in ISCC-NBS Level 2, and inject this proxy name into the text prompt for the main
subject. At gate step s, we decode the predicted clean latent ˆz0 and partition the scene into spatial
regions: the focal object segmented with SAM 3, secondary entities, and the background (which can
be tonally partitioned into highlight and shadow zones). We assign each region to its corresponding
reference color. The pretrained MLP then evaluates each pair of measured and target CIELAB colors
independently, predicting a displacement (m1, m2, m3) for each region along the color basis. See
qualitative results in Figure 18.

ABSTRACT
Recent work on language-model adaptation has shown that single models can
obtain informative training signals by evaluating their behavior in demonstration-
or feedback-augmented contexts, with the help of a teacher network, which is
driven by the students’ learned parameters. Inspired by this internal-reference
principle, we investigate how diffusion models can identify self-referenced training
signals without external demonstrations or teacher networks. We introduce SALD,
a self-referenced training framework that evaluates each image-caption pair at
two noise levels using the same model. The easier, lower-noise path is evaluated
without gradient tracking to provide a reference, while the harder, higher-noise
path provides the training gradient. Rather than directly distilling the easy-path
prediction, SALD uses the difference between two path errors to adapt the hard-
path objective. The proposed Advantage-Guided Diffusion (AGD) converts this
relative error into a differentiable sample-level weight. Temporal Advantage
Memory (TAM) accumulates relative difficulty across training and adapts the
future gap between the two noise levels. Spectral Advantage Decomposition (SAD)
further compares the residual power spectra of the two paths and constructs a
differentiable, frequency-derived latent-element weight. All components share a
single set of model parameters, requiring neither an external teacher network nor
additional trainable parameters during training or inference, and no modification to
the inference procedure. Experiments across multiple architectures and datasets
demonstrate consistent improvements in generation quality, while component-wise
ablations quantify the contributions of the proposed components.

INTRODUCTION
Diffusion Ho et al. (2020); Song et al. (2020) and flow-matching Lipman et al. (2022); Albergo et al.
(2025) based generative models have become standard backbones for high-fidelity image synthesis,
with Diffusion Transformers further improving scalability and generation quality Peebles & Xie
(2023). In parallel, recent work on language-model adaptation has shown that a model can obtain

richer online training signals by evaluating its behavior under additional information. Self-Distillation
Fine-Tuning conditions a self-teacher on expert demonstrations, whereas Self-Distillation Policy
Optimization conditions a self-teacher on feedback obtained from the model’s own attempts Shenfeld
et al. (2026); Hübotter et al. (2026). These approaches show that useful supervision can be constructed
without relying on a stronger external teacher. Transferring this principle to diffusion training is not
straightforward: diffusion models do not naturally receive textual demonstrations or retrospective
feedback, and their training states are sampled from a prescribed corruption process rather than
from autoregressive policy rollouts. Moreover, standard diffusion objectives still average gradients
uniformly across training pairs and latent elements, although their difficulty varies substantially across
images, captions, noise levels, and visual details Vincent (2011); Choi et al. (2022). Neural networks
also exhibit spectral bias, learning coarse low-frequency structure before fine high-frequency detail
Rahaman et al. (2019); Wang & Pehlevan (2025a). Consequently, uniform training can continue
to emphasize already learned signals while underweighting examples and visual components that
remain difficult.
Can a diffusion model construct its own easier reference and use it to adapt training across
harder examples, prompts, noise levels, and visual frequencies, without teachers,
demonstrations, or feedback datasets?
A unified answer remains missing because existing methods address only parts of this problem. Noise-
level weighting Karras et al. (2022); Esser et al. (2024); Hang et al. (2025) adjusts training according
to timestep or signal-to-noise ratio, but normally applies the same rule to all examples evaluated at
a given noise level. Noise selection methods show that different noise instances provide unequal
training signals Qi et al. (2024a), but they do not maintain a persistent estimate of how the difficulty
of an individual image-caption pair changes throughout training. Curriculum learning Bengio
et al. (2009) orders examples by difficulty, but typically depends on a fixed or externally defined
curriculum and are not adaptive noise selection. Distributionally robust optimization emphasizes
difficult subpopulations Sagawa et al. (2019), whereas frequency-aware objectives address spectral
imbalance; neither directly combines the sample-level, temporal, and spectral difficulty in diffusion
training. Likewise, demonstration- or feedback-conditioned language-model self-distillation cannot
be transferred directly because diffusion training provides neither an in-context demonstration, nor
a feedback-conditioned output distribution to distill Shenfeld et al. (2026); Hübotter et al. (2026).
These limitations motivate a self-referenced diffusion objective in which an easier view of the same
training pair provides a relative difficulty signal rather than a teacher prediction target.
We introduce SALD, which evaluates every image-caption pair at two noise levels using one shared
model: an easier low-noise path and a harder high-noise path. The easy path is evaluated without
gradient tracking and serves as an internal reference, while model parameters are updated through
the hard path. SALD does not directly distill the easy-path prediction into the hard path. Instead,
it measures the increase in weighted prediction error from the easy path to the hard path and uses
this relative signal to adapt the hard-path objective. Advantage-Guided Diffusion (AGD) converts
the scalar error difference into a normalized, differentiable sample weight, emphasizing training
pairs whose error grows under the harder condition. Temporal Advantage Memory (TAM) maintains
an exponential moving average of relative difficulty for every training pair and uses it to adapt
future noise-level gaps, forming a continuous per-example curriculum Bengio et al. (2009). Spectral
Advantage Decomposition (SAD) compares the residual power spectra of the two paths and constructs
a differentiable, frequency-derived latent-element weight, providing a finer training signal related to
spectral bias Rahaman et al. (2019); Wang & Pehlevan (2025a). All components reuse the same set
of model parameters and require neither a teacher network nor additional trainable model parameters
during training or inference. The method introduces no change to the inference procedure. Our
contributions are summarized as follows:
• We introduce AGD, a self-referenced weighting mechanism that estimates sample importance from
the relative error between hard and easy noise paths, without any teachers, demonstration datasets,
or architectural changes.
• We introduce TAM and SAD to extend relative difficulty across training history and spectral
structure, enabling an adaptive per-example noise curriculum and frequency-derived, latent-element
weighting.
• We characterize the optimization behavior of the resulting differentiable weighting objective and
evaluate SALD across multiple architectures and datasets. The experiments demonstrate consistent

PROPOSED APPROACH
The same diffusion model evaluates each image-caption pair at two noise levels. The lower-noise
evaluation forms an easy path, which is computed without gradient tracking and serves as an internal
reference. The higher-noise evaluation forms a hard path, through which the model parameters
are updated. By comparing these paths, SALD addresses three questions: (i) Which examples are
currently difficult? (ii) How large should the easy-to-hard noise gap be for each example? and (iii)
At which frequencies does the hard-path residual remain large? Advantage-Guided Diffusion (AGD),
Temporal Advantage Memory (TAM), and Spectral Advantage Decomposition (SAD) address these
questions, respectively. All components share the same model parameters; therefore, SALD requires
neither an external teacher nor an additional trainable model during training or inference.

RELATED WORK
Denoising diffusion models Ho et al. (2020) and score-based generative models Song et al. (2020)
established iterative denoising as a powerful paradigm for image synthesis. Flow matching Lipman
et al. (2022); Albergo et al. (2025) provides a continuous-time alternative by learning velocity fields
along interpolation paths. Recent transformer-based generators, including DiT Peebles & Xie (2023),
rectified-flow transformers Esser et al. (2024), SANA Xie et al. (2024), PixArt-α Chen et al. (2023),
and PixArt-Σ Chen et al. (2024), further improve scalability and high-resolution generation. However,
most objectives still treat samples, timesteps, and spatial locations uniformly. Several works improve
training by reallocating the gradient budget across noise levels. EDM Karras et al. (2022) studies
principled noise schedules and σ-weighting, while P2 Choi et al. (2022) emphasizes perceptually
important SNR regions. Improved noise schedules Hang et al. (2025) and noise-instance analysis Qi
et al. (2024a) further show that different noise levels and noise samples provide unequal training
signals. These approaches define global timestep-dependent weights, whereas AGD computes
per-sample relative difficulty from the loss gap between low- and high-noise paths.
Our work is also related to curriculum learning Bengio et al. (2009), self-paced learning Kumar
et al. (2010), dynamic instance hardness Chang et al. (2020), adaptive curricula Kong et al. (2021),
diffusion curricula Liang et al. (2025), and adaptive point-weighting Li et al. (2025). Hard-example
mining Felzenszwalb et al. (2010); Shrivastava et al. (2016) and DRO Sagawa et al. (2020); Feoktistov
et al. (2025); Ma et al. (2024) similarly emphasize difficult examples, while meta-hard mining Mu
et al. (2025) and coreset selection Killamsetty et al. (2021) select informative samples. Unlike these
methods, TAM stores an EMA of relative advantage and uses it to adapt the timestep gap for each
sample. Finally, SAD connects to spectral bias studies Rahaman et al. (2019); Xu et al. (2020); Wang
& Pehlevan (2025b) and frequency-aware diffusion methods Benita et al. (2025); Si et al. (2024);
Jiralerspong et al. (2025); Esteves & Makadia (2026); Liu et al. (2025). Instead of modifying the
architecture, sampler, or noising process, SAD uses contrastive spectral residuals to construct spatial
importance weights for loss reweighting. Recent work uses feedback-conditioned output distribution
to distill Shenfeld et al. (2026); Hübotter et al. (2026) from teacher to student in the language domain
but requires demonstration example and EMA based teacher. The proposed model can be seen as
distillation from easy to hard sample, however it does not requires extra copy of the model parameters.

CONCLUSION
We presented SALD, a unified training framework for flow-based diffusion models that reallocates gra-
dient budget across samples, noise levels, and frequency components. AGD estimates relative sample
difficulty by contrasting losses between low and high-noise states, producing normalized within-batch
9


Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027
importance weights. TAM extends this signal across the training set through persistent per-sample
memory, enabling an adaptive curriculum over timestep gaps. SAD further refines optimization
by converting contrastive spectral residuals into frequency-derived latent weights, emphasizing fre-
quency patterns that remain under-learned. Since only the easy-path reference is detached, SALD
preserves the original prediction target while improving where and how training effort is applied.
In general, SALD offers a simple, architecture-agnostic approach for improving diffusion training
without modifying the model, sampler, or inference procedure. Extensive experiments across diverse
datasets and architectures demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, while theoretical
analysis supports the empirical findings.

APPENDIX
A
WHY IS UNIFORM TRAINING SUBOPTIMAL?
The standard flow-matching loss used in Section 2.1 averages the losses of all training samples and
all elements of their latent residuals. Apart from the time-dependent factor w(t), it assigns the same
explicit coefficient to every sample and latent element. This does not mean that all samples produce
equal gradients; samples with larger residuals can naturally produce larger gradient magnitudes.
Rather, the objective has no explicit mechanism for adapting a sample’s importance according to its
current difficulty or for distinguishing the frequency content of its remaining residual. This uniform
treatment leads to two limitations that can become more important as training progresses, especially
during domain-specific fine-tuning.
The first limitation is at the sample level. Not all images are equally difficult for the current model. A
clean portrait against a plain background may be learned early, after which its loss becomes small and
further updates provide limited new information. Meanwhile, a cluttered street scene with reflections,
thin text, and overlapping objects may remain difficult for much longer. Under uniform weighting,
both samples retain the same explicit coefficient in the mini-batch objective, even though reallocating
more emphasis to the difficult sample may be more useful. Prior work on hard-example mining and
diffusion-noise selection similarly shows that examples and noise realizations can provide unequal
training value Shrivastava et al. (2016); Qi et al. (2024b). Consequently, uniform weighting can
spend part of the finite optimization budget on already well-learned samples while under-emphasizing
examples that remain difficult.
The second limitation concerns the frequency content of the residual within each image. Deep
networks are known to learn low-frequency content faster than high-frequency content, a behavior
referred to as spectral bias or the frequency principle Rahaman et al. (2019); Xu et al. (2020). Related
frequency-dependent behavior has also been studied in diffusion models Yang et al. (2023); Si et al.
(2024); Wang & Pehlevan (2025a). Smooth structures such as skies and walls may be reconstructed
earlier, whereas fine details such as hair strands, feather textures, specular highlights, and small text
can lag behind. However, the standard mean-squared error collapses the full latent residual into a
single scalar and does not explicitly indicate which frequency bands retain comparatively large error.
Importantly, frequency magnitude does not identify the spatial locations of those errors because the
power spectrum discards Fourier phase. The relevant goal is therefore to identify difficult frequency
components and use them to construct a frequency-derived latent weight, rather than to claim a
localized pixel-level error map.
One natural idea is curriculum learning Bengio et al. (2009); Kumar et al. (2010), in which easy
samples are shown first and harder samples are introduced later. Classical curricula, however, often
rely on a fixed notion of difficulty. In diffusion training, a sample’s difficulty changes as the model
improves, so a static ordering can quickly become stale. What is needed instead is a strategy that (i)
measures which samples become difficult relative to the current model when noise is increased, (ii)
remembers this relative difficulty across training steps, and (iii) identifies which residual frequencies
remain difficult. Unlike demonstration- or feedback-conditioned language-model self-distillation
Shenfeld et al. (2026); Hübotter et al. (2026), SALD constructs its reference from two noise states of
the same image–caption pair: a lower-noise easy path evaluated without gradient tracking and a higher-
noise hard path that supplies the training gradient. Advantage-Guided Diffusion (AGD) performs
differentiable sample-level reweighting, Temporal Advantage Memory (TAM) maintains persistent
relative-difficulty estimates and adapts future noise gaps, and Spectral Advantage Decomposition
(SAD) constructs a frequency-derived latent weight. These components share one set of model
parameters and require no external teacher or additional model copy during training or inference.

ADDITIONAL RESULTS
In this section, we present additional qualitative and quantitative results to further analyze SALD’s
behavior across different datasets, architectures, and training settings. We include extended visual
comparisons and supplementary experiments that highlight the contributions of AGD, TAM, and
SAD to generation quality, optimization stability, and fine-grained detail preservation.
Algorithm 1 summarizes a single SALD training step. Given a mini-batch of image–caption pairs, we
first determine a base noise-level gap using a simple curriculum: during warm-up, the gap increases
from 1 to ∆, after which it is sampled uniformly from {1, . . . , ∆}. This base gap is then modulated
for each sample using Temporal Advantage Memory (TAM), which scales the gap according to the
sample’s stored difficulty score and clips it to the valid range. For each example, we sample a lower-
noise easy level and obtain the corresponding higher-noise hard level by adding the TAM-adjusted gap.
Two noisy views of the same latent are then constructed using independent Gaussian perturbations.
The same model performs two forward passes: the easy path is evaluated under no_grad and
serves as an internal reference, whereas the hard path remains differentiable and supplies the training
gradient. From the residuals of these two paths, Spectral Advantage Decomposition (SAD) compares
their frequency-domain error magnitudes and maps a zero-phase spectral gate back to the latent
coordinates to obtain a frequency-derived weighting kernel. Since this construction does not retain
the residual phase or recover the locations of the original errors, the kernel should not be interpreted
as a localized pixel-level advantage map. Instead, it reweights the hard-path residual loss using the
relative spectral errors of the two paths. In parallel, Advantage-Guided Diffusion (AGD) defines the
sample advantage as the difference between the differentiable hard-path loss and the stop-gradient
easy-path reference loss. It converts this advantage into a normalized sample weight that emphasizes
harder examples within the batch. Finally, TAM stores the numerical advantage for future visits to
the sample; the memory is non-trainable, and no gradient is propagated through this state update.
The SALD objective is then formed by averaging the sample-weighted SAD losses of the hard paths
before backpropagation. In this way, the algorithm adapts the training signal across samples, noise
levels, and spectral components through a dynamic, self-referenced curriculum.

LIMITATIONS
SALD evaluates each training example through two noise levels using the same diffusion model:
the lower-noise easy path provides an internal reference under no_grad, whereas the higher-noise
hard path supplies the training gradient. Consequently, SALD requires neither an external teacher
nor additional trainable model parameters during training or inference. Nevertheless, the additional
easy-path evaluation increases the per-step training cost by approximately 25%. Although our
wall-clock-matched experiments (Table 5) show that the resulting improvement in optimization
efficiency can offset this overhead, reducing the per-step training cost remains an important direction
for future work. One possibility is to cache easy-path reference predictions from earlier checkpoints.
However, such cached predictions would become stale as the model changes and would depart from
the formulation considered in this work, where the reference is always computed with the current
model parameters.
TAM stores one non-trainable scalar for each training sample. This memory cost is negligible for the
dataset sizes considered in our experiments but grows linearly with the number of training examples.
Moreover, TAM updates a sample’s memory only when that sample is revisited, so its curriculum
effect is weaker when each example is seen only a few times during training. For extremely large
web-scale datasets, group-level, clustered, or compressed difficulty statistics could reduce this cost
and increase the update frequency, although such approximations may sacrifice the precision of the
per-sample curriculum.
Finally, our empirical evaluation is limited to fine-tuning pretrained text-to-image flow-matching
models; we do not evaluate SALD for training from scratch. Our convergence analysis also holds
only for a fixed TAM memory state (Remark 4). While we describe how the same easy–hard
self-referencing principle can be extended to DDPM-style noise-prediction objectives, we do not
experimentally validate that extension in this work. Evaluating SALD with noise-prediction models,
in from-scratch training, and in other generative modalities, including video and 3D generation,
remain directions for future research.

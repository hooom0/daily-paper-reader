# ABSTRACT 

Continual personalization of text-to-image diffusion models requires sequentially acquiring new concepts while retaining previously learned ones. However, exist-ing methods either suffer from catastrophic forgetting or rely on storing additional concept-specific parameters and spatial components, causing their parameter foot-print to grow with the concept stream. This limits their ability to scale to long sequences of personalization tasks. We propose a rehearsal-free approach that uses a single fixed-size hypernetwork to continually personalize a frozen diffusion model. Instead of expanding the model as new concepts are acquired, the hyper-network dynamically produces the concept-specific adaptations required for per-sonalization while preserving previously learned concepts. Our framework further integrates spatial control into the personalization process, allowing users to spec-ify where a personalized concept should appear without introducing additional per-concept components. This formulation enables continual personalization with a parameter footprint that remains independent of the number of learned concepts, aside from compact concept representations. Experiments demonstrate strong re-tention of previously learned concepts and reliable spatial grounding, matching or improving upon existing methods while scaling effectively to long streams of personalization tasks. 1arXiv:2610.01331v1 [cs.CV] 1 Oct 2026 Preprint. # 1 INTRODUCTION 

Continual learning (McCloskey & Cohen, 1989; Wang et al., 2024) considers settings in which models must acquire new knowledge while retaining what they have previously learned. This prob-lem arises in text-to-image diffusion models (Ho et al., 2020; Rombach et al., 2022), where users may progressively personalize a pretrained model to new visual concepts. Personalization adapts a model to a specific subject or style from a small set of reference images (Gal et al., 2023; Ruiz et al., 2023). The model can then generate that concept under new prompts. When concepts arrive sequentially, the model must acquire each new concept without forgetting earlier ones. This is con-tinual personalization . This setting is harder when storing previous training data is undesirable or infeasible (Carlini et al., 2023; Dubi´ nski et al., 2025; Verwimp et al., 2023). Existing continual personalization methods typically store a separate adaptation for each con-cept (Dong et al., 2024), so the number of stored parameters grows linearly with the number of concepts. This limits their scalability to long concept sequences. We address this limitation with a fixed-size hypernetwork (Ha et al., 2017), which generates low-rank updates (Hu et al., 2022) for a frozen diffusion model from a task embedding per concept, kept orthogonal to those of earlier con-cepts. Thus, the same network can generate adaptations for an increasing number of concepts, with each concept requiring only a compact representation rather than a separate adapter. Because the hypernetwork is updated continually, it can also forget previously learned adaptations. We mitigate this interference with output-space regularization (von Oswald et al., 2020). Personalization also requires controlling where a concept appears. Existing concept adapters do not encode spatial information, while storing a separate spatial module per concept would again sacrifice scalability. We extend the same hypernetwork to generate placement tokens, learned from synthetic composites with controlled object locations. At inference time, a user-provided bounding box guides the personalized concept to the desired region without additional per-concept parameters. We evaluate our approach on the Concept-Incremental Flexible Customization (CIFC) bench-mark (Dong et al., 2024) and extend it to sequences of up to 100 concepts. On SD-1.5, we out-perform CIDM (Dong et al., 2024) in image alignment at matched text alignment and achieve ap-proximately 4× lower forgetting than CIDM and 23 × lower forgetting than fine-tuning. Along the stream, our method retains the first ten concepts better on average than CIDM at every length CIDM can reach, and it continues up to 100 concepts. We also demonstrate reliable placement, placing seven personalized concepts in their requested quadrants in every evaluated case. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: • We propose a continual personalization method in which a fixed-size hypernetwork gener-ates concept-specific low-rank adaptations without storing an adapter per concept. • We extend the same hypernetwork to generate spatial conditioning for user-specified bounding boxes without additional per-concept modules. • We evaluate our approach on sequences of up to 100 concepts, beyond the 37 that CIDM supports, and show that it outperforms CIDM in image alignment on SD-1.5 and achieves lower forgetting, while maintaining a fixed parameter count. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Continual learning and personalization. Continual learning trains a model on a sequence of tasks without forgetting earlier ones (McCloskey & Cohen, 1989; De Lange et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024), through regularization (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Li & Hoiem, 2016), rehearsal (Rebuffi et al., 2017; Shin et al., 2017) or parameter-efficient adaptation (Wang et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2023; Liang & Li, 2024). Diffusion personalization adapts a text-to-image model to a subject or style from a few images, with learned tokens (Gal et al., 2023), fine-tuning (Ruiz et al., 2023; Kumari et al., 2023) or low-rank adapters (Hu et al., 2022). Continual personalization learns such concepts in sequence, through concept-specific isolation (Smith et al., 2024b;a), replay or distillation (Sun et al., 2024; Jha et al., 2025), or parameter merging and allocation (Staniszewski et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025). CIDM (Dong et al., 2024; 2026), whose CIFC benchmark we adopt, keeps a separate set of low-rank parameters per concept, so its storage grows with the stream. CNS (Liao et al., 2025) 2Preprint. Under review. instead allocates part of the backbone to each concept, which bounds their number by its capacity. We keep the backbone fixed and generate every adaptation with one hypernetwork. 

Hypernetworks and concept separation. Hypernetworks (Ha et al., 2017) generate the parameters of a target network from a compact task representation. In continual learning, von Oswald et al. (2020) regularize the generated parameters rather than the weights of the hypernetwork, and we adopt this output-space view for diffusion personalization. Hypernetworks have also been used for fast personalization (Ruiz et al., 2024) and concept removal (W´ ojcik et al., 2026), but not for learning concepts in sequence. Orthogonal Adaptation (Po et al., 2024), SeqLoRA (Parsa et al., 2026) and InfLoRA (Liang & Li, 2024) instead place the adapters of different concepts in distinct subspaces, so their capacity stays tied to the adaptation parameters. Our network is shared by all concepts, and only the task embedding tells them apart. Appendix A discusses both lines of work in more detail. 

Spatial grounding. Controlling the spatial location of generated content has been studied through box-conditioned tokens (Li et al., 2023; Lee & Sung, 2024), attention manipulation (Kim et al., 2023), and regional denoising (Bar-Tal et al., 2023). These methods primarily provide spatial con-trol for concepts specified directly by the text prompt, rather than for personalized subjects learned from reference images. Mix-of-Show (Gu et al., 2023) extends spatial control to personalized sub-jects, but relies on retaining a collection of concept-specific adapters. In our setting, adding spatial control should not reintroduce the per-concept parameter growth that limits existing methods. We generate both the concept adaptation and its spatial conditioning from the same compact concept representation, so a user-given box controls placement without any per-concept module. 

# 3 PRELIMINARIES 

Latent diffusion models. A latent diffusion model (Rombach et al., 2022) generates an image by denoising a latent in the space of a pretrained autoencoder. A UNet (Ronneberger et al., 2015) εθ

predicts the noise in a noisy latent xs at timestep s, conditioned on text embeddings c, and is trained with the loss LLDM of Eq. 11 (Appendix B). We reserve t for the concept index. The text conditions the UNet through cross-attention, whose query, key, value and output projections are WQ, WK ,

WV and WO , and we adapt all four. 

Low-rank adaptation. LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) adapts a pretrained weight matrix W0 ∈ Rm×n by adding a rank-r update, 

W = W0 + γr AB , A ∈ Rm×r , B ∈ Rr×n, r ≪ min( m, n ), (1) where γ controls the update magnitude. Only A and B are trained, reducing the number of trainable parameters from mn to r(m + n). Because our updates act only at selected token positions, they are applied as a separate branch rather than merged into W0.

Hypernetworks. A hypernetwork (Ha et al., 2017) generates the parameters of another network from a conditioning vector. We use a hypernetwork hϕ with parameters ϕ, shared across all concepts, that maps a task embedding vt ∈ Rdv of concept t to the matrices (At,ℓ , Bt,ℓ ) of Eq. 1 for each of the L adapted UNet projections. The updates of individual concepts therefore need not be stored. Each concept is represented by its task embedding vt, which generates its adaptation on demand. When the hypernetwork is trained sequentially, however, updating ϕ for a new concept can alter the adaptations generated for earlier concepts, which our regularizer addresses (Section 4.1). 

Problem setting. We consider a sequence of T concepts indexed by t = 1 , . . . , T , where each concept t is associated with a small reference set Dt. Training proceeds sequentially, with access only to the reference set of the current concept. After learning concept t, the model should be able to generate any previously learned concept j ≤ t in response to a natural-language prompt specifying that concept, while preserving its visual identity as subsequent concepts are learned. We additionally consider spatially controlled generation, where a bounding box b ∈ [0 , 1] 4 specifies the desired location of the personalized concept in the generated image. The four adapted projections act on different parts of the model, and each is applied independently at every position, which lets us control where the update takes effect. WK and WV process text tokens, mapping each token to a key and a value that determine how it contributes to the image representation. Updating them at every token would allow the concept adaptation to affect the entire prompt. We therefore restrict these two to the token positions of the concept’s class word , which is its identifier in the prompt, following the masking strategy of TARA (Peng et al., 2026), and leave the remaining text tokens to the frozen projections. In contrast, WQ and WO operate on image features: WQ produces a query for each image position, while WO maps the resulting attention features back into the layer. Their updates can therefore be applied at every spatial position. Only the class word thus reaches the generated update, so we use it as the concept identifier and exclude descriptive attributes from the captions (Appendix E.1). 

Preserving earlier concepts. Although the layer-specific heads separate the generation of updates across layers, all concepts still share the same hypernetwork parameters ϕ. Updating ϕ for a new concept can change the mapping at the inputs corresponding to previously learned concepts, altering their generated updates. We reduce this interference in two complementary ways. First, we assign each concept a distinct direction in the hypernetwork’s input space, so mutually orthogonal inputs represent different concepts. Second, we constrain the hypernetwork outputs at the inputs of earlier concepts, explicitly preserving the adaptations learned for them during subsequent training. We represent each concept with a task embedding in a fixed dv -dimensional space. # 5 EXPERIMENTS 

Setup. We use the CIFC benchmark of Dong et al. (2024), with ten concepts (seven objects and three styles) learned sequentially from four to seven photographs each. Unless stated otherwise, results are averaged over three training seeds and reported with their standard deviation. Methods are compared at matched text alignment, and differences below 0.0091 DINO are treated as within seed-to-seed variation. We use SD-1.5 unless stated otherwise; results on SDXL-base-1.0 are reported in Appendix I. Further details are provided in Appendix C.

# 6 CONCLUSIONS 

We presented a continual personalization framework that uses a single fixed-size hypernetwork to generate concept-specific adaptations for a frozen diffusion model. Each concept is represented by a compact task embedding, while output-space regularization preserves the adaptations generated for previously learned concepts as the hypernetwork is updated. The framework also supports spatial control, allowing the learned concept to be placed within a user-specified bounding box. Across the benchmark, our approach achieves competitive personalization quality while substantially reducing forgetting and keeping the model-side storage constant as new concepts are acquired. These results show that a single hypernetwork can generate the required adaptation for each concept, avoiding the need to store separate parameters for every concept. 

Limitations. The method has three practical limitations. First, image quality depends on the number of reference images available for each concept, with fewer examples providing less information for personalization. Second, the hypernetwork has finite representational capacity, which may become a limiting factor as the number and diversity of concepts in the task sequence grow substantially. Finally, performance depends on the underlying diffusion backbone, and the hypernetwork and reg-ularization settings may require adjustment when transferred to a different architecture. 9Preprint. # ETHICS STATEMENT 

The two benchmarks we use contain photographs of people. CustomConcept101 (Kumari et al., 2023) includes person concepts, and several object concepts in the benchmark of Dong et al. (2024) are photographed being worn or held. We use both datasets only as released, learn no concept outside them, and release no images of people with the code. Personalization of a text-to-image model is dual use: the same mechanism that lets a photographer re-render their own subject lets someone synthesize a likeness without consent. Our contribution is to learning many concepts in sequence in one network rather than to the fidelity of any one of them, so it does not make a likeness easier to forge than the per-concept adapters it replaces. It does make holding many of them at once cheaper, and a store of a hundred identities in one fixed-size network is easier to distribute than a hundred separate adapters. The placement module of §4.2 carries the same dual use at the level of composition: placing a subject in a chosen part of a scene is what a photographer wants and also what a fabricated photograph needs. We report both capabilities, including where they fail (Appendix M.2), because a reader weighing that risk needs the limits as much as the results.

# A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Continual learning and personalization. Continual learning studies models trained on a sequence of tasks while retaining previously acquired knowledge, with catastrophic forgetting as its cen-tral challenge (McCloskey & Cohen, 1989; De Lange et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). Existing approaches mitigate forgetting through regularization , which constrains changes to parameters or functions important for previous tasks (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Li & Hoiem, 2016; Aljundi et al., 2017; Zenke et al., 2017), rehearsal , which stores or generates data from previous tasks (Rebuffi et al., 2017; Castro et al., 2018; Hou et al., 2019; Douillard et al., 2020; Shin et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2018; Cong et al., 2020; Van de Ven et al., 2020; Yin et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2022; Qiu et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2025), and parameter-efficient adaptation , which learns task-specific prompts, adapters, or low-rank updates while keeping a pretrained backbone fixed (Wang et al., 2021; 2022; Smith et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024; Liang & Li, 2024). Most of this literature focuses on discriminative models, where forgetting is measured by degradation in accuracy on previously learned classes. Our setting instead considers generative models, where each task corresponds to a visual concept, and forgetting manifests as a degradation in the ability to reproduce previously learned concepts under novel prompts. Diffusion personalization adapts a pretrained text-to-image model to a specific subject or style from a small set of reference images using learned token em-beddings (Gal et al., 2023), full or partial fine-tuning (Ruiz et al., 2023; Kumari et al., 2023), or low-rank adapters (Hu et al., 2022). Continual personalization extends this setting to a sequence of concepts, introducing the additional challenge of preserving previously learned concepts as new ones are acquired. Existing approaches address this through concept-specific isolation (Smith et al., 2024b;a), replay or distillation (Sun et al., 2024; Jha et al., 2025), and parameter merging or allo-cation (Staniszewski et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2025). CIDM (Dong et al., 2024; 2026), whose CIFC benchmark we adopt, maintains a separate set of low-rank parameters for each concept, causing its parameter storage to grow with the sequence length. CNS (Liao et al., 2025) avoids this grow-ing storage by allocating subsets of the backbone to individual concepts, but consequently limits the number of concepts by the available parameter capacity. Our approach instead keeps the diffu-sion backbone fixed and uses a single hypernetwork to generate concept-specific adaptations for an increasing number of concepts. 

Hypernetworks and concept separation. Hypernetworks (Ha et al., 2017) generate the parame-ters of a target network conditioned on a compact task representation. In continual learning, von Oswald et al. (2020) show that regularizing changes in the generated parameters, rather than the hypernetwork weights themselves, can substantially reduce forgetting. We adopt this output-space perspective for continual diffusion personalization. Hypernetworks have also been explored for fast diffusion personalization (Ruiz et al., 2024) and concept removal (W´ ojcik et al., 2026), but these methods do not consider sequential acquisition of concepts or the interference that arises when a shared hypernetwork is repeatedly updated. A complementary line of work addresses concept in-terference by separating concept-specific adaptations in weight space. Orthogonal Adaptation (Po et al., 2024) and SeqLoRA (Parsa et al., 2026) constrain adapters of different concepts to occupy distinct subspaces, while related interference-free low-rank adaptation has been studied in discrim-inative continual learning (Liang & Li, 2024). These approaches separate concepts directly in pa-rameter space, so the capacity for storing distinct concepts remains tied to the available adaptation parameters. In contrast, our method uses a single hypernetwork shared across all concepts. Each concept is represented by a distinct task embedding, allowing the hypernetwork to generate a differ-15 Preprint. Under review. ent adaptation for each concept while keeping its own size fixed. TARA (Peng et al., 2026) addresses a different source of interference by restricting a concept-specific update to the token positions as-sociated with that concept, rather than allowing it to affect the entire prompt.

# C TRAINING AND GENERATION DETAILS 

Backbones and parameters. We use two backbones, SD-1.5 at 512 2 and SDXL-base-1.0 at 

1024 2. The UNet, the VAE and the text encoders are frozen, and only the hypernetwork trains. It has 26 .0M parameters on SD-1.5 and 191 .1M on SDXL. The content heads account for 21 .9Mand 167 .5M of them, over L = 64 and L = 280 adapted layers, and the placement module for the remaining 4.1M and 23 .5M. SD-1.5 conditions its cross-attention on the last hidden state of a CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder (Radford et al., 2021), of width 768 , which is also the width of the placement tokens. SDXL-base-1.0 conditions on the concatenated penultimate hidden states of its two text encoders and on the pooled projection of the second, of width 1280 .

Training. Each task is trained for 800 steps with batch size 2, AdamW at learning rate 10 −4

and weight decay 10 −2, and the lookahead rate η = 10 −4. Following von Oswald et al. (2020), the output-space regularizer of Eq. 6 is evaluated at the lookahead parameters ϕ − η∇ϕLrec rather than at ϕ. The regularizer weight is β = 7120 on SD-1.5 and β = 3000 on SDXL. It is the only hyperparameter that does not transfer between the two backbones, and Appendix I shows what carrying it over unchanged costs. The hypernetwork trains in fp32. The frozen backbone is kept in fp32 on SD-1.5 and in bf16 on SDXL, and the VAE in fp32 on both. 

Content module. We adapt the attn2 .{to q , to k , to v , to out .0} projections of every attention block and generate adapters at rank r = 4 . Each head consists of two two-layer MLPs of hidden width 50 , one per factor, and each layer has a learned scalar gain folded into A. Every linear layer of a head draws its weights and biases uniformly from [−1/√nin , 1/√nin ] (the default of a PyTorch linear layer), with nin its input width ( 256 for the first layer, 50 for the second). The exception is the output layer that produces B, whose weights and bias are set to zero, and the gain starts at one. The generated update is therefore exactly zero while A is not, so the first gradient reaches the B branch, as for a LoRA whose B starts at zero. The network is initialized once, before the first concept, and every later concept starts from the parameters its predecessors left. Placement module. The box mask of Eq. 9 uses λ = 40 , which makes its edge about a tenth of the frame wide. The positional encoding PE( b) ∈ R64 is the sine and cosine of each of the four box coordinates at eight frequencies. The token head generates M = 4 tokens of the width of the text embeddings, and the placement branch projects queries, keys and values to dk = 64 dimensions. 

Training composites. The placement branch cannot be trained on the concept’s photographs alone. In a photograph the subject is wherever it happens to be, and the noisy latent already re-veals that, so the box adds no information. On half of the training steps we therefore replace the photograph with a composite, and only in the high-noise half of the schedule, s ∈ [S/ 2, S ), where layout is decided and the appearance of the composite is not yet resolved. To build a composite, we segment the subject, shrink its alpha matte by three pixels, and paste it onto one of about one hundred backgrounds synthesized once by the frozen backbone, where it covers 45% to 85% of the frame’s long side. Without the shrink, the soft edge carries the source background into the composite, and at inference it appears as a rim around every subject. The benchmark’s seven object concepts are segmented with ISNet (Qin et al., 2022), and the added concepts with Grounding-DINO boxes for the class word refined by SAM (Liu et al., 2024; Kirillov et al., 2023). For the 45 of 515 photographs where the automatic mask failed, we chose among the candidate masks by hand and recorded the choice. The remaining training steps use the concept’s own photographs, with a random square crop of 80 to 100% of the short side and a random horizontal flip. 

Generation. Generation uses 50 denoising steps at guidance 7.5, and for evaluation we draw ten images per prompt. Bootstrapping, in the manner of MultiDiffusion (Bar-Tal et al., 2023), replaces everything outside the box by a background latent for the first K steps, so the subject can form only inside it. With a box, unless stated otherwise, the gain is κ = 2 , bootstrapping covers the first 

K = 15 steps, and slora = 0 .7. The generated adapters and the attention branch enter only the conditional pass of classifier-free guidance, and the unconditional pass runs the frozen backbone, so guidance amplifies both like any other condition. The gain schedule and the bootstrap each span the first 30% of the denoising steps. The background latent is generated in ten steps with both modules off, so it agrees with the scene and needs no pre-rendered images. The box is dilated by three latent cells before the replacement. The replacement mask of the bootstrap is binary, because blending two noise draws leaves the input under-noised for its timestep and desaturates the output.

E.1 CAPTION ATTRIBUTES 

The training captions describe the scene around a concept but not the concept’s own attributes, such as its color or material. An attribute named in the caption competes with the adapter for carrying it. If every caption of the backpack says “red”, the text branch already accounts for the color, and the adapter has no reason to encode it. At inference the prompt is “a photo of backpack” and names no color. The backpack then comes out gray. Stripping the attribute from the captions moves the color into the adapter. E.2 CAPTIONS FOR THE ADDED CONCEPTS 

The benchmark provides one caption per image, and CustomConcept101 provides none. Training on the two together would confound the length of the sequence with a change of captioning regime. The captions for the ninety added concepts were therefore written from the images themselves, following the argument of Appendix E.1. For each concept, all of its images were considered together. What stays constant across them (color, material, shape, markings, a face) was separated from what varies (where the object is, what it rests on, what is next to it), and only the latter was described. Each of the 515 captions contains its class word exactly once, in its base form. The surroundings may be described, so 32 captions name an object that is also another concept of the sequence, most often a table. In three captions a content word directly precedes the class word, and in each case it describes a posture rather than an attribute ( a seated person , a stretching cat , a centered toy figure ). For evaluation we use the CIFC prompts and assign each added concept to one of its four categories.

# H COMPARISON WITH INDEPENDENTLY TRAINED ADAPTERS 

It is not obvious that a generated adapter can be as good as a trained one. To test this, we train ten independent rank-4 adapters, each directly on the frozen backbone, with the same data and the same text side as our recipe: no identifier token, the delta applied only at the class-word tokens, the same captions and augmentation, and 800 steps per concept. At inference, each adapter is selected by its task index. This control stores a separate adapter for every concept ( 0.40 M parameters each). Its storage grows with T , but it cannot forget. At a common TA of 75 .6, ours at slora = 0 .45 , the control reaches an IA of 78 .5 and a DINO of 0.594 , against 79 .1 ± 0.3 and 0.617 for the generated adapters (two seeds for the control, three for ours). Doubling its training to 1600 steps per concept leaves it at 78 .4 and 0.593 . The generated adapters therefore match independently trained ones on this benchmark, from a network whose size does not depend on T .

Orthogonality of the generated updates. We take the trained model and compute, for ev-ery pair of concepts and every adapted layer, the cosine between the generated updates, 

⟨∆Wϕ,ℓ (vi), ∆Wϕ,ℓ (vj )⟩F /(∥∆Wϕ,ℓ (vi)∥F ∥∆Wϕ,ℓ (vj )∥F ), using the same r × r Gram matri-ces as in Appendix D, so that no ∆W is ever formed. Table 5 reports the mean absolute off-diagonal cosine. The updates of different concepts sit at 0.056 after ten tasks and 0.044 after fifty, compared with 0.27 for the A factors that produce them and 0.001 for random matrices of the same shape. The closest pairs are the two cats ( 0.07 ) and a cat with a dog ( 0.06 ), and Figure 12 shows that no pair stands far above the rest. Nothing in the objective asks for this, and neither does the geometry of the inputs: retraining without Gram–Schmidt, that is, with random non-orthogonal embeddings, reproduces the same overall figures ( 0.056 at T = 10 and 0.043 at T = 50 , against 0.056 and 0.044 

here), with every projection within 0.006 . Near-orthogonality of the generated updates therefore comes from the generator itself, and the orthogonalized inputs do not create it. Gram–Schmidt leaves this statistic unchanged because of the regime we operate in. The embeddings are dv = 256 wide, and two random unit vectors in that many dimensions already meet at an expected cosine of p2/(πd v ) ≈ 0.05 . At T = 50 the space is far from crowded, and forcing the task embeddings apart changes little about how the updates sit relative to one another. The change from T = 10 to T = 50 agrees: the mean cosine falls rather than rises as concepts are added. More concepts do not crowd the output space. Four further mechanisms were measured and rejected after the main design was fixed. Restricting the injection to coarse attention resolutions costs 16 points of IoU > 0.5 and improves color by only 0.007 , so the finest layers take part in the layout. A penalty on the cross-attention logits of the concept’s tokens, applied at every image position outside the box, has no effect: 62% , 64% , 64% 

and 65% of generations reach IoU > 0.5 at penalties of 0, 3, 6 and 10 . The text encoder is causal, so every token after the concept still carries it. Extending the penalty to the whole causal tail raises containment to 0.81 , against 0.67 for the branch alone, but in the one pair where TA was also scored it costs six points ( 80 .7 to 74 .6). The words that describe the scene come after the concept in the prompt and are suppressed along with it, which is why the method adds content inside the box and suppresses nothing. Isolating self-attention inside the box destroys placement (from 75% to 4% ), which shows that the region outside the box generates the subject independently. Feathering the bootstrapping mask reduces detection to 74 of 84 generations and desaturates the outputs, for the reason given in Appendix C. The background that bootstrapping paints outside the box could instead be drawn from a set of images rendered in advance. On this benchmark, such a background contains the subject no better than the prompt-derived one ( 0.92 in both) and places it worse (IoU 0.63 against 0.73 , and 76% against 90% of generations above 0.5), because the scene it paints disagrees with the one the prompt asks for. It also requires that set of images at inference. The placement controls leave the no-box protocol of Table 6 unchanged. With every control enabled and no box supplied, identity, color, fill and IoU stay within sampling noise of a clean run, so the results with and without a box come from one checkpoint under one protocol. 

# N MULTI -CONCEPT COMPOSITION 

Because CIFC defines no protocol for scoring scenes with several concepts, this appendix reports our own mechanism on its own terms. We follow the region-based sampling of CIDM (Dong et al., 2024). At every step, each box is denoised with its concept’s prompt, the adapter generated for that concept and the placement module addressed by that box, and the region predictions are merged with a global one. In CIDM, each region is also helped by the token embeddings that CIDM learns for its concept at every layer. Our network keeps no per-concept tokens, so its regions rely on the generated adapter and the placement module alone. We run the eleven composite scenes of CIFC on SDXL, with four seeds each and one setting for all of them. Inside the boxes, composition works. Of the 124 regions the scenes request, the requested concept is the best match for its own region in 121 . The regions also keep the identity of their concepts, with a mean DINO of 0.716 against their own references. The concepts become part of one scene rather than a collage. They share the lighting and drawing style of the background and sit at a plausible depth within it (Figure 16). 30 Preprint. Under review.

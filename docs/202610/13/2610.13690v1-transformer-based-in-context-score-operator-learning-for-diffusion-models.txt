ABSTRACT
Diffusion models typically learn the generative dynamics of a single target distribution or a fam-
ily of distributions indexed by conditioning variables fixed during training. This limits their ability
to adapt to a previously unseen distribution from only a small set of examples. We introduce an
in-context solution operator learning framework for diffusion models, in which each target distri-
bution is treated as a task drawn from a meta-distribution and is specified at inference time by an
empirical context set. Our method learns an in-context score operator that maps the context samples,
a noisy query point, and the diffusion time to the task-dependent score, enabling reverse-diffusion
sampling on unseen tasks without retraining or parameter updates. A key observation is that the task-
dependent score admits a Gaussian-kernel conditional-mean representation. Its empirical Nadaraya–
Watson approximation can be represented exactly by a model in our constructed transformer class,
providing a principled foundation for the proposed query-dependent attention architecture. The op-
erator is trained through amortized denoising score matching across task distributions. We establish
generalization-error bounds that quantify the effects of the context size, the numbers of training
tasks and within-task samples, model complexity, and intrinsic and ambient dimensions. Numeri-
cally, we evaluate the method on a range of scientific and visual datasets, including Navier-Stokes,
ERA5 climate data [12], QuickDraw sketches [20], and Food-101 [3]. The results show that the
proposed method performs well on relatively simple task families, while its performance degrades
as task complexity increases, thereby illustrating both the promise and the limitations of in-context
learning for diffusion models.

Introduction
Diffusion models have emerged as a powerful class of generative models, achieving strong empirical performance
across a broad range of domains. They have been widely adopted in computer vision [42, 13, 43, 21, 2, 36], audio
generation [23, 32, 18, 19], and reinforcement learning and control [35, 38, 56]. More recently, their applications have
expanded to scientific and engineering problems, including data purification [34, 48, 49, 52], climate-data downscaling
[44, 41], and partial differential equation (PDE) problems [17, 15, 51, 29]. Their success has been further enhanced by
conditional generation techniques such as classifier guidance [8], classifier-free guidance [14], and conditioned latent
diffusion [39], which provide substantial control over the generated samples.
Despite this progress, standard diffusion models typically learn the reverse generative dynamics associated with the
distribution represented by a particular training dataset [42, 13, 43]. Conditional diffusion models extend this for-
mulation to families of distributions indexed by predefined conditioning variables, such as class labels, classifier
outputs, text embeddings, or other task-specific signals [8, 14, 39]. Although these models can generate from multi-

Consequently, they are not generally designed to infer a previously unseen target distribution solely from a small,
unordered set of contextual samples. Adapting to such a distribution may therefore require retraining, fine-tuning, or
the construction of an additional conditioning representation. Recent few-shot and score-operator approaches have
begun to relax this restriction [11, 27], but learning a general in-context score operator directly from empirical context
sets remains underexplored.
Many applications, however, require generative models to operate over an entire family of target distributions. In
scientific computing, for example, the relevant solution or data distribution may vary with the governing equation,
physical parameters, forcing terms, initial or boundary conditions, or available observations. This family-level struc-
ture is central to operator-learning methods such as DeepONet and the Fourier neural operator, which learn mappings
between problem specifications and their corresponding solutions [28, 26, 24]. Training an independent diffusion
model for every possible configuration is computationally prohibitive and fails to exploit the structure shared across
related problems. This motivates a solution-operator perspective for diffusion models: rather than learning the score
function associated with a single prescribed target distribution, the objective is to learn an operator that maps informa-
tion specifying a target distribution to its corresponding time-dependent score function [27]. Such an operator should
generalize across a family of distributions and, ideally, adapt to previously unseen members of that family without
retraining or parameter updates.
In this work, we introduce an in-context solution operator learning framework for diffusion models. Each target
distribution, referred to as a task, is drawn from a meta-distribution over probability measures. More precisely, we
learn an in-context score operator that maps the empirical context distribution, together with a noisy query point
and the diffusion time, to the task-dependent score. This formulation casts task adaptation as an operator-evaluation
problem: the context set identifies the target distribution, while the shared operator captures common structure across
the task family. It thus extends diffusion modeling from learning a score function for a fixed target distribution to
learning a reusable score operator over a family of distributions. The solution operator is trained through amortized
denoising score matching across task distributions, allowing knowledge to be shared across the entire task family.
Once trained, it can perform reverse diffusion for a previously unseen task directly from its prompt samples, without
retraining, fine-tuning, or task-specific network parameter updates. This perspective is also closely related to recent
work on in-context operator learning over probability measures [7].
Our method operates directly on the empirical context set and constructs score estimates through query-dependent
interactions among the context samples, the noisy query point, and the diffusion time. A key observation is that the
task-dependent score can be expressed through a Gaussian-kernel conditional-mean representation. Replacing the
population conditional mean with its empirical counterpart yields a Nadaraya–Watson estimator based on the context
samples. We show that this empirical estimator can be represented exactly by a model in our constructed transformer
class. This connection provides a principled motivation for the proposed transformer-based in-context score operator,
rather than treating the transformer architecture as an ad hoc design choice.
Theoretically, We further establish generalization-error bounds for the learned score operator in Section 4. This bounds
quantify the dependence on the context size n, the number of training tasks K, the number of episodes per task J,
the number of diffusion samples per episode NT , and the intrinsic and ambient dimensions d and D. The finite-
context error decays as n−2/(d+6), with the intrinsic dimension governing the exponent and the ambient dimension
entering only through polynomial and logarithmic factors. This distinction indicates that in-context score estimation is
governed primarily by the intrinsic geometry of the task distributions, exhibiting a curse of intrinsic rather than ambient
dimensionality. The statistical error contains terms of order K−1/2, (KJ)−1/2, and (KJNT )−1/2, separating the
effects of the numbers of tasks, episodes, and diffusion samples. In particular, the task-level persistent K−1/2 term is
unaffected by J and NT . Thus, within the present upper-bound analysis, increasing within-task sampling alone cannot
reduce this contribution, highlighting the number and diversity of training tasks as fundamental to generalization..
We summarize our main contributions as follows:
• We formulate in-context learning for diffusion models at the level of task distributions. Each task is modeled
as a probability distribution drawn from a meta-distribution, and a previously unseen task is specified at
inference time by a small context set represented as an empirical measure.
• We show that the task-dependent score can be expressed in terms of a Gaussian-kernel conditional mean.
We further prove that its empirical Nadaraya–Watson approximation, and hence the corresponding empirical
score estimator, can be represented exactly by a model in our constructed transformer class. This connec-
tion provides a principled foundation for the proposed transformer-based in-context score operator. Trained
through amortized denoising score matching across task distributions, the operator enables reverse-diffusion
sampling on unseen tasks without retraining, fine-tuning, or task-specific parameter updates.

Related work
Our framework is closely related to the broader paradigm of in-context learning (ICL), in which a model adapts
to a new task through contextual demonstrations without updating its parameters. ICL was initially studied exten-
sively in large language models [4, 31, 55, 30], and related ideas have since been developed for scientific computing
and operator learning. ICON [54] studies supervised in-context operator learning for differential equations, using
prompted input-output examples to adapt a single network to previously unseen operators. PROSE-PDE [45] takes
a complementary multimodal approach, combining numerical solution data with symbolic equation representations
for multi-operator learning. Self-supervised in-context operator learning in probability-measure spaces has also been
investigated for optimal transport, mean-field games, and Wasserstein gradient flows [16, 9, 7]. This measure-valued
task perspective is especially relevant to our formulation, in which each task is itself a target probability distribution.
In parallel, several works have incorporated contextual demonstrations into diffusion-based image generation. Prompt
Diffusion [47], iPromptDiff [5], and Context Diffusion [33] demonstrate that diffusion models can use visual examples
as context for image generation and editing. These methods, however, primarily address visually prompted image-
to-image generation in paired or query-conditioned settings. They do not formulate diffusion modeling as in-context
learning of a solution operator over a family of target probability distributions, which is the focus of the present work.
Other two related directions are Score Neural Operator (SNO) [27] and few-shot diffusion models [11], both of which
can be viewed as forms of context-conditioned generation. In SNO, the prompt set is first summarized by a distribution-
level representation, specifically a kernel mean embedding, and the score function is then predicted conditioned on
this embedding. In few-shot diffusion models, the support set is encoded into a task-dependent feature, for example
by aggregating patch-level information through an encoder, and diffusion sampling is performed conditioned on this
feature. In contrast, our method operates directly on the empirical context set and constructs score estimates through
query-dependent interactions among the context samples, the noisy query point, and the diffusion time, rather than
conditioning solely on a compressed global representation.
A growing theoretical literature seeks to explain the mechanisms and generalization properties underlying ICL. Exist-
ing studies interpret ICL as implicit Bayesian inference [50], characterize the function classes that transformers can
learn from demonstrations [10], and connect transformer forward passes to gradient-based meta-learning [46]. Other
works show that transformers can implement statistical learning algorithms in context [1] and study their generaliza-
tion and stability from an algorithm-learning perspective [25]. The diversity and number of pretraining tasks have also
been identified as central factors governing the emergence of ICL and its generalization to unseen tasks [37]. In scien-
tific computing, [6] develops an operator-learning analysis of ICL for linear systems and elliptic PDEs. [7] studies ICL
directly on probability-measure spaces and establishes generalization-error bounds for in-context operator learning of
optimal transport maps. Particularly relevant to our analysis is [40], which connects attention mechanisms to kernel
regression on structured manifolds and establishes generalization guarantees governed by intrinsic rather than ambient
dimension. Our theory complements these results by analyzing an in-context score operator and separating the effects
of finite context, the number of meta-training tasks, within-task sampling, model complexity, and intrinsic dimension.

Methodology

defined on the space of task distributions and the learning objective is given by the population loss. To approximate

Theoretical results
We now present the theoretical results for the proposed in-context score operator. We begin with the observation
that the task-dependent population score admits a Gaussian-kernel conditional-mean representation. Replacing the
unknown task distribution with its empirical context measure naturally yields a vector-valued Nadaraya–Watson es-
timator and, consequently, an empirical score estimator. This kernel representation observation motivates both our
transformer architecture and its theoretical analysis: we show that the empirical estimator can be represented exactly
by a model in our constructed transformer class, revealing a direct correspondence between Gaussian-kernel regres-
sion and the attention mechanism. Guided by this connection, we learn an in-context score operator through amortized
denoising score matching across task distributions and analyze its generalization to unseen tasks drawn from the meta-
distribution. Section 4.1 develops the kernel representation and its exact transformer realization. Section 4.2 then
presents and interprets the main generalization theorem, followed by Section 4.3, which outlines the corresponding
error decomposition and the proof strategy.

The accuracy of the empirical kernel estimator depends on how well the context samples cover the support of the
task distribution ρ. In a general ambient space RD, achieving sufficient coverage may require an impractically large
context set. Fortunately, many high-dimensional datasets possess substantially lower-dimensional intrinsic structure.
Natural images provide a familiar example: although they are represented as high-dimensional pixel arrays, semanti-
cally meaningful images are often modeled as concentrating near a low-dimensional image manifold. Similar structure
arises in scientific applications. For instance, in the Navier–Stokes problem considered in Section 5, task distributions
induced by different viscosity parameters retain shared coherent flow structures. Although the probability law varies
with viscosity, the resulting solution fields may remain concentrated near a common low-dimensional solution mani-
fold. Motivated by these observations, we assume that all task distributions are supported on a common d-dimensional
manifold M ⊂RD, where d ≪D. Under this assumption, the complexity of context-based score estimation is
governed primarily by the intrinsic dimension d, rather than by the potentially much larger ambient dimension D. For
the finite-sample analysis, a uniform loss bound follows from the bounded mean-network output and the positive time
cutoff. The precise assumptions are stated below.

initial conditions, we first draw samples from a log-normal distribution on a uniform 256 × 256 mesh over the spatial
domain x ∈[0, 2π]2. We parameterize Navier-Stokes tasks by ξ ∈Ξ, here the ξ := ν is the viscosity. Let π0 be a
probability measure over initial conditions ω0, and for each ξ ∈Ξ, we let ST,ξ denote the solution operator mapping
ω0 to the vorticity field at time T, i.e., ST,ξ : ω0(x) 7→ω(T, x; ω0, ξ); this induces the task distribution
ρξ := (ST,ξ)#π0 ∈P(X).
Let ν be a probability distribution on Ξ, and define the mapping F : Ξ →P(X) by F(ξ) = ρξ. The meta-distribution
over task distributions is then the pushforward
µ := F#ν ∈P(P(X)).
In the following experiments, we set T = 1 and use the finite volume method (FVM) implemented in JAX-CFD [22]
for generating data. To evaluate both in-distribution and out-of-distribution generalization, we construct 100 tasks by
uniformly sampling ν from the union of two intervals,
ν ∼Unif ([1.5e −3, 1.2e −2] ∪[1.5e −2, 5e −2]) .
(39)
For the in-distribution setting, we randomly sample five values of ν from the first interval and five values from the
second interval, and evaluate the model on these 10 values. The results of the different methods are reported in Table 1.
We also present three representative examples with ν = 1.5e−3, ν = 2.2e−3, and ν = 3e−2, as shown in Figures 1
and 2. For the out-of-distribution setting, we evaluate the model at ν1 = 1.2e−3, ν2 = 1.3e−2, and ν3 = 6.4e−2, all

quality, we compare the generated and reference distributions using the weighted mean energy log ratio (MELRw) and
the JSD between empirical scalar-valued PDFs. The results are reported in Table 1. As shown in Table 1, our method
outperforms the baselines in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings, except for MELRw in the OOD(high)
setting, where Cond DM achieves a lower value.

Food-101 Dataset
Food-101 [3] is a large-scale food image dataset consisting of 101 food categories, with 1,000 images in each cate-
gory. The images exhibit substantial variation in viewpoint, scale, illumination, occlusion, and plating style. In this
setting, the meta-distribution can be viewed as the overall distribution over food images, while each task distribution
corresponds to a specific food category. Following the same procedure as in the QuickDraw experiments, we consider
K = 5, 10, and 100 categories and perform the same quantitative evaluation. Note that all 1,000 images in each
selected category are used to construct the empirical task distribution from which both training samples and inference
prompts and reference samples are drawn. Thus, this experiment evaluates in-distribution generation only and does
not assess out-of-distribution generalization.
As shown in Table 4, our method outperforms the baselines across most metrics and settings. This suggests that our
method more effectively captures the class-conditioned data distribution and generates samples that are closer to the
reference distribution. As K increases, the performance of all methods generally degrades. As in the QuickDraw
experiment, this reflects the increased complexity of the categorical task family under a fixed per-task data budget,
prompt size, and shared model capacity, rather than the K−1/2 scaling under a fixed meta-distribution. Even in this
more challenging regime, however, our method retains a substantial advantage over the baselines.
These results should not be interpreted as an inherent limitation of the proposed method to categorical task distribu-
tions. Rather, they reflect the resource-limited regime considered here, where the per-task data and model capacity
are fixed relative to an increasingly broad task family. Whether additional data and model capacity can mitigate this
degradation is left for future work.

Conclusion
We introduced an in-context solution operator learning framework for diffusion models. In our formulation, each target
distribution is treated as a task drawn from a meta-distribution and is specified at inference time by a finite set of i.i.d.
context samples. The proposed in-context score operator acts directly on this empirical measure, constructing task-
dependent score estimates through interactions among the context samples, the noisy query point, and the diffusion
time. By training the operator through amortized denoising score matching across task distributions, reverse-diffusion
sampling can be performed on previously unseen tasks without retraining, fine-tuning, or task-specific parameter
updates.
A central observation underlying our method is that the task-dependent score admits a Gaussian-kernel conditional-
mean representation. Its empirical approximation leads to a Nadaraya–Watson estimator that can be represented
exactly by a model in our constructed transformer class. This connection provides a principled explanation for the pro-
posed architecture: the transformer parameters encode a distribution-independent kernel-regression algorithm, while
all task-specific information is supplied through the context set. We further established generalization-error bounds
that separate the effects of the context size, the number of meta-training tasks, the number of episodes per task, the
number of diffusion samples per episode, model complexity, and data geometry. The finite-context rate is governed
by the intrinsic dimension of the task manifold, whereas the ambient dimension enters only through polynomial and
logarithmic factors. Moreover, the persistent task-level error term shows that additional within-task sampling cannot
compensate for insufficient meta-training task coverage, highlighting the importance of training on a sufficiently large
and diverse family of distributions.
Our empirical results further demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework. For task families of lower
complexity, such as Navier-Stokes equation, the method achieves both strong in-distribution performance and modest
out-of-distribution generalization. For task families of intermediate complexity, such as ERA5, it remains effective in
the in-distribution setting. By contrast, for more complex task families, such as QuickDraw and Food-101, generaliza-
tion becomes substantially more challenging, and even strong in-distribution performance can be difficult to achieve
when both the number of in-context prompts and the amount of training data are limited. Importantly, these findings
should not be interpreted as evidence that the method fails on more complex task families. Rather, they indicate that
generalization in these settings are intrinsically more challenging under limited context and finite training data.

D
Additional Experimental Details
D.1
Network Architecture
We provide additional implementation details for the architectures used in our experiments. While the models em-
ployed in practice are more expressive than the simplified versions presented in the main text for theoretical analysis,
they retain the same core design principles. All three methods use a time-conditioned, multi-resolution U-Net as the
denoising backbone, and differ primarily in how the prompt set is encoded and injected. In ICL DM, the prompt set
is represented by multi-scale features that interact with the noisy query through cross-attention. In Cond DM, the
prompt set is compressed into a single learned embedding that modulates the U-Net through FiLM-style conditioning.
In SNO, the learned prompt embedding is replaced by a projected kernel mean embedding (KME), while the denoising
U-Net remains otherwise the same as in Cond DM.
ICL DM.
Our implementation of ICL DM uses a time-conditioned, multi-resolution U-Net with prompt-dependent
cross-attention. Given an unordered prompt set ˆρ = {x(i)}n
i=1, a noisy query x(t) and diffusion time t, we first
encode the diffusion time using a sinusoidal embedding followed by a multilayer perceptron. The prompt set is then
processed by a prompt encoder composed of an input convolution, an initial downsampling layer, and a hierarchy of
residual blocks with time modulation. At each resolution, a set-transformer block applies self-attention across the
prompt elements, allowing information to be exchanged before the features are aggregated by permutation-invariant
mean pooling. This produces multi-scale key-value feature maps that summarize the prompt set. In parallel, the noisy
query x(t) is passed through a U-Net encoder-decoder with residual blocks at corresponding resolutions, while cross-
attention modules at every scale in both the encoder and decoder inject the pooled prompt features into the query
pathway. After a final upsampling stage to the original spatial resolution, the network outputs the predicted noise
field, which serves as the model prediction for denoising score matching. Specifically, we use a U-Net architecture
with 4 resolution levels, where the number of channels at each level is [64, 128, 256, 512]. The timestep embedding
is produced by a two-layer MLP with hidden dimension 256, each residual block contains two convolutional layers,
cross-attention uses 4 heads at every scale, and the prompt set transformer uses 2 attention heads.
Cond DM.
Our conditional diffusion model uses a time-conditioned, multi-resolution U-Net in which the prompt set
is compressed into a single global conditioning vector rather than injected through cross-attention. Given an unordered
prompt set ˆρ = {x(i)}n
i=1, a noisy query x(t), and diffusion time t, we encode t using a sinusoidal embedding followed
by a multilayer perceptron. The prompt set is passed through a prompt encoder consisting of an input convolution, an
initial downsampling layer, and a hierarchy of residual blocks, and only the deepest prompt feature is retained. This
feature is averaged over the prompt dimension to ensure permutation invariance, aggregated over spatial locations
by a learned attention-based pooling module, and mapped to a prompt embedding vector. The resulting prompt
embedding is added to the time embedding and used to modulate all residual blocks of the denoising U-Net through
FiLM-style conditioning. The denoising network follows an encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections and
includes a self-attention block at the bottleneck to enhance global spatial interactions. A final upsampling stage restores
the original spatial resolution, after which the network predicts the noise field used for denoising score matching.
Specifically, the U-Net has 4 resolution levels with channel dimensions [64,128,256,512]; the timestep embedding
and prompt embedding both have dimension 256; the spatial attention pooling module uses a hidden dimension of
128; and both the deepest prompt self-attention block and the bottleneck self-attention block use 2 attention heads.
SNO.
Our implementation of SNO uses a time-conditioned, multi-resolution U-Net in which the conditioning infor-
mation is provided by a kernel mean embedding (KME) of the prompt set rather than by cross-attention over prompt
features. Given a KME vector associated with the prompt set, a noisy query x(t), and diffusion time t, we first encode
the diffusion time using a sinusoidal embedding followed by a multilayer perceptron. The KME vector is then mapped
by a two-layer projection network to the same latent dimension as the timestep embedding, and the resulting condi-
tioning vector is added to the time embedding. This combined embedding is used to modulate all residual blocks in
the denoising U-Net through FiLM-style conditioning. Other than replacing the prompt encoder and attention-based
prompt pooling in Cond DM by the KME projection module, the rest of the denoising architecture is identical to the
conditional diffusion model baseline. Specifically, we use a U-Net architecture with 4 resolution levels, where the
number of channels at each level is [64, 128, 256, 512]. The timestep embedding and projected KME embedding both
have dimension 256, and the bottleneck self-attention block uses 2 attention heads.

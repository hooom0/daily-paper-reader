ABSTRACT
Few-step text-to-image generators produce high-quality images with few network
forward passes, yet struggle with complex prompts involving multiple subjects,
interactions, spatial relations, counting, and attribute binding. Can these errors
be reduced without changing the generator’s parameters or increasing its forward-
pass count? We propose a lightweight correction framework for a frozen 6B-
parameter generator.
The generator predicts velocities that determine how its
intermediate image representation is updated; a corrector with fewer than 20M
parameters learns residual adjustments at selected sampling steps. Because ear-
lier corrections change the states encountered at later steps, our On-Trajectory
Flow-Bridge Supervision derives residual targets at the states actually reached
during corrected generation. A single corrector is shared across selected steps
and runs in parallel with the generator. Only the corrector is trained, requiring
neither reward optimization nor re-distillation. To our knowledge, this is the first
framework for lightweight velocity-residual adaptation of fully frozen few-step
text-to-image generators whose corrector runs in parallel with the generator while
preserving its original sampling schedule and forward-pass count. On our target
complex-scene distribution, the framework achieves a NetWin of +17.60 percent-
age points over frozen Z-Image-Turbo in pairwise instruction-following evalua-
tion, with only 26.9 ms (0.84%) additional end-to-end latency per image and no
increase in backbone evaluations.

INTRODUCTION
Few-step text-to-image generation aims to recover the effectiveness of multi-step denoising with
only a limited number of network forward passes. Recent diffusion and flow distillation methods
have made substantial progress toward this goal (Kim et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2024;
Zheng et al., 2026b). Models such as Z-Image-Turbo already produce images of high visual quality
(Z-Image Team et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025), yet their adherence to complex instructions remains
limited, particularly for multi-subject interactions, spatial relations, counting, and attribute binding.
Thus, visually plausible outputs can still miss important details specified in the prompt.
Existing post-training methods improve instruction following by adapting the generator (Jiang et al.,
2024; Black et al., 2024; Prabhudesai et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; 2026). However, adapting
a step-distilled generator can disrupt its learned few-step sampling behavior, compromising image
quality under the original schedule. To explore an alternative, we draw inspiration from the stronger
complex-instruction following of 50-step Z-Image compared with eight-step Z-Image-Turbo on our
target distribution (Fig. 1(c)). Comparing their sampling trajectories from shared initial noise reveals
substantial differences in update direction over matched noise intervals, despite broadly compara-
ble relative update magnitudes (Fig. 1(a,b)). This suggests intermediate velocities as a potential

intervention point: can we correct these velocities to improve few-step instruction following while
keeping the generator frozen and its sampling schedule unchanged?
Our key idea is to keep the generator frozen and train a lightweight auxiliary branch to add residual
corrections to its predicted latent-space velocities, preserving the sampling schedule and backbone
forward-pass count. A step-distilled generator has learned update behavior adapted to its own sam-
pling schedule, so directly matching a multi-step model’s intermediate velocities may not preserve
its few-step denoising behavior. We therefore learn residual corrections from target-image super-
vision along the corrected few-step trajectories. Their effectiveness depends on both prediction
accuracy and intervention timing. When a correction is insufficiently accurate, applying it at a late
step may leave too few subsequent updates to resolve the resulting deviation. We therefore apply
corrections at selected early steps, allowing the remaining frozen updates to accommodate residual
inaccuracies and complete denoising.
Realizing this idea requires addressing three challenges: limiting inference overhead, defining suit-
able residual targets, and learning sufficiently accurate corrections. Parallel Velocity Residual
Correction addresses the first by conditioning on the current latent and cached previous-step ve-
locity, allowing the corrector to run alongside the current generator evaluation. To define suitable

targets, On-Trajectory Flow-Bridge Supervision constructs a linear reference bridge from the ini-
tial noise to a target latent obtained by offline editing of the generator’s output. Because earlier
corrections change the states encountered at later steps, it recomputes residual targets at the states
actually visited by the corrected sampler, combining reference-velocity correction with trajectory
recovery. To improve correction accuracy, Shared Multi-Step Learning jointly trains a single
noise-conditioned corrector across multiple selected early steps using local supervision, without
backpropagating through preceding sampling updates. Figure 2 illustrates the resulting improve-
ment within the original eight-step schedule: the animal’s pose better satisfies “its front and hind
legs spread wide apart.”
Our main contributions are:
• We introduce a lightweight external velocity-correction framework for fully frozen few-step text-
to-image generators. To our knowledge, it is the first to learn velocity residuals with a corrector
running in parallel with the generator while preserving its parameters, sampling schedule, and back-
bone forward-pass count.
• We empirically characterize how correction timing affects few-step generation. These findings
motivate applying corrections at selected early steps, leaving subsequent denoising updates available
to accommodate inaccuracies in the learned residuals.
• We develop On-Trajectory Flow-Bridge Supervision for state-dependent residual targets combin-
ing reference-velocity correction with trajectory recovery, and Shared Multi-Step Learning for train-
ing a single corrector across selected steps with local supervision.
• On our target complex-scene distribution, an 18.6M-parameter corrector achieves +17.60 pp
NetWin over frozen Z-Image-Turbo, with only 0.84% additional inference latency and 0.04 GiB
additional peak allocated GPU memory.

RELATED WORK
Fine-Tuning for Text-to-Image Generation. Supervised and feedback-based fine-tuning improves
text–image alignment: CoMat addresses concept omission and attribute binding, while CRAFT and
DreamSync use curated or feedback-filtered supervision (Jiang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026; 2025).
LoRA supports parameter-efficient adaptation (Hu et al., 2022). For few-step generators, fine-tuning
can disrupt sampling behavior learned through specialized distillation objectives, including LCM,
TCD, Hyper-SD, DMD2, and SDXL-Lightning (Luo et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024; Ren et al.,
2024; Yin et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024). Our method keeps the generator frozen and trains an
auxiliary velocity corrector.
Reward- and Preference-Based Alignment.
ImageReward, PickScore, and HPSv2 provide
learned preference signals (Xu et al., 2023; Kirstain et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023), while DDPO,
AlignProp, and DRaFT optimize generators through reward feedback (Black et al., 2024; Prabhude-
sai et al., 2024; Clark et al., 2024). Diffusion-DPO, D3PO, and Diffusion-RPO support preference
optimization without separately trained reward models (Wallace et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Gu
et al., 2024), with further developments in DiffusionNFT, TreeGRPO, and DiffusionOPSD (Zheng
et al., 2026a; Ding & Ye, 2026; Zhou et al., 2026). Multiple compositional requirements and evalua-
tor reliability complicate reward design (Chen et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026). Although generated and
target images could also form preference pairs for Diffusion-DPO, we use target images to derive
explicit stepwise velocity-residual supervision at corrected sampler states.
Trajectory-Based Adaptation and Control. ControlNet and T2I-Adapter add conditioning mod-
ules to frozen diffusion models (Zhang et al., 2023; Mou et al., 2024); Universal Guidance and
FreeControl provide training-free control (Bansal et al., 2024; Mo et al., 2024); and D-Flow,
FlowChef, and VESFlow intervene in flow trajectories or vector fields (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024;
Patel et al., 2024; Choi & Yoon, 2026).
D-OPSD adapts step-distilled generators by matching target-image-informed teacher predictions on
student rollouts (Jiang et al., 2026). We instead derive residual targets from the target latent and
current frozen-generator prediction, without a teacher branch or generator updates.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Data. We use approximately 4K compositional captions with a 90/10 training/validation split. Of-
fline target images are generated by editing frozen-backbone outputs with GPT-Image-2 (OpenAI,
2026). Each target is paired with the original caption and the initial noise used to generate the back-
bone output. Evaluation uses 574 held-out prompts with no normalized exact overlap with either the
training or validation prompts (Appendix A.1).
Implementation and baselines. We equip frozen eight-step Z-Image-Turbo (Z-Image Team et al.,
2025) with an 18.6M-parameter S3-DiT corrector shared across S = {2, 3, 4}. Only the correc-
tor is trained, using AdamW with learning rate 2 × 10−4 and Smooth L1 loss (β = 2), without
backpropagation through rollout updates; the backbone, text encoder, and VAE remain frozen. We
compare against frozen Turbo, SFT+LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), DPO+LoRA (Wallace et al., 2024), D-
OPSD (Jiang et al., 2026), and DiffCon (Yang et al., 2026), using the same backbone and velocity pa-
rameterization and evaluating all methods with eight sampling steps. SFT+LoRA, DPO+LoRA, and
D-OPSD each use 19.85M trainable parameters, while DiffCon uses a 20.21M-parameter controller.
Architecture, optimization, and baseline implementation details are provided in Appendix A.2.
Evaluation metrics. We conduct Good/Same/Bad (GSB) comparisons against frozen Turbo on 574
held-out target-domain prompts with matched seeds and both image orders. We report NetWin =
100(G −B)/N in percentage points (pp), where G, S, and B denote adapted-model wins, ties,
and baseline wins, respectively, and N = G + S + B = 574. GSB is used only for evaluation;
judging details and repeatability results appear in Appendix A.3. We additionally report category-
wise scores on T2I-CompBench++ (Huang et al., 2025), using the complete official test set with
four images per prompt. Evaluator and sampling details appear in Appendix A.4.
Inference efficiency. We measure end-to-end latency on one NVIDIA A800 at batch size one, BF16

CONCLUSION
We introduced a lightweight parallel velocity corrector trained with on-trajectory Flow-Bridge su-
pervision, preserving the frozen generator’s parameters, sampling schedule, and generator forward-
pass count. With fewer than 20M trainable parameters, it achieves +17.60 pp target-domain NetWin
on Z-Image-Turbo with minimal inference overhead.
Very short schedules, such as four-step generation, may leave insufficient updates after correction
to complete denoising. Offline targets from the proprietary GPT-Image-2 editor may also introduce
biases and limit reproducibility. Future work will explore schedule-aware correction across few-step
generators and reproducible supervision from open models.

ADDITIONAL EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
A.1
DATA CONSTRUCTION AND OVERLAP CHECKS
We construct approximately 4K compositional captions by sampling structured scene specifica-
tions from curated pools of subjects, scenes, and actions under predefined constraints, and using
claude-opus-4-8 (Anthropic, 2026) to expand them into descriptions. The descriptions un-
dergo consistency checks, verification of decomposed atomic requirements, and checks for ambigu-
ous subject assignment, with revisions as needed, before being formatted into final image-generation
prompts. The resulting captions cover multi-subject scenes, human actions, interactions, spatial re-
lations, counting, and attribute binding.
For each caption, we sample an initial noise ϵ and generate an image with the frozen back-
bone. The generated image and caption are provided to the GPT-Image-2-based editing model
gpt-image-2-0421-global-edit (OpenAI, 2026). The edited image serves as the super-
vision target, while the original caption and initial noise are retained to form the training tuple
(c, ϵ, x⋆). The editor is used only for offline target construction and is not queried during corrector
training or inference.
We reserve 10% of the caption pool for validation and use the remainder for training. Final target-
domain evaluation uses 574 held-out prompts. Exact string matching after whitespace normalization
confirms that these evaluation prompts are unique and do not overlap with either the training or
validation set.
A.2
ARCHITECTURE, OPTIMIZATION, AND BASELINES
Corrector architecture.
The S3-DiT corrector contains six Transformer blocks with hidden di-
mension 384, three attention heads, head dimension 128, and patch size two, totaling approximately
18.6M trainable parameters. A single corrector is shared across S = {2, 3, 4} of the frozen eight-
step Z-Image-Turbo generator. It reuses the generator’s precomputed text token embeddings without
an additional text-encoding forward pass, and the generator and corrector branches execute in par-
allel at active steps.
Corrector optimization.
We train only the corrector using AdamW with learning rate 2 × 10−4,
weight decay 10−2, effective batch size 64, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. Training uses BF16
precision, a per-GPU micro-batch size of one, gradient checkpointing, and the Smooth L1 residual
objective with β = 2. The backbone, text encoder, and VAE remain frozen, and rollout states and
cached velocities are detached between successive sampling steps, as described in Section 3.3.
Baseline configuration.
Our implementation uses DiffSynth-Studio (DiffSynth-Studio Contribu-
tors, 2023). The three LoRA baselines use rank r = 8, scaling factor α = 8, and zero dropout,
with adapters applied to the attention query, key, value, and output projections and the three feed-
forward projections across the 30 main, two noise-refiner, and two context-refiner blocks. Each
has 19,845,120 trainable student parameters; the original backbone weights, text encoder, and VAE
remain frozen. All four trainable baselines use AdamW with a configured learning rate of 10−5,
weight decay 10−2, and effective batch size 64, measured in preference pairs for DPO+LoRA.
SFT+LoRA.
We train the adapters by flow matching on paired image–text data. Given a target
latent x0 and Gaussian noise ϵ, we construct xσ = (1 −σ)x0 + σϵ and regress the velocity target
ϵ −x0. Training samples an index uniformly from a 1,000-point noise grid with shift three and uses
the implementation’s normalized Gaussian-envelope loss weighting.
DPO+LoRA.
We perform offline diffusion preference optimization with target images as chosen
samples and fixed eight-step frozen-Turbo generations from the same prompts as rejected samples.
Chosen and rejected samples share the same noise and noise level, and the frozen reference predic-
tions are obtained by disabling the LoRA adapters. We use preference coefficient βDPO = 2000 and
select the checkpoint with the lowest validation DPO loss on a prompt-disjoint holdout.

We train an independent controller with eighteen Transformer blocks, hidden dimension
256, two attention heads, and 20,206,401 trainable parameters (20.21M). It receives the current noisy
latent and the frozen backbone’s denoised estimate, together with text and timestep conditioning,
and predicts a scalar z and residual field h to form vDiffCon = vbase + (1 −z)h −zvbase. Its
output projection is zero-initialized. Training uses flow-matching mean squared error on noised
target latents, sampling uniformly from the eight inference noise levels. At inference, the controller
is evaluated after the backbone at every sampling step.
A.3
GSB JUDGING DETAILS AND REPEATABILITY
Evaluation consists of two stages. First, gpt-5-chat-0807 (OpenAI) decomposes each prompt
into independently verifiable visual requirements, annotated by importance (core or detail) and di-
mension. Second, the visual judge claude-opus-4-8 (Anthropic, 2026) compares each image
pair requirement by requirement and checks for severe structural failures. Images are resized to a
longest side of 768 pixels before judging.
Each pair is evaluated in both image orders. After mapping judgments back to model identities, we
retain a directional requirement preference if at least one order supports it and neither order supports
the opposite direction. Sample-level outcomes are determined lexicographically by severe-failure
counts, net core-requirement preferences, and net detail-requirement preferences. Atmosphere re-
quirements are recorded but excluded from the decision.
For each judging run, NetWin is computed using NetWin = 100(G −B)/N . Ties remain in
the denominator, so the metric reflects both the direction of preferences and the prevalence of pairs
without a decisive difference.
To assess automatic-judging stability, we perform five independent re-evaluations of the same fixed
image pairs, applying the image-order reversal and aggregation protocol in each run. The stan-

A.4
T2I-COMPBENCH++ EVALUATION DETAILS
We evaluate compositional generalization on T2I-CompBench++ (Huang et al., 2025) using its offi-
cial automatic evaluators: disentangled BLIP-VQA based on BLIP (Li et al., 2022) for color, shape,
and texture binding; UniDet-based metrics (Zhou et al., 2022) for 2D/3D spatial relationships and
numeracy; CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021) for non-spatial relationships; and 3-in-1 for complex
compositions.
We use the complete official test prompt set, comprising 300 prompts in each of eight categories. Our
evaluation differs from the official protocol only in the number of generated images per prompt: we
generate four images (seeds 0–3) instead of ten. We report the mean score over all 1,200 generated
images in each category.

## Abstract 

Attention sinks are tokens or positions that consistently re-ceive disproportionate attention mass, often beyond what their semantic content would suggest. Despite extensive study in large language models, their behavior and func-tional relevance in text-to-image (T2I) Transformers remain largely unexplored. To fill this gap and better understand how text-to-image Transformers allocate and route atten-tion, we explore this phenomenon of text-to-image trans-formers across bidirectional diffusion models and causal autoregressive models. Specifically, we explore whether at-tention sinks exist , where they emerge , and how they func-tionally influence generation . Through systematic attention analysis and targeted interventions, we identify that atten-tion sinks emerge in both bidirectional diffusion and au-toregressive models but exhibit distinct patterns. The au-toregressive model concentrates attention on the first and 

1arXiv:2610.13337v1 [cs.CV] 9 Oct 2026 global-scale tokens, whereas the bidirectional models de-velop sinks near modality boundaries, within text regions, and at spatial corners. Moreover, these patterns vary across generation stages and are localized to a sparse sub-set of layers and heads. To assess their functional rele-vance, we perturb sink-related heads and token positions. It turns out that perturbing sink-ranked heads produces larger average changes than non-sink comparisons, while some sink-position perturbations have limited effects on bidirec-tional model scores. We further introduce register tokens to redistribute attention sinks and find that several config-urations preserve or modestly improve generation perfor-mance. Overall, attention sinks are architecture-, inference-stage-, and component-dependent phenomena, providing a basis for mechanistic analysis and principled attention de-sign in text-to-image transformers. 

## 1. Introduction 

Transformers have become a central architecture for mod-ern generative modeling, but their attention maps often con-tain attention sinks , where a small set of token positions ab-sorb disproportionately large attention mass [35, 41]. Such phenomenon has been extensively studied in large language models (LLMs), where causal masking and sequence or-ganization often cause early tokens to act as persistent at-tention anchors [13, 14, 32, 41]. However, the prevailing understanding of attention sinks remains largely centered in LLMs, with limited exploration in other domains. Text-to-image Transformers differ substantially from LLMs. They jointly process text and image tokens, orga-nize visual tokens according to spatial or multiscale struc-tures, and generate images either through iterative diffusion denoising or autoregressive image-token prediction [3, 9, 15, 21, 34]. Therefore, their attention distributions might be relevant to modality boundaries, spatial topology, atten-tion directionality, and generation stage. These properties may not be reflected by the conventional first-token sink of causal LLMs. Moreover, sink patterns reported in vision models [26, 45] do not directly explain how attention is al-located during multimodal T2I generation. A systematic study of attention sinks in T2I models is thus needed not only to determine whether the phenomenon occurs beyond LLMs, but also to better understand how T2I Transformers allocate and route attention during generation. This paper addresses three fundamental questions: 1) whether attention sinks exist in modern T2I Transformers, 2) where they are located, and 3) are they functionally rele-vant to generation? To answer them, we conduct a system-atic empirical study across three models: FLUX.1 and Z-Image as diffusion-based T2I Transformers, and Infinity-2B as an autoregressive T2I Transformer, as shown in Fig. 1(a). We first characterize their attention distributions by sink strength, attention inequality, layer-head localization, po-sitional preference, prompt sensitivity, and inference-time dynamics. Concretely, we evaluate these properties on GenEval [11] and DPG-Bench [17], which cover diverse compositional generation and fine-grained text-image align-ment. Then, we perform targeted interventions on attention routing, value contributions, sink-related heads, and can-didate sink positions to assess their functional relevance. Finally, we introduce register tokens as dedicated non-semantic positions to examine whether sink-related atten-tion can be redistributed without comprising synthesis qual-ity. Our results show that attention-sink patterns are struc-tured in both diffusion and autoregressive T2I transform-ers, yet their locations differ substantially across architec-tures. Autoregressive model exhibits pronounced concen-tration on the global first token, while the diffusion mod-els concentrate attention near modality boundaries, within text-token regions, and at spatial corners. Moreover, sink behavior is localized to a sparse subset of layers and heads and evolves across denoising or coarse-to-fine generation stages, suggesting that sink locations of T2I transformers are associated with the inference stage rather than fixed to-ken positions. To investigate their influence on generation, we per-form interventions on sink tokens, which reveal heteroge-neous functional effects. Perturbing sink-ranked heads pro-duces larger average changes than the reported random or low-sink comparisons, indicating that certain sink-related heads participate in consequential generation pathways. In comparison, several sink-position perturbations have lim-ited effects in bidirectional diffusion models. Addition-ally, register-token experiments further show that several configurations can redistribute attention while preserving or modestly improving generation performance. Together, these results extend the study of attention sinks beyond its language-centric formulation. Our analysis character-izes attention sinks as architecture-, stage-, and component-dependent phenomena and establishes an important distinc-tion between where attention accumulates and where gen-eration is functionally sensitive. Our contributions are sum-marized as: • We systematically explore and characterize attention sinks in bidirectional diffusion and causal autoregressive T2I Transformers across token positions, layers, heads, prompts, and generation stages. • We evaluate the functional relevance of sink-related com-ponents through targeted interventions and investigate register tokens as dedicated non-semantic positions for redistributing attention. • We provide an empirical foundation for controlled mech-anistic analysis and for the principled design of attention mechanisms and token layouts in T2I Transformers. 22. 3.3. Experimental Setups 

Models . We study two T2I Transformer paradigms: bidi-rectional denoising models, including FLUX.1-dev [21] and Z-Image [3], and a causal autoregressive model, Infinity-2B [15]. During inference, all models generate 1024 ×1024 

images using their official inference settings. FLUX.1 and Z-Image use the EDM [20] sampler with 28 denoising steps, while Infinity-2B uses a bitwise sampler with a 13 -scale schedule. 

Token Layouts . Token layouts differ across models: In-finity follows a causal sequence, whereas FLUX.1 and Z-Image adopt a fixed interleaved structure. Moreover, FLUX.1 places text tokens first and Z-Image places image tokens first. We therefore define three positional relations: 1) sequence position, 2) text-side relation, and 3) image-side relation. In the following, token position refers to the index within the sequence, while modality relations are dis-cussed separately. 

Datasets and Metrics . We evaluate on two complementary benchmarks: GenEval [11], which contains 553 prompts for compositional generation such as counting, color attri-bution, and spatial relations, and DPG-Bench [17], which contains 1065 prompts for fine-grained text-image align-ment. Together, they provide 1618 prompts with distinct lengths and semantic densities. 4.4. Effects of Prompt on Attention Sinks 

We compare GenEval [11] and DPG-Bench [17] to ex-amine whether attention sinks are stable under different prompt distributions. GenEval contains shorter compo-sitional prompts, while DPG-Bench contains longer and denser prompts. 

Sink heads are stable across datasets . The layer-head concentration maps are highly correlated across the two datasets, with Pearson correlations above 0.98 for all three models, as shown in Tab. 3. This indicates that the heads prone to sink behavior are largely stable under different prompt sets. Together with the pruning results in Tab. 6, these results suggest that sink-head identity is more tied to model structure than to a specific prompt benchmark. 

Sink-position entropy differs across prompt datasets .The normalized entropy of sink positions Hnorm is com-puted for each model and dataset. Although sink heads remain stable, sink positions show stronger dataset depen-dence. In the diffusion models, the high-entropy ratio in-creases substantially on DPG-Bench, from 6.8% to 92.2% for FLUX.1 and from 2.7% to 49.4% for Z-Image. Infinity-2B remains nearly unchanged. This suggests that prompt distribution mainly affects where stable sink heads route attention, rather than which heads become sink-dominant. Because the datasets differ in prompt length, semantics, and complexity, we do not attribute this change to a single prompt factor.

## 5. Intervention Results 

We next move from descriptive sink patterns to functional probes. Given the sink positions and sink-dominant heads identified in Sec. 4, we perturb the corresponding pathways and measure generation changes relative to each model’s baseline. Because several frequent sinks also coincide with modality boundaries, corner positions, or global-scale to-kens, we interpret score changes as pathway sensitivity rather than isolated causal effects of sink behavior alone. 

5.1. Impact of Intervention Strategies. 

We evaluate the three strategies from Sec. 3.2 using GenEval, DPG, HPSv2, and ImageReward. Fig. 1(c) illus-trates their intended targets, Fig. 1(d) shows their visual ef-fects, and Tab. 4 reports the aggregate scores. Across mod-els, severity follows the intervention scope. 5.4. Probing Sink Attention with Register Tokens. 

The interventions above weaken native sink tokens to test generation sensitivity. We further ask the complementary question of whether sink attention can shift to a dedicated non-semantic position without hurting generation quality. Following Zhang et al. [46], we insert one learnable regis-ter token to the FLUX.1 image-token sequence, expose it to joint attention, freeze the pretrained backbone, and re-move the register output before unpatchification.. The reg-ister thus serves as an auxiliary routing slot rather than a direct image-content token. Implementation details are in the Appendix. Tab. 7 shows that adding the register improves GenEval from 67 .14 to 68 .85 and DPG from 83 .67 to 84 .01 . Mean-while, the two recurring native sink positions are weakened but not removed, with ρ1(7) decreasing from 12.95% to 12.44%, and ρ1(4544) from 3.04% to 2.72%. These results show partial redistribution, not full replacement, of native sinks, suggesting that the added low-semantic slot redirects a small share of sink-dominant routing while keeping the original structural anchors. Since ρ1 measures top-1 sink frequency, not total attention mass, we view this as a re-distribution of sink dominance, not a full absorption of dis-placed attention. 

## 6. Related Work 

6.1. Text-to-Image Transformer 

Transformer-based T2I generation has developed along two complementary paradigms: diffusion transformers and au-toregressive models . Both of them rely heavily on multi-head attention as the core computational primitive. The seminal Diffusion Transformer (DiT) [27] first replaced the U-Net with a pure transformer architecture, building upon foundational diffusion frameworks [7, 16, 30]. SD3 [9] in-troduced the MMDiT, which employed joint self-attention for text and image modalities. This paradigm has since been advanced by open-source models such as FLUX.1 [21], Z-Image [3], Qwen-Image [37, 47], and Hunyuan-Image [4], as well as commercial systems [1, 10, 12]. In contrast, autoregressive Transformers treat images as discrete token sequences and generate them left-to-right. Early works in-cluding DALL-E [28], CogView [8], and Parti [43] estab-lished the paradigm with large-scale transformer models. More recent efforts such as LlamaGen [33] revisited next-token prediction without visual inductive biases, VAR [34] introduced next-scale prediction for faster inference, and In-finity series [15, 24] applied bit-wise quantization for effi-cient high-resolution generation. Despite these architectural differences, both paradigms exhibit structured attention be-haviors, motivating our analysis. 

6.2. Attention Sinks in Transformers 

Attention sinks (AS) are tokens that absorb extremely high attention. This phenomenon arises from the sum-to-one constraint of Softmax: when no semantically relevant key exists, attention mass is forced onto specific uninformative tokens as a numerical no-op buffer [2, 32]. AS were first characterized in autoregressive LLMs, where initial tokens dominate under causal masking [41]. Subsequent work em-pirically traces their emergence [13, 14, 31], exploits them for context anchoring [25], attention redistribution [36, 44], and connects them to activation outliers [32]. In Vision Transformers , Bondarenko et al. [2] show that sinks are lo-calized on low-information background patches, not on po-sitional tokens. Darcet et al. [6] and Lappe and Giese [22] demonstrate that injecting learnable register tokens absorbs this excess attention. In Multimodal LLMs , visual sinks on non-semantic tokens degrade grounding [19, 45], and fur-ther studies examine visual information pathways [26] and audio-visual speech recognition [5]. Diffusion LMs exhibit distinct features compared to autoregressive counterparts. Rulli et al. [29] observe moving sinks that shift through-out the denoising process. Zhang et al. [46] further iden-tify moving sinks as protecting against over-mixing in dif-fusion while dynamic position hurts. In Diffusion Models ,attention sinks have high norms [18, 40], while Wu and Summa [38] argue that they are not functional structures es-sential for semantic alignment. Li et al. [23] show that text template tokens act as implicit semantic registers. How-ever, the extent to which findings transfer across diffusion and autoregressive T2I Transformers remains unclear. Aunified comparison across layer, head, token-position, and generation-stage dimensions is still limited for recent text-to-image architectures. 

## 7. Conclusions 

We presented a systematic study of attention sinks in text-to-image Transformers. Our results show that the evaluated T2I models do not all reproduce the canonical language-model first-token pattern, varying with multimodal token layouts and generation stages. The evaluated autoregres-sive model exhibits strong first-token attraction, whereas the evaluated bidirectional models concentrate sink mass near 8modality boundaries and spatial corner positions. Inter-vention experiments further show that model performance is sensitive to selected sink-related heads and positions, which motivates further study of dedicated non-semantic positions. Overall, attention sinks provide a useful lens for interpreting and potentially controlling generative attention in T2I models. 

Limitations and Future Work . Our study covers three text-to-image Transformers, leaving broader architectures, scales, and training recipes for future validation. We only focus on inference-time behavior, while understanding how attention sinks emerge during training remains an important next step.
